    1  Sweet it is, when on the great sea the winds are churning the waters,
    2  to watch from land the great toil of another;
    3  not because anyone's distress is a delightful pleasure,
    4  but because it is sweet to see what evils you yourself are free of.
    6  Sweet, too, to look upon the great contests of war {{NOTE: 2.6 printed before 2.5 in the source (editorial transposition); order kept}}
    5  drawn up across the plains, with no share of your own in the danger;
    7  but nothing is sweeter than to hold, well fortified,
    8  the serene precincts built high by the teaching of the wise,
    9  from where you can look down on others and see them everywhere
   10  wandering and, straying here and there, seeking the road of life,
   11  contending in talent, competing in nobility of birth,
   12  striving night and day with surpassing labor
   13  to rise to the height of wealth and gain the mastery of things.
   14  O miserable minds of men, O blind hearts!
   15  In what darkness of life and in how great dangers
   16  this span of life, whatever it is, is passed! Do you not see
   17  that nature barks for nothing else for herself than this, that {{NOTE: "barks" (latrare): Lucretius's own blunt word for nature's demand}}
   18  pain be parted from the body and be absent, and the mind enjoy
   19  a pleasant sense of things, set apart from care and fear?
   20  Therefore we see that for the body's nature few things
   21  are needed at all: such things as take away pain,
   22  and such that they can also spread out many delights for us,
   23  more welcome now and then; nor does nature herself require,
   24  if there are no golden images of youths about the halls
   25  holding fire-bearing lamps in their right hands
   26  so that light may be supplied for feasts by night,
   27  nor the house gleam with silver and shine with gold,
   28  nor lyres echo through paneled and gilded rafters, {{NOTE: templa here = the roof-beams or coffered ceiling of a hall, not temples}}
   29  when still, lying together on the soft grass
   30  beside a stream of water under the boughs of a tall tree,
   31  with no great wealth they tend their bodies pleasantly,
   32  above all when the weather smiles and the seasons
   33  of the year sprinkle the greening grass with flowers.
   34  Nor do hot fevers leave the body any faster
   35  if you are tossed on woven pictures and blushing purple
   36  than if you must lie beneath a common man's coverlet.
   37  Therefore, since in our body treasures are of no profit,
   38  nor nobility, nor the glory of a kingdom,
   39  for the rest, we must think they do the mind no good either;
   40  unless perhaps, when you see your legions over the spaces of the Campus {{NOTE: the Campus Martius at Rome, where troops drilled}}
   41  seething as they stir up the images of war, {{NOTE: belli simulacra: mock battles, field exercises; simulacra kept as "images"}}
   42  made firm with great reserves and a force of wealth,
   43  your ships fitted out with arms, all alike full of spirit, {{NOTE: stlattas (a broad-built ship) is the printed text of a corrupt line; readings and sense here are disputed}}
   44  then, before these things, the religions in their terror
   45  flee panic-stricken from your mind, and the fears of death
   46  then leave your heart empty and loosed from care.
   47  But if we see that these things are laughable and a mockery,
   48  and in truth the fears of men and the cares that dog them
   49  fear neither the clash of arms nor savage weapons,
   50  and boldly move among kings and the masters of things,
   51  and do not stand in awe of the glitter that comes from gold
   52  nor the bright splendor of a purple robe,
   53  why do you doubt that this power belongs wholly to reasoning,
   54  especially when all life labors in darkness?
   55  For just as children tremble and fear everything
   56  in blind darkness, so we in the light are afraid
   57  at times of things that are no more to be feared than
   58  what children in the dark shudder at and imagine will come.
   59  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness {{NOTE: 2.59-61 repeat 1.146-148 verbatim; the English repeats its own lines}}
   60  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
   61  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
   62  Come now, by what motion the generative bodies of matter
   63  beget the various things and dissolve them once begotten,
   64  and by what force they are compelled to do it, and what
   65  mobility is given them for travelling through the great void,
   66  I shall explain; you, remember to give yourself to my words.
   67  For certainly matter does not cling together, packed close
   68  in a mass, since we see each thing diminish,
   69  and we perceive all things flowing away, as it were, over long age,
   70  and age withdrawing them from our eyes,
   71  while yet the sum is seen to remain unimpaired,
   72  because the bodies that depart from each thing
   73  diminish what they leave, and on what they come to bestow increase.
   74  They force the one to grow old and the other in turn to flower,
   75  and do not linger there. So the sum of things is ever
   76  renewed, and mortals live by exchange among themselves.
   77  Some races increase, others are diminished,
   78  and in a brief space the generations of living things are changed
   79  and like runners pass on the torch of life. {{NOTE: the relay torch race, in which runners handed a lit torch from one to the next}}
   80  If you think the first-beginnings of things can stand still
   81  and by standing still beget new motions of things,
   82  you wander far astray from true reasoning.
   83  For since they wander through the void, all the first-beginnings
   84  of things must be carried either by their own weight
   85  or perhaps by the blow of another. For when, swift, they have often
   86  met and clashed, it comes about that suddenly they spring
   87  apart in different ways; and no wonder, since they are very hard,
   88  solid in their weight, and nothing blocks them from behind.
   89  And so that you may see more clearly that all the bodies
   90  of matter are tossed about, remember that the whole
   91  has no bottom in the sum, and the first bodies have no place
   92  to come to rest, since space is without end or limit,
   93  and that it spreads out immeasurable in all directions everywhere
   94  I have shown in many words and proved by sure reasoning. {{NOTE: referring back to 1.951-1051}}
   95  And since this is settled, surely no rest
   96  is given to the first bodies through the deep void,
   97  but rather, driven by unceasing and varied motion,
   98  some, when they have collided, leap back over great intervals,
   99  and some are harried by the blow within short spaces.
  100  And all those that, in a denser gathering,
  101  brought together, rebound across small intervals,
  102  entangled by their own interlocking shapes,
  103  these form the strong roots of rock and the savage bodies
  104  of iron, and the other things of this kind.
  105  Those few others that wander on through the great void {{NOTE: the text of 2.105-106 is uncertain; rendered as printed}}
  106  leap far apart and run far back again
  107  across great intervals; these furnish us the thin air
  108  and the shining light of the sun.
  109  And many besides wander through the great void
  110  which have been thrown out of the unions of things and nowhere
  111  have been able, even when let in, to join their motions together.
  112  Of this thing, as I tell, an image and picture
  113  is always turning before our eyes and pressing on us.
  114  For look closely, whenever the sun's light is poured
  115  by rays let in through the darkened places of houses:
  116  you will see many minute bodies in many ways, through the void,
  117  mingling in the very light of the rays,
  118  and as though in everlasting conflict waging wars and battles,
  119  fighting troop by troop, and giving no pause,
  120  driven on by frequent unions and partings;
  121  so that from this you may guess what it is for the first-beginnings
  122  of things to be tossed forever in the great void.
  123  So far as it goes, a small thing can give
  124  a pattern of great things and the tracks of knowledge.
  125  For this reason, too, it is more fitting that you attend to these
  126  bodies that are seen to be in turmoil in the sun's rays,
  127  because such turmoils signify that there are motions
  128  of matter, too, hidden and blind, beneath them.
  129  For you will see many there, struck by blind blows,
  130  change their course and, driven back, return,
  131  now this way, now that, in all directions everywhere.
  132  This wandering, you may be sure, all comes from the beginnings. {{NOTE: error = "wandering", the literal sense; principia rendered "beginnings", distinct from primordia "first-beginnings"}}
  133  For first the first-beginnings of things move of themselves;
  134  then those bodies that are formed of a small gathering
  135  and are, as it were, nearest to the strength of the beginnings
  136  are set going, struck by their blind blows,
  137  and they in turn assail bodies a little larger.
  138  So motion rises from the beginnings and comes out
  139  little by little to our senses, so that those too
  140  are moved which we can make out in the light of the sun,
  141  though by what blows they do it does not plainly appear.

  142  Now what swiftness of motion has been given to the bodies
  143  of matter, Memmius, you may learn from this in a few words.
  144  First, when dawn sprinkles the lands with new light,
  145  and the many-colored birds, flitting through the pathless woods,
  146  fill the places through the soft air with liquid voices,
  147  how suddenly the sun, once risen, is used at such a time
  148  to clothe all things, flooding them with his light,
  149  we see to be ready to hand and plain for all.
  150  But that heat which the sun sends out, and its calm light,
  151  does not travel through empty void; and so it is forced {{NOTE: inane vacuum, "empty void": Lucretius doubles the term for emphasis; "void" stays fixed}}
  152  to go more slowly, as it beats its way, as it were, through waves of air;
  153  nor do the little bodies of heat travel one by one,
  154  but tangled with one another and massed together;
  155  and so at once they are dragged back by one another and hindered
  156  from outside, so that they are forced to go more slowly.
  157  But the first-beginnings that are of solid singleness,
  158  when they travel through empty void and nothing delays them
  159  from outside, and they themselves, one whole from their own parts,
  160  are borne, straining, toward the one place they began to move to,
  161  must surely surpass in mobility
  162  and be carried far more swiftly than the light of the sun,
  163  and run through many times the extent of space in the same
  164  time in which the sun's flashes spread through heaven.
 164a  * * * {{NOTE: the source marks lost text here; the sense picks up mid-sentence, apparently about people who do not follow out the motions of the atoms}}
  165  nor to follow out each single first-beginning,
  166  to see by what principle each thing comes about.
  167  But some, against this, ignorant of matter,
  168  hold that nature cannot, without the power of the gods,
  169  so closely fitted, in such measure, to human needs,
  170  change the seasons of the years and create the crops
  171  and all the other things that mortals are urged to approach,
  172  and to which divine pleasure herself, the guide of life, leads them,
  173  coaxing them through the works of Venus to propagate their generations,
  174  so that the human race should not perish. When they imagine
  175  the gods have ordered all things for men's sake, in every way
  176  they seem to have fallen far from true reasoning.
  177  For even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  178  this, still, from the very order of heaven I would dare
  179  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  180  that by no means for us, by divine power, was created
  181  the nature of the world: so great the flaws it stands endowed with. {{NOTE: the argument is given at 5.195-234}}
  182  These things, Memmius, I will make plain to you later;
  183  now I will set out what remains concerning motions.
  184  Now is the place, I think, in these matters to prove this too
  185  to you: that no bodily thing can by its own force
  186  be carried upward and travel upward.
  187  Do not let the bodies of flames deceive you in this;
  188  for they are born upward and take on their increase,
  189  and upward the shining crops and the trees grow,
  190  though all weights, as far as in them lies, are carried downward.
  191  Nor, when fires leap up to the roofs of houses
  192  and with swift flame lick the rafters and beams,
  193  must we think they do it of their own accord, with no force thrusting from beneath.
  194  Just so the blood let out from our body
  195  spurts leaping on high and scatters gore.
  196  Do you not see, too, with what force the water's moisture
  197  spits out rafters and beams? For the more we have pushed them deep,
  198  straight down, and many of us with great force have pressed them, straining,
  199  the more eagerly it thrusts them back up and sends them out,
  200  so that they rise and leap out by more than half their length.
  201  And yet we do not doubt, I think, that these, as far as in them lies,
  202  are all carried downward through the empty void.
  203  So in the same way flames too must be able, through the breezes
  204  of air, squeezed out, to climb upward, although
  205  their weights, as far as in them lies, fight to draw them down.
  206  And the night torches of heaven, flying aloft, {{NOTE: meteors}}
  207  do you not see them draw long trails of flame
  208  toward whatever quarter nature has given them passage?
  209  Do you not see stars and constellations falling to the earth?
  210  The sun also from the summit of heaven scatters its heat
  211  to every side and sows the fields with light;
  212  to the earth, therefore, the sun's heat also tends.
  213  And you see thunderbolts fly slantwise through the rains;
  214  now from here, now from there, fires broken from the clouds
  215  rush together; the flaming force falls everywhere upon the earth.
  216  This too, in these matters, we long for you to know:
  217  that when bodies are carried straight down through the void
  218  by their own weights, at quite uncertain times
  219  and uncertain places they push a little from their course,
  220  just so much that you could call it a change of motion.
  221  For if they were not used to swerve, all things would fall {{NOTE: declinare, "swerve": first statement of the swerve; the noun clinamen comes at 2.292}}
  222  downward like drops of rain through the deep void,
  223  and no collision would arise, no blow be created
  224  for the beginnings; so nature would never have created anything. {{NOTE: principia, "beginnings": Lucretius's synonym for primordia, kept distinct from "first-beginnings"}}
  225  But if perhaps someone believes that heavier bodies,
  226  because they are carried more swiftly straight through the void,
  227  can fall from above upon the lighter, and so beget
  228  blows that could give rise to generative motions,
  229  he strays far away, off the road, from true reasoning.
  230  For whatever falls through water and thin air,
  231  these must quicken their fall in proportion to their weights,
  232  because the body of water and the thin nature
  233  of air cannot delay each thing equally,
  234  but give way more quickly, overcome by heavier things;
  235  but on the other hand the empty void cannot, on any side or at any
  236  time, hold up anything at all,
  237  but must go on giving way, as its nature seeks;
  238  therefore all things must, through the calm void,
  239  be carried at equal speed, though driven by unequal weights.
  240  Therefore heavier things will never be able to fall
  241  from above upon lighter ones, nor of themselves beget the blows
  242  that vary the motions by which nature carries on things.
  243  Therefore, again and again, bodies must incline
  244  a little; but no more than the least, so that we do not seem to invent
  245  slanting motions, and the true facts refute it.
  246  For this we see to be ready to hand and plain,
  247  that weights, as far as in them lies, cannot travel slantwise
  248  when they plunge down from above, so far as you can see;
  249  but that nothing at all, from the straight line of its path,
  250  swerves of itself, who is there that could perceive?
  251  Finally, if every motion is always linked,
  252  and the new always arises from the old in a fixed order,
  253  and the first-beginnings do not by swerving make
  254  some beginning of motion to break the covenants of fate, {{NOTE: fati foedera, "the covenants of fate": set against nature's covenants (foedera naturae)}}
  255  so that cause should not follow cause from infinity,
  256  from where does it arise, free, for living things throughout the earth,
  257  from where, I ask, is this will wrested away from the fates,
  258  by which we go forward wherever pleasure leads each one,
  259  and likewise swerve our motions, at no fixed time
  260  and at no fixed place, but where the mind itself has carried us?
  261  For beyond doubt in these things each one's own will
  262  gives the beginning, and from it motions flow out through the limbs.
  263  Do you not see too that, when in an instant of time the starting-gates
  264  are thrown open, the eager strength of the horses still cannot
  265  burst forth as suddenly as their mind itself longs to?
  266  For all the matter throughout the whole body, its whole
  267  store, must be roused, so that, roused through all the limbs,
  268  it may strain together and follow the mind's desire;
  269  so you may see that the start of motion is created by the heart,
  270  and that it goes forth first from the will of the mind,
  271  and is then passed on through the whole body and limbs.
  272  Nor is it the same as when we go forward pushed by a blow,
  273  by another's great strength and great compulsion;
  274  for then all the matter of the whole body,
  275  it is clear, goes and is swept along against our will,
  276  until the will reins it back throughout the limbs.
  277  Do you see now, then, that although an outside force drives many
  278  and often compels them against their will to go forward
  279  and be swept headlong, still there is in our breast
  280  something that can fight against it and resist?
  281  At whose command the store of matter too
  282  is forced at times to turn through the limbs and joints,
  283  and, thrown forward, is reined back and settles back again.
  284  Therefore in the seeds too you must admit the same,
  285  that there is besides blows and weights another cause
  286  of motions, from which this power is born in us,
  287  since we see that nothing can come to be from nothing.
  288  For weight prevents all things from coming about by blows,
  289  as by a force from outside; but that the thing itself should not {{NOTE: res ipsa, "the thing itself", as in the source; many editors read mens ipsa, "the mind itself"}}
  290  hold an inward necessity in doing all things,
  291  and be compelled, as if conquered, to bear and suffer,
  292  this is brought about by the tiny swerve of the beginnings {{NOTE: clinamen, "swerve": the poem's only use of the noun}}
  293  at no fixed place and at no fixed time.

  294  Nor was the store of matter ever more tightly packed
  295  than now, nor again spread apart by greater intervals;
  296  for nothing is added to it, and nothing perishes from it.
  297  Therefore the motion in which the bodies of the beginnings {{NOTE: principia, "beginnings", Lucretius's alternative term for the first-beginnings (primordia)}}
  298  are now is the same motion they were in through ages gone by,
  299  and hereafter they will always be carried in like manner,
  300  and the things that have been used to be born will be born under the same
  301  condition, and will be, and grow, and be strong in their strength,
  302  as much as is granted to each by nature's covenants.
  303  Nor can any force change the sum of things;
  304  for there is nothing outside into which any kind of matter
  305  could flee out of the whole, nor any source from which, into the whole,
  306  a new force could rise and burst in and change the whole
  307  nature of things and turn its motions aside.
  308  In these matters it is no wonder why,
  309  although all the first-beginnings of things are in motion,
  310  the sum nonetheless seems to stand in utmost rest, {{NOTE: summa ... summa: Lucretius plays "the sum" against "utmost"}}
  311  except where some thing makes motions with its own body.
  312  For the whole nature of the first bodies lies far below
  313  our senses, out of reach; therefore, since you cannot see
  314  the bodies themselves, their motions too must steal away from sight;
  315  the more so since even things that we can see still often hide
  316  their motions when withdrawn from us by a distance of place.
  317  For often on a hill, cropping the glad pastures,
  318  the woolly flocks creep on wherever, calling each one,
  319  the grasses invite them, jeweled with fresh dew,
  320  and the lambs, filled full, play and frisk and gently butt;
  321  yet all these, seen from far off, seem to us blurred together,
  322  like a whiteness standing still on a green hill.
  323  Again, when great legions fill the spaces of the plains
  324  with their running, stirring up the images of war, {{NOTE: simulacra belli, mock battle or maneuvers; "images" kept as the fixed rendering of simulacra}}
  325  when the glitter lifts itself to the sky and all the earth around
  326  shines back with bronze, and beneath, from the force of men,
  327  a sound is roused by their feet, and the mountains, struck
  328  by the shouting, throw back the voices to the stars of the world,
  329  and horsemen wheel around and suddenly cross through
  330  the middle of the plains, shaking them with their strong charge;
  331  and yet there is some place on the high mountains from which
  332  they seem to stand still, a glitter resting on the plains.
  333  Come now, next learn of what sort the starting-points
  334  of all things are, and how far they differ in their forms,
  335  how varied they are, in shapes of many kinds;
  336  not that many are furnished with forms too little alike,
  337  but because, as a rule, they are not all alike, each to each.
  338  Nor is it a wonder; for since their store is so great
  339  that there is neither end, as I have taught, nor any sum to it,
  340  it must surely be that not all are in every way
  341  alike to all, of the same contour and of similar shape.
  342  Further, the human race, and the mute swimming flocks
  343  of scaly creatures, and the glad herds, and wild beasts,
  344  and the various birds, those that throng the gladdening
  345  watering-places around the banks and springs and lakes,
  346  and those that flit abroad through the pathless woods:
  347  go on and take any one of these you will, kind by kind;
  348  you will find that they still differ from one another in shape.
  349  Nor in any other way could offspring know its mother,
  350  nor mother offspring; yet we see that they can,
  351  and that they are known to one another no less than men are.
  352  For often before the fair shrines of the gods a calf
  353  falls slaughtered beside the incense-burning altars,
  354  breathing out a warm river of blood from its breast;
  355  but the bereaved mother, wandering through the green glades,
  356  knows the prints pressed into the ground by cloven hooves,
  357  searching every place with her eyes, if she can anywhere
  358  catch sight of her lost young, and, stopping, fills
  359  the leafy grove with her lament, and often goes back
  360  to the stall, pierced through with longing for her calf;
  361  nor can tender willows, nor grasses quickened with dew,
  362  nor any rivers gliding level with their banks
  363  delight her mind and turn aside the sudden care,
  364  nor can other shapes of calves in the glad pastures
  365  draw her mind away and lighten it of care;
  366  so much does she seek something her own and known.
  367  Again, the tender kids with trembling voices
  368  know their horned mothers, and the butting lambs
  369  the bleating flocks; so, as nature demands,
  370  each runs, for the most part, to its own udder of milk.
  371  Finally, take any grain you will: you will still not see
  372  that every grain in its kind is like the others,
  373  without some difference of form running between them.
  374  And by a like principle we see the race of shells
  375  paint the lap of the earth, where with soft waves
  376  the sea beats the thirsty sand of the curving shore.
  377  Therefore again and again by like reasoning it must be,
  378  since they exist by nature and were not made by the hand
  379  of one, to a fixed form, the first-beginnings of things,
  380  that some of them fly about with shapes unlike each other.
  381  It is very easy for us, by the mind's reasoning, to explain
  382  why the fire of the thunderbolt is far more piercing
  383  than ours, which flows from torches, born of the earth;
  384  for you could say that the heavenly fire of the thunderbolt
  385  is subtler, consisting of small shapes,
  386  and so passes through openings that this fire of ours,
  387  born from wood and created from the torch, cannot.
  388  Again, light passes through horn, but rain
  389  is thrown back. Why so, unless those bodies of light
  390  are smaller than the ones that make the kindly liquid of the waters?
  391  And although we see wine in an instant pass
  392  through a strainer, yet on the other hand oil lingers slow,
  393  either because, surely, it is made of larger elements,
  394  or ones more hooked and tangled with one another,
  395  and so it happens that they cannot be so suddenly drawn apart
  396  from one another that the first-beginnings, each one,
  397  might, one by one, seep through the openings of each thing.
  398  Add to this that the liquids of honey and of milk
  399  are handled in the mouth with a pleasant feeling on the tongue;
  400  but on the other hand the loathsome nature of wormwood and of wild
  401  centaury twists the mouth with its foul taste;
  402  so that you can easily recognize that of smooth and round bodies
  403  are made the things that can touch the senses pleasantly,
  404  but on the other hand whatever seems bitter and harsh,
  405  these are held bound together by more hooked ones,
  406  and for that reason are used to tear their way into our
  407  senses, and to burst through the body as they enter.
  408  Finally, all things good to the senses and bad to the touch
  409  are at war with one another, made of unlike shape;
  410  lest you should perhaps think that the harsh shudder
  411  of the screeching saw consists of elements as smooth
  412  as the Muses' melodies, which musicians, along the strings,
  413  wake with nimble fingers and give form to;
  414  nor should you think that first-beginnings of like form pierce
  415  into men's nostrils when foul corpses are burning
  416  and when the stage is freshly sprinkled with Cilician saffron
  417  and the altar nearby breathes out the perfumes of Panchaea; {{NOTE: Panchaea, a fabled land of incense in the far east, off Arabia}}
  418  nor should you suppose that the pleasant colors of things are made of like
  419  seed, the colors that can feast the eyes,
  420  and those that prick the sight and force it to weep,
  421  or look foul and ugly with their foul appearance.
  422  For every thing whatsoever that caresses the senses
  423  was not created without some smoothness in its beginnings;
  424  but on the other hand whatever is troublesome and harsh
  425  is not found without some roughness of matter.
  426  There are also things that are rightly thought neither to be smooth
  427  nor altogether hooked with bent points,
  428  but rather made with little angles jutting out a little, such as
  429  can tickle the senses rather than hurt them;
  430  of which kind are wine-lees and the flavors of elecampane.
  431  Finally, that hot fires and icy frost
  432  are toothed in unlike ways and so prick the senses
  433  of the body, the touch of each gives us proof.
  434  For touch, touch, by the holy powers of the gods,
  435  is the sense of the body, whether when a thing from outside
  436  works its way in, or when a thing born in the body hurts it,
  437  or gives delight going out through the generative acts of Venus,
  438  or when, from a collision, they riot within the body itself,
  439  and the seeds, roused, throw the sense into confusion among themselves;

Notes (DRAFT)

[DRAFT] (2.1-4) The opening image (suave, mari magno) is the poem's most famous prologue; Lucretius is not praising cruelty but the security of the observer, who sees what evils he is free of (quibus ipse malis careas). The sea-and-shore picture sets up the contrast between the philosopher's detached vantage and the struggle of ordinary life that runs through the whole proem.

[DRAFT] (2.5-6) The source prints 2.6 before 2.5 (editorial transposition, so that "suave etiam belli certamina magna tueri" reads directly after 2.4); the translation follows the source order and keeps both line keys, so the English "drawn up across the plains" completes the sense of 2.6.

[DRAFT] (2.7-8) The "serene precincts" (templa serena) are Epicurean philosophy imagined as a fortified high sanctuary, built by "the teaching of the wise" (doctrina sapientum), meaning Epicurus and his followers, whom Lucretius does not name here. Templa means a consecrated or marked-off region, not a building, in keeping with the register's rendering of Acherusia templa as "precincts."

[DRAFT] (2.14-19) The "blind hearts" and the barking of nature (latrare) introduce Epicurus's doctrine that nature demands only the absence of bodily pain and a mind free of care and fear. The verb is deliberately blunt, as if nature were a dog insisting on its few needs.

[DRAFT] (2.20-22) "The body's nature" (corpoream naturam) and the few things it needs state compressedly the Epicurean division of desires into natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, and empty. The verse treats luxuries (delicias) as permissible additions, not necessities.

[DRAFT] (2.24) The golden youths holding lamps (aurea iuvenum simulacra) are statues of the kind that lit rich Roman dinner halls. Here simulacra means "statues" in an ordinary sense, kept as "images" in the translation, and is not the Book 4 technical term for the films shed by things.

[DRAFT] (2.28) Templa laqueata aurataque are the coffered, gilded ceilings of a rich hall (the English "rafters" follows this sense), a non-sacred use of templa; the word's sense of a defined space of roof or sky is why the same word is used of the "serene precincts" at 2.8.

[DRAFT] (2.40-43) The reading and sense of 2.43 are disputed: the transmitted text has stlattas (a broad merchant ship, with the odd spelling stlatta), and the line is often considered corrupt or the sense uncertain. The translation takes the line as describing fleets "fitted out with arms," following the transmitted text, with the whole movement being a mock review of land and naval forces meant to show that such displays do not calm the mind.

[DRAFT] (2.44) Religiones is rendered "religions," as the register requires, even though the plural strains English; the sense is the terrors and scruples that religio imposes, which Lucretius treats as the real enemy throughout the poem (compare 1.63 and the proem of Book 1).

[DRAFT] (2.55-58) The comparison of adult fears to children's fear of the dark is an image Lucretius uses in all his refrain passages. It anticipates the refrain at 2.59-61 by supplying its ground: terror of the mind (animi terrorem) is darkness of imagination, not a real danger.

[DRAFT] (2.59-61) These three lines repeat 1.146-148 word for word (and recur at 3.91-93 and 6.39-41); the English repeats its own earlier rendering. This is the first appearance of the refrain in Book 2, and "the face and reasoning of nature" (naturae species ratioque) renders species as "face" per the register.

[DRAFT] (2.62-66) The "generative bodies of matter" (genitalia materiai corpora) are the first-beginnings, i.e. what a modern reader would call atoms, and the passage announces the topic of the book: the motion of atoms in the void. The "mobility" (mobilitas) given to them is the source of everything that follows, down to the swerve at 2.216ff.

[DRAFT] (2.79) The runners passing on the torch (cursores vitai lampada tradunt) refer to the Greek lampadedromia, a relay in which a lit torch was handed on. The image of life as handed from generation to generation was widely quoted afterward.

[DRAFT] (2.83-88) The sentence compresses the Epicurean claim that atoms move either by their own weight (gravitate sua) or by blows from other atoms. Nothing in the void stops them (neque quicquam a tergibus obstet), so collisions send them rebounding at once. A reader who knows the swerve may see a gap here, since the third cause, the swerve, is not yet introduced.

[DRAFT] (2.92-94) The infinity of space (spatium sine fine modoquest) and the claim that the whole has no bottom, no "lowest point" (imum), are proved in 1.951-1051, as the English note flags. The point matters because it means there is no floor on which atoms could come to rest.

[DRAFT] (2.105-106) The text and sense of these lines is uncertain, and the translation renders what is printed without resolving the difficulty: the few atoms which wander in loose freedom are set against the dense mass that makes rock and iron. The modern reader may recall a contrast between solids and gases, which is the reader's association, not Lucretius's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.112-113) Simulacrum et imago ("an image and picture") here means an analogy or likeness for the eye, and not the technical simulacra of Book 4. The register keeps "images" for simulacra, and the word is used here in a non-technical sense, so a reader who knows the later doctrine should not read it back into this line.

[DRAFT] (2.114-124) The dust motes dancing in a sunbeam are Lucretius's famous visible analogue for atomic motion; he says the little bodies in the sunbeam merely give a sample (exemplare) of what he cannot show, not that they are atoms. A modern reader may hear Brownian motion here, but that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.132) Error is rendered "wandering," its literal sense, and principia is "beginnings," kept distinct from primordia ("first-beginnings") as the register requires. The line says that the motes' wandering derives from the invisible motion of the atoms themselves.

[DRAFT] (2.133-141) The passage explains the chain by which motion passes upward in scale: atoms move of themselves, then the smallest compounds (nearest to them in strength) are set moving, then larger bodies, until the motion reaches the visible motes. Lucretius is giving a causal ladder from the invisible to the seen, with the final sentence admitting that the blows that move the visible motes are not themselves plain to see.

[DRAFT] (2.142) The doctrine here is the atoms' speed (mobilitas), which the poem proves by contrast with the slower travel of sunlight through air. The "bodies of matter" (materiai corporibus) are the first bodies of the register, here named by their physical side.

[DRAFT] (2.151) Lucretius says "inane vacuum" together (non per inane meat vacuum). In Epicurean terms, light and heat are compound and meet resistance from the air, whereas a single first-beginning moving through pure void meets none. The translation keeps "void" and renders the doubled term as "empty void", as at 2.158.

[DRAFT] (2.164a) Editors mark a lacuna here, so the sentence that governs "nor to follow out" (nec persectari) is lost. The translation prints the gap unnumbered and supplies nothing. The infinitive in 165 depends on a lost main verb. The people at issue are probably those who will not trace the motions of individual atoms.

[DRAFT] (2.167) "Some, against this" (quidam contra) are never named. They hold the teleological view of nature, which the Epicureans attacked. In Roman terms the target is the Stoic doctrine of providence, though Lucretius does not name the school. The "guide of life, divine pleasure" (dux vitae dia voluptas) is Lucretius's own recasting, with the Venus of the proem hidden behind the sexual drive.

[DRAFT] (2.181) The faults of the world (tanta stat praedita culpa) are what Lucretius argues from, not the world's goodness. He answers his own promise (posterius, 182) at 5.195-234, where he lists the uninhabitable regions, the toil of farming, and the dangers of infancy and disease.

[DRAFT] (2.184) The topic is "upward" motion (sursum ferri). Flame, smoke and growing plants seem to defy weight. Lucretius argues that they rise only because something pushes them. The doctrine behind it is that every body has weight and by itself tends only downward (pondera, quantum in se est). This sets up the swerve argument at 216ff.

[DRAFT] (2.206) The "night torches of heaven" (nocturnas faces caeli) are meteors or shooting stars, which Lucretius treats as fire falling toward earth. Stars and sunlight (209-212) are offered as further instances of downward tendency. The "constellations" (sidera) are plural.

[DRAFT] (2.213) The slanting thunderbolts (transversos fulmina) are the lightning of 6.160ff, where Lucretius gives the full account. Here they serve as one more fire moving down, and across, under a force from outside.

[DRAFT] (2.216) The swerve is announced here (216-220). A reader will hear a precursor of modern indeterminacy, which is the reader's association, not the poet's claim. The Latin keeps the sense of "uncertain time and place" (incerto tempore ... incertisque locis), and the same phrase returns in the refrain at 292-293 (nec regione loci certa nec tempore certo).

[DRAFT] (2.221) The verb declinare is first used here, with its noun clinamen at 292. The register fixes both as "swerve". "Rain-drops falling through the deep void" (imbris uti guttae ... per inane profundum) pictures Epicurus's parallel fall, with no collisions and so no world.

[DRAFT] (2.224) The "beginnings" (principiis) are a variant name for the primordia, the first-beginnings. The register keeps the two English words apart for the two Latin words. The line's last clause, "nature would never have created anything", states the argument's stake.

[DRAFT] (2.225) The objection, which says heavier bodies falling faster would strike lighter ones, is answered at 230-239. Epicurus held that atoms in void move at equal speed whatever their weight (aeque ponderibus non aequis, 239). The modern reader may hear Galileo's falling bodies, which is the reader's association only.

[DRAFT] (2.254) "The covenants of fate" (fati foedera) is a Stoic idea of an unbroken chain of causes. The phrase echoes the poem's "nature's covenants" (foedera naturae) but reverses their force. Cause follows cause "from infinity" (ex infinito) in a chain the swerve would break.

[DRAFT] (2.256) "Free will" is Lucretius's argument from experience. "This will wrested from the fates" (fatis avolsa voluntas) is the first claim in the poem that living things possess a will that is more than mechanical. The line reads "libera ... voluntas" across 256-257, with "will" (voluntas) as the noun. The same argument is picked up at 2.261.

[DRAFT] (2.263) The starting-gates (carceribus) are the stalls of the Roman circus, thrown open at the start of a chariot race. The horses' bodies hold back for an instant behind the mind's wish, which is the proof that the will begins the motion and the body follows.

[DRAFT] (2.269) "Heart" (corde) is where Lucretius locates the mind in the breast. Compare "in pectore nostro" at 279. The word is plain and anatomical here, and the register keeps animus as "mind" in the next line (animi voluntate).

[DRAFT] (2.289) The manuscripts read "res ipsa", which makes "the thing itself" a general subject, while many editors since the early editions print "mens ipsa" ("the mind itself"), tying the swerve to the will of 260. The translation follows the source, the manuscript reading. The sense is fully available either way.

[DRAFT] (2.292) "Swerve" is the register's rendering of clinamen, the poem's only use of the noun. Compare declinare at 221, 253, 259. It is "tiny" (exiguum) because the swerve must not be seen as a slant (244-245), so the translation should keep the diminutive force.

[DRAFT] (2.297) Lucretius here says *principiorum* ("beginnings") rather than *primordia*; it is one of his interchangeable names for the first-beginnings, and the English keeps it distinct from "first-beginnings" (the register's rule that distinct Latin gets distinct English). The sense is the same bodies.

[DRAFT] (2.300) The lines 2.294-302 state the conservation of matter: the total stock never grows or shrinks (*nam neque adaugescit quicquam neque deperit inde*), so the pattern of birth and growth stays fixed by *foedera naturai*. A modern reader may hear an anticipation of conservation laws; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.310) *summa tamen summa* repeats the word in two senses: the first is "the sum" (the whole of things, as at 2.303), the second is the adjective "utmost" (*summa quiete*, "in highest rest"). The translation keeps the pun by using "the sum" and "utmost" in the same line.

[DRAFT] (2.317) The sheep on the hill (317-322) and the legions on the plain (323-332) are the first of the poem's illustrations that motion can be present but unseen at a distance. The same sheep image is reused in the Book 2 argument about invisible motion; the legions are a second example of the same point, seen from a high place (*altis montibus*).

[DRAFT] (2.324) *belli simulacra* are mock-battles or military exercises, not images in the Epicurean technical sense (the films of Book 4). The translation keeps "images" so the register's fixed term for *simulacra* is not varied, but the sense here is "the semblance of war."

[DRAFT] (2.333) *exordia rerum* ("starting-points") is a further synonym for the first-beginnings, here introducing the doctrine of the variety of atomic shapes. The register leaves *exordia* unfixed; "starting-points" keeps it distinct from "first-beginnings" and "beginnings."

[DRAFT] (2.339) *uti docui* refers back to the proof of the infinity of the all and of the number of bodies in Book 1 (the argument at 1.951 ff.). The infinity of the supply of first-beginnings is what makes it necessary that not all have the same shape (340-341).

[DRAFT] (2.353) *delubra* and *turicremas aras*: a Roman or Italic sacrificial scene, with a calf slaughtered at an incense-burning altar before the shrines. The calf-and-mother passage (352-366) is a famous set-piece; Lucretius uses the grief of the cow to prove that offspring recognise their own by individual shape, and the grief is not used here as a comment on religion.

[DRAFT] (2.362) The reading *summis ... ripis* ("level with their banks") means the river flows full to the top of the banks, as in spate; it is not the sense "from the top of the banks." The translation takes the first sense.

[DRAFT] (2.381) *animi ratione* ("by the mind's reasoning"): *ratio* here is the act of reasoning, not the doctrine, and the register's "reasoning" is kept. The passage 381-397 begins the series of explanations by shape: lightning through small pores, light through horn, wine through a strainer, oil held back.

[DRAFT] (2.401) *ferique centauri*: "wild centaury," the plant (*centaurion*), named in myth after the centaur Chiron, who was said to have used it as a cure; the Latin adjective *feri* ("wild") gives the pun with "centaur," and the translation takes it as the plant.

[DRAFT] (2.412) *musaea mele*: the melodies of the Muses, that is, music in general. The contrast of the screech of the saw (*serrae stridentis*) with musical sound is an argument from sense experience: sounds that please come from smooth bodies, harsh ones from rough.

[DRAFT] (2.416) *croco Cilici*: saffron from Cilicia, used to scent and colour the Roman stage, where perfumed water or saffron was sprinkled; the *scena* here is the theatre, set against the funeral pyre (*cadavera torrent*) as a foul smell and the incense on the altar as a pleasant one. The line on Panchaea (417) is marked in the draft already.

[DRAFT] (2.430) *fecula ... inulaeque sapores*: wine-lees and elecampane (*inula*), given as examples of tastes that are neither smooth nor hooked but have "little angles jutting out a little" (*angellis*), tickling rather than wounding. The translation follows the manuscripts' *inulae*.

[DRAFT] (2.434) *tactus enim, tactus, pro divum numina sancta*: an emphatic repetition with an oath ("by the holy powers of the gods"), unusual in Lucretius, who stresses that touch is the sense underlying all the others. The doctrine is that all sensation is finally a kind of touch by bodies, which is why shape matters.

[DRAFT] (2.437) *genitalis per Veneris res*: "the acts of Venus," that is, sexual pleasure, as one of the ways touch can delight when something goes out of the body. The Venus here is the goddess as a figure for sex, not a divine agent, as in the proem (1.1).

[DRAFT] (2.447) *adamantina saxa*: "rocks of adamant," a legendary hardest substance, not the modern diamond; the translation keeps "adamant." *prima acie* is a military phrase ("in the front line"), continuing the image of hardness as resistance.

[DRAFT] (2.453) *papaveris haustus*: a scoop or draught of poppy-seed, which pours like water because its little globes do not cling together (*glomeramina*). The Latin is difficult here, and the translation follows the reading *haustus itemst facilis quod aquarum*.

[DRAFT] (2.465a) The sentence begun at 464-465 ("that you see the same things bitter that are fluid ... ought to be no wonder") has no verb or conclusion in the text as printed, because a line has been lost after 465; 466 then begins *nam* with the explanation. The translation prints the lacuna as unnumbered `* * *`, supplies nothing, and leaves the sentence broken as in the Latin.

[DRAFT] (2.472) Neptune's body (Neptuni corpus) is the sea, a metonymy like Ceres for grain and Bacchus for wine, which Lucretius takes up at 2.655ff. The proof is the filtering (percolatur, 475). Sea water turns sweet when it passes through earth, which shows that rough and smooth first-beginnings can be separated, the rough ones catching in the soil.

[DRAFT] (2.476) Primordia, "first-beginnings", is the register's name for what a modern reader will know as atoms. The translation keeps it distinct from principia (472, 573) "beginnings", semina (481) "seeds" and corpora prima (486) "first bodies", though all name the same entities.

[DRAFT] (2.478) The two-line formula (Quod quoniam docui, pergam conectere rem) recurs verbatim at 522-523, and the English repeats itself word for word. It marks the two stages of the argument: first-beginnings vary within a finite range of shapes (478-521), and those of any one shape are infinite in number (522ff).

[DRAFT] (2.485) The "least parts" (minimis e partibus) are the Epicurean minimal parts, the indivisible units of which a first body is composed (argued at 1.599ff). A body of fixed small size has few of them and so admits few arrangements (ordo). More shapes would therefore need more parts and larger bodies, which is the absurdity named at 498-499.

[DRAFT] (2.500) Meliboea was a Thessalian coastal town known for purple dye made from shellfish (concharum), so "Thessalico ... colore" names the same dye. The luxuries listed (robes, peacocks, myrrh, swan-song, lyre music) stand for the best the senses know. If shapes were infinite, something better would always supersede them (507).

[DRAFT] (2.505) Here daedala is an adjective of the carmina, whereas the register fixes it as "artificer" for daedala tellus (1.7) and natura daedala rerum (5.234). "Intricate" is a departure from the register, which the translator may keep, change, or note in the register. Phoebus is Apollo, god of the lyre, and cycnea mele alludes to the swan's proverbial song.

[DRAFT] (2.528a) The source marks lost text after 528, and the translation prints the same marker with its lettered key and supplies nothing. The jump from the conclusion at 528 to the new promise at 529 (versibus ostendam) is abrupt, but what the missing line said is not known.

[DRAFT] (2.531) Protelum (protelo plagarum continuato) comes from a word for a team pulling in succession, and "relay" renders it. The plagae are the collisions of first bodies. The doctrinal point is that only an infinite supply of matter can keep the blows coming without a break.

[DRAFT] (2.533) Crux: the transmitted magis in "fecundamque magis naturam cernis in illis" sits uneasily with rara animalia, since rarer kinds would be expected to be less fruitful. The translation keeps magis and takes in illis of the other, commoner kinds, which strains the Latin. Some editors read minus, which makes in illis the rare animals themselves and fits 534-535, where the same kind is numerous elsewhere.

[DRAFT] (2.537) "Snake-handed" (anguimanus) is a Latin compound for the elephant's trunk, and the animal was familiar to Romans from the wars with Pyrrhus and Hannibal. India stands for the far-off land where a kind that seems rare is abundant, and the ivory rampart (vallo ... eburno) is hyperbole for their numbers.

[DRAFT] (2.552) The shipwreck simile (naufragiis ... aplustra) illustrates 547-551. A few finite bodies of one kind, scattered in a vast sea of unlike matter, would drift apart like wreckage and never reassemble into a ship. The "laughing" calm sea (ridet ... ponti, 559) recalls the proem's rident aequora ponti (1.8), where the sea smiles at the goddess's coming and here smiles in treachery. That echo is the reader's association, not something Lucretius points out.

[DRAFT] (2.569) The "motions" of destruction and of generation (exitiales, genitales) are the movements of first-beginnings that break compounds up or build them. Their equal contest (aequo certamine) is the Epicurean balance that keeps the sum of things from ending either in total ruin or in unchecked growth.

[DRAFT] (2.577) The register lists 2.577 under the refrain "dias in luminis oras", but the Latin here is visentis luminis oras, with visentis agreeing with pueri ("as they come to look on"), and it has no dias. The translation rightly omits "shining". This is a variant of the refrain rather than an instance, so the register's list should probably be corrected.

[DRAFT] (2.581-585) The sealed-up principle (obsignatum) is the premise of the whole passage: no visible thing consists of one kind of first-beginning, and everything is "of mingled seed" (permixto semine). Everything from here to 2.729 argues that premise from the earth, from living bodies, from fruit, and from the letters of the alphabet.

[DRAFT] (2.589-593) "First bodies" (corpora prima) is the register's rendering of what Lucretius also calls primordia; he uses the two terms interchangeably. The earth is shown to hold the first bodies of water (springs feeding the sea), of fire (burning ground, Etna), and of food, which is why it is rich in kinds.

[DRAFT] (2.598-599) The Great Mother (Magna Mater, Cybele) is introduced here because the earth, as source of everything, has been called "mother of the gods" (deum mater) and "mother of wild beasts" (materque ferarum). The long description of her Phrygian cult that follows (600-643) is given in order to be set aside at 644-645, and the English line should carry this explanation. In 599 "she alone" (una) means that this one earth is given all three titles. The line also plays on mater and materies, "matter", which the English cannot reproduce.

[DRAFT] (2.600-609) The "learned poets of the Greeks" (Graium docti poetae) read the image allegorically: the lions are the wild offspring tamed by parental kindness, the mural crown is the earth bearing cities, and the chariot in the air shows the earth hung in space. "Earth cannot rest upon earth" (neque posse in terra sistere terram) is the argument that the earth needs no support beneath it. It is a rare Lucretian use of a mythic image to carry doctrine, and he treats it as picture, not as truth.

[DRAFT] (2.611) Mount Ida in Phrygia is the Idaean Mother's home, as the note on the English line says. The "bands of Phrygians" (Phrygias catervas) are her escort in procession, and Lucretius reports the Phrygian origin of grain as one of the ancient explanations (quia primum ex illis finibus).

[DRAFT] (2.614-617) The Galli (Gallos) were her eunuch priests. The allegory is that those who wronged the Mother or their parents are shown as unfit to "bring forth living offspring" (vivam progeniem edant), since they are themselves unable to. The phrase "into the shores of light" (in oras luminis) is the register's refrain, first at 1.22.

[DRAFT] (2.618-623) The instruments (tympana, cymbala, cornua, tibia) and the weapons (tela) of the procession are meant to terrify the "ungrateful minds" (ingratos animos) of the crowd. Lucretius is describing the effect as a deliberate religious device. The sixfold ingratos / impia anticipates his later charge that fear of the gods is a tool of religio.

[DRAFT] (2.624-628) During the procession (the Megalensia at Rome) the image of the goddess is carried silently through cities, and people strew the road with coins and roses. "Wordless blessing" (muta salute) is the goddess's gift of prosperity, mute because the image does not speak.

[DRAFT] (2.629-633) The "armed troop" (armata manus) imitates the Curetes, the attendants of the infant Jupiter in Crete. Lucretius gives the Greek name (Curetas nomine Grai) and glosses it with the Cretan myth: they danced in armor, clashing bronze, to drown the infant's cries (vagitum) so that Saturn would not find him. "Dictaean" belongs to Mount Dicte in Crete. The ritual dancers in Phrygian processions are identified with them by the Romans.

[DRAFT] (2.635-637) The Latin text of 635-636 is corrupt in the source: 636 repeats the end of 635 (pernice chorea / armat et in numerum) and is generally deleted by editors. The English prints both as given in the source, so the repetition in 635 and 636 reflects the damage and not Lucretius's own emphasis.

[DRAFT] (2.640-643) The second explanation (aut quia) is that the armed attendants signify the goddess's command to defend one's country and be a protection and honor to one's parents. The first reading (638-639) was mythical; this one is moral, and both are said to be "well and excellently set out" (bene et eximie disposta) before being rejected.

[DRAFT] (2.644-651) After the rejection (longe a vera ratione repulsa) comes the doctrine that the gods live in peace, untouched by human affairs. Lines 646-651 repeat 1.44-49 verbatim, and the English repeats the earlier rendering. In the Latin the repetition is probably the poet's own insertion, and some editors have suspected it here as a misplaced duplicate (the passage reads on without it, from 645 to 652), but the source text keeps it and so does the translation. It is the Epicurean doctrine that the gods need nothing from us and are neither pleased nor angered, so that cult is pointless.

[DRAFT] (2.652-654) The earth, though called a goddess, is "without sense" (caret sensu), since it has the first-beginnings of many things but is not itself alive. This is the point of the passage: calling the earth "mother" is a harmless name if it is understood as a metaphor for this multiplicity of seeds.

[DRAFT] (2.655-659, 680) "Neptune" for the sea, "Ceres" for crops and "Bacchus" for wine are metonymy (the god's name for his domain), and Lucretius allows it (concedamus) so long as no one is stained by "foul religion" (religione turpi). Line 680 is printed after 659 in the source because editors moved it here from its transmitted place. The sense is carried by 659 + 680 together, with the clause "provided he refrains" (dum ... parcat) breaking across them. Here "religion" is the register's plain word, with no softening to "superstition".

[DRAFT] (2.660-667) The flocks and herds eat the same grass and drink the same water yet remain unlike and keep their parents' nature (retinentque parentum naturam), which is evidence that the grass and water themselves contain many kinds of first-beginnings. The "order of matter" (herbae materiai dissimilis ratio) renders ratio here as arrangement, not "reasoning".

[DRAFT] (2.668-671) Bones, blood, veins, heat, moisture, flesh, and sinews (ossa cruor venae calor umor viscera nervi) are the parts that make up an animal and differ from one another, so their first-beginnings must differ in shape. The grammar is compressed; the sentence has its main verb only in constituunt, with "formed of unlike shapes" (dissimili perfecta figura) as a loose addition.

[DRAFT] (2.672-675) Things that burn must contain the seeds of fire, sparks, and ash (ignem ... lumen ... scintillas ... favillam). This repeats the argument at 2.589-593 on a small scale: even a log holds the bodies of fire.

[DRAFT] (2.679-685) The argument from the senses: color, taste and smell reach the senses by different paths (nidor enim penetrat qua fucus non it), so the bodies that give them differ in shape. This is the Epicurean doctrine that perceived qualities depend on atomic shape, compressed into a single syllogism.

[DRAFT] (2.688-694) The alphabet analogy: "elements" (elementa) is both the Latin for letters and the register's word for the elements of the physical world, so "elements common to many words" is the pun the register says to note once. Lucretius uses the same analogy at 1.196-198 and 1.823-829, and returns to it at 2.1013-1022. The point is that shared letters make different words, as shared first-beginnings make different things.

[DRAFT] (2.700-706) Monsters such as the Chimaera (Chimaeras, flame-breathing, a lion-goat-serpent of Greek myth) and the half-human beasts are what we would see if any seeds could combine freely. Since we do not see them, the combination of seeds must be limited. The reader may hear in this an argument that anticipates the later account of failed hybrids at 5.878-924, where the same impossibility is argued again; that is a reader's association, not part of this passage.

[DRAFT] (2.707-710) "A fixed mother" (certa genetrice) and "fixed seeds" (seminibus certis) state the principle of fixed kinds: each thing comes from its own seed and keeps its kind. "Fixed principle" (certa ratione) is the register's "principle" for ratio.

[DRAFT] (2.711-717) The argument from nutrition: only the food bodies that fit the animal's motions are taken up, and the rest are expelled (reicere). "Unseen bodies" (caecis corporibus) are first-beginnings too small to see, driven out by blows (plagis). "Vital motions" (vitalis motus) is the register's way of saying the motions that make a living body what it is.

[DRAFT] (2.718-719) In the source the second half of 719 appears to lack its sense: "the same principle bounds all things" (quaedam ratio res terminat omnis) is the English for an obscure line. Some editors mark 718-719 as corrupt; the translation follows the source as printed.

[DRAFT] (2.723-724) Line 724 repeats 694 verbatim (sed quia non volgo paria omnibus omnia constant), and the English repeats its earlier rendering. The first instance of 723 differs slightly from 692; the English follows the Latin.

[DRAFT] (2.725-729) The list "intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows, meetings, motions" (intervalla vias conexus pondera plagas concursus motus) is a compressed summary of the properties of atoms in motion and combination that Lucretius has worked out since 2.62. The sentence closes on the largest division, the separation of land, sea, and sky.

[DRAFT] (2.730) The argument that the first-beginnings have no color begins here and runs to 841. "Matter" (materiai) in 735 and 737 is the same thing as the first-beginnings; the English keeps both words for the Latin's two.

[DRAFT] (2.740) "Casting of the mind" (animi iniectus) renders the Epicurean epibole tes dianoias, the mind's direct application to an object that the senses cannot reach. The English keeps the phrase fixed so that it can recur wherever Lucretius uses it again.

[DRAFT] (2.745) The blind from birth (caecigeni) are Lucretius's test case: touch gives knowledge of bodies without any color, so knowledge of the colorless is possible. The step from touch to the mind's grasp of the atomic is the argument's real claim.

[DRAFT] (2.748) "Quod quoniam vinco fieri, nunc esse docebo" says that having proved colorless knowledge possible (vinco fieri), he will now show it actual (esse). The English keeps the two verbs distinct.

[DRAFT] (2.753–754) These lines repeat 1.670–671 and 1.792–793, where the same argument is made against Heraclitus and the monists: if a thing changes it leaves its own bounds, and that is the death of what it was. The English must match Book 1 word for word.

[DRAFT] (2.755) "Seeds of things" (semina rerum) is a register term for the same entities called "first-beginnings" (principiis) a few lines before; the two are used interchangeably in this passage, so the shift of word does not mark a shift of subject.

[DRAFT] (2.766–767) The simile of the dark sea turning to white foam under wind (canos... fluctus, candenti marmore) is used as an example of color change without any change in the underlying seeds; "marble" carries "shining" more than "hard".

[DRAFT] (2.778–779) The analogy of the square built from unlike forms is a mosaic image: the whole shows one shape (quadratum) though its parts differ. Lucretius uses it to argue that if colored seeds composed a uniformly colored sea, the varied colors would show through, as varied shapes would show within a square.

[DRAFT] (2.801–805) The dove's neck and the peacock's tail (806–807) are Lucretius's examples of structural or iridescent color, which changes with the angle of light and so cannot belong to the thing itself. A modern reader may hear an anticipation of the wave theory of light; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.803) Pyropus (pyropo) is a red-gold bronze alloy, not a gemstone, here. The English "fire-bronze" follows the Greek roots, "fire-eyed".

[DRAFT] (2.805) "Zmaragdos" (smaragdos) is the Greek-derived Latin for emerald, or green gemstones generally, so "emeralds" is approximate.

[DRAFT] (2.810) "Blow" (plagae) is the Epicurean term for impacts of atoms; the pupil receives different "blows" from different atomic configurations. The English "blow" is kept here and "stroke" (ictu) in 808 for the different Latin word.

[DRAFT] (2.829) The Latin text prints "austrum" (not ostrum, "purple dye"), but the sense requires the dye or purple cloth, since filatim in 831 means "thread by thread". The translation follows the sense of ostrum; "gold" (aurum) is a less likely conjecture.

[DRAFT] (2.830) "Poeniceus" (poeniceus color) means Phoenician-red, a scarlet or crimson that is a dye color. The English "scarlet" renders it, but it is the same family as "purple" (purpura), so the pair is almost a doublet.

[DRAFT] (2.833) "Particulas" (particles, "particulas") is here a non-technical word for the small parts of a composite, distinct from the semina rerum. The English "particles" is acceptable for this ordinary sense but should not be used for primordia (register: never "particles" for corpora).

[DRAFT] (2.842) The argument that first-beginnings lack all secondary qualities continues from the preceding lines on color (the "solo colore" of 842 means color is only the first item in a longer list). Here Lucretius adds warmth, sound, flavor, and odor (teporis, sonitu, suco, odorem).

[DRAFT] (2.847) The perfumer's analogy: amaracinum (oil of marjoram) and stacte (oil of myrrh) were costly scented unguents, and nard was an imported aromatic. The base for such compounds had to be odorless olive oil (inolentis olivi) so that it would not taint the added scents. The analogy supports the claim that the first-beginnings must contribute no quality of their own to the things they compose.

[DRAFT] (2.861) The Latin has principiis here, a second name for the primordia (854, "primordia rerum"). The translation renders both as "first-beginnings" in keeping with the register's deliberate merge of the cluster.

[DRAFT] (2.862) Immortal foundations (inmortalia... fundamenta) are what keep things from returning "ad nihilum funditus" (864). This is the Epicurean principle that nothing is wholly destroyed, and Lucretius argues that if the first-beginnings were mortal, everything would dissolve into nothing.

[DRAFT] (2.865) The new argument starts here, against the objection that sentient things cannot come from senseless first-beginnings. Lucretius's thesis is that sense arises from the arrangement of senseless first-beginnings (ex insensilibus... principiis), not from sense already present in them. The rival view is that of the earlier philosophers (the Ionian physicists, and Anaxagoras in particular) who gave the elements the properties of the things made from them.

[DRAFT] (2.871) Worms from dung (vivos... vermes / stercore de taetro) is the spontaneous generation Lucretius uses as evidence for life from the lifeless, a common ancient belief. The same example recurs at 898-901 and 926-930, and Lucretius himself calls the later one a repetition ("quod fudimus ante", 926).

[DRAFT] (2.874) The chain river-leaf-cattle-human-beast-bird (875-878) is an argument from nutrition: food becomes living flesh. The fire comparison at 881-882 supports the same point, that different things arise from the same material by new arrangement. Lucretius uses it as the proof of 883-885 that "order" and motions matter (referre in quali sint ordine).

[DRAFT] (2.881) The comparison of logs turning to flame (arida ligna / explicat in flammas) is another case of the same stuff becoming something of a different kind through rearrangement. It recalls the letters analogy (elementa) used in Book 1 and again at 2.688-699, where order, position, and motion of the same elements produce different results.

[DRAFT] (2.903) The line numbered 903a marks a lacuna after 903. The sentence starting "Deinde ex sensilibus..." (902) breaks off without its main verb or its refutation, and 904 picks up in the middle of a thought ("mollia cum faciunt"). The translation prints the gap unnumbered and supplies nothing.

[DRAFT] (2.904) The opponents' claim, as the argument can be reconstructed, is that the supposed sentient first-beginnings are made of soft things (mollia), since sense is found in flesh, sinews, and veins. Lucretius's reply (906) is that these are mortal and "creta", fashioned of a mortal body, so they cannot be the eternal first-beginnings.

[DRAFT] (2.911) The text is disputed here ("namque animus sensus membrorum respuit omnis"). The translation takes it that the mind rejects the idea of senses belonging to the limbs alone, so that sense belongs to the whole creature. Here animus is rendered "mind" in accord with the register.

[DRAFT] (2.919) The refutation turns on the point that, even if the first-beginnings were living creatures, their union (coetu concilioque) would yield only a crowd of living things (volgum turbamque animantum), as humans and animals mating do not create anything new in kind. The regress is Lucretius's reductio against giving the first-beginnings sense.

[DRAFT] (2.944) Lucretius now argues from the effects of blows. A blow too great for the creature's nature (grandior ictus) dissolves the positions of the first-beginnings (positurae) and the "knots of the vital spirit" (vitalis animae nodos), and so drives the spirit out through "caulas" (passages). Here anima is rendered "spirit", the vital principle, and the nodi are the bonds that hold it in the body, a doctrine developed at length in Book 3.

[DRAFT] (2.963) The last argument is from pain and pleasure. Pain is the disturbance of bodies from their seats (sollicitata suis... sedibus) and pleasure their return (remigrant). First-beginnings, which are not compounded of smaller bodies, have no such parts to disturb, so they feel neither. This is the Epicurean definition of pleasure and pain as motion and settling, and "voluptas" is rendered "pleasure" in the register.

[DRAFT] (2.973-990) This is the last stretch of the argument that the first-beginnings cannot themselves have sense (the "laughing atoms" argument). Lucretius reasons that if sense is granted to the beginnings, they must laugh, weep, and reason, and so be made of further beginnings that do the same, without end (nusquam consistere ut ausis). Since that is absurd, sensing things must come from seeds that lack sense (seminibus carentibus undique sensu). The translation renders *principia* (974) as "beginnings", and *elementa* (981) as "elements" and *semina* (988, 990) as "seeds", following the register. Here the three words name the same things.

[DRAFT] (2.991) Sky (caelesti semine) is father and earth is mother, an old pairing that Lucretius takes over here. He then gives it a physical sense: rain is the father's seed that the earth receives, and the earth's feeding of her creatures earns her the name of mother (maternum nomen, 998). Greek and Roman myth and cult both knew the pairing, and the poet uses it in a purely natural sense.

[DRAFT] (2.1000-1001) The ether (aetheris oris) and "the precincts of heaven" (caeli templa) are given the same meaning. Lucretius is following the doctrine that the finer parts of the human body (the spirit and the seeds from the sky) return to the upper air at death, as the grosser parts return to earth. *Templa* is rendered "precincts" in the register, not "temples". The passage is often compared with an epitaph preserved from the Athenian dead at Potidaea in 432 BC, which says much the same.

[DRAFT] (2.1002-1006) Death does not annihilate things (nec... interemit) but scatters their union (coetum dissupat). The matter is rejoined (aliis aliud coniungit) so that things take on new shapes, colors, and sense (capiant sensus). This is the Epicurean doctrine that nothing comes from nothing and nothing returns to nothing, stated compressedly. A modern reader may hear conservation of matter in it; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.1013-1018) The alphabet analogy, with its pun on *elementa* ("elements" and "letters"), recurs through the poem (first at 1.196-198, and again at 2.688-699). The same letters (eadem) spell out sky, sea, lands, and so on, so a difference of position (positura) makes different words and different things. Here the argument is put to the particular use of showing that the first-beginnings can differ only in arrangement and still produce things that differ in kind.

[DRAFT] (2.1019-1022) The list of nine terms (intervalla vias conexus pondera plagas concursus motus ordo positura figurae) is Lucretius's catalogue of the ways atoms can differ in relation. It is an asyndetic run, and the translation keeps that. *Plagae* is "blows"; *figurae* "shapes".

[DRAFT] (2.1023) The line opens a new section on the plurality of worlds (nunc animum nobis adhibe veram ad rationem). *Ratio* is "reasoning" in the register, and "true reasoning" is the translation of *veram rationem*. The address to Memmius is by the second person only, as elsewhere.

[DRAFT] (2.1030-1039) The argument from habituation: the sky would astonish us if we saw it for the first time, but we have grown weary of looking (fessus satiate videndi). Lucretius uses it to prepare Memmius to accept the new doctrine of many worlds, and the same thought recurs at 5.1204-1217 in a different setting. *Templa* again is "precincts".

[DRAFT] (2.1044-1047) *Moenia mundi* ("walls of the world") echoes the proem's *flammantia moenia mundi* (1.73), the edge of the world that Epicurus crossed in thought. Here the reader is asked to do the same. In the translation, "free cast of the mind" renders *animi iactus liber*, the mind's unobstructed projection (see the translator's note at 1047). *Mens* and *animus* both occur in 1046-1047 and are both "mind" or "thought" per the register.

[DRAFT] (2.1048-1051) The argument for the infinity of the universe, which Lucretius says he has already taught (uti docui, 1050), refers back to 1.958-1051 in Book 1. *Profundum* is the "deep", the unbounded extent. The line "the thing itself cries aloud" (res ipsaque per se vociferatur) is the poet's appeal to evidence over argument.

[DRAFT] (2.1052-1066) The inference from infinite space and matter to many worlds is the center of the argument. Nature made this world by chance collisions (forte offensando, temere), with no plan, so the same must be happening elsewhere (alios alibi congressus). A modern reader may hear the plurality of worlds of later cosmology; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.1061) The text is disputed: the manuscripts have *coluerunt*, which means "cultivated" or "inhabited" and does not give sense. Many editors emend to *coaluerunt* ("grew together"), as the translation does; the translator's note at this line should say so.

[DRAFT] (2.1067-1076) A second argument for many worlds: where matter, place, and the absence of any obstacle all exist, things must come to be. *Materies* is "matter", *semina rerum* "seeds of things", and the argument supposes that the same nature (eadem natura) that threw the seeds together here can do so elsewhere.

[DRAFT] (2.1077-1089) The argument from uniqueness: nothing in the sum is unique, since every kind has many members (in summa res nulla sit una). Lucretius then applies the principle to heaven, earth, sun, moon, and sea (1084-1086), which are likewise not unique but numberless. The conclusion rests on analogy with living species, and the reader should note that it depends on the premise that worlds, like animals, are born and die (1087-1089).

[DRAFT] (2.1087) The phrase *vitae depactus terminus alte* ("the boundary-stone of life, set deep") echoes the proem's *alte terminus haerens* (1.77, also 1.596, 5.89), the boundary-stone of each thing's power. Here the boundary is mortality, applied to worlds as well as to creatures.

[DRAFT] (2.1090-1092) The conclusion of the section, the freedom of nature from the gods (dominis privata superbis), prepares the attack on divine governance. This is the doctrine of the poem in miniature: nature acts by herself (ipsa sua per se sponte) without the gods (dis... expers). It also sets up the argument of 1093-1104.

[DRAFT] (2.1093-1104) Lucretius's question runs through a series of infinitives (regere, habere, convertere, suffire, esse praesto, faciat, concutiat, mittat, disturbet, saeviat). It is an argument from the gods' own nature: beings at peace (tranquilla pectora, placidum aevom) cannot also run the cosmos or throw thunderbolts. *Aevom* is "age", the register's rendering. The thunderbolt that strikes the guilty and the innocent alike (nocentes... indignos) is a standard Epicurean argument against divine justice, and it recurs at 6.387-422 and in Book 1.

[DRAFT] (2.1105) Lucretius's argument is that the world's growth came from bodies added "from outside" (extrinsecus) and gathered by the whole's own hurling (iaculando). The same intake from outside later reverses into loss, and that sets up the decay of the world at 1144–1145. "The great whole" renders omne, i.e. the sum of things.

[DRAFT] (2.1112) The principle is that like bodies are sorted to like by blows (plagis): moisture to moisture, earth to earth, fire to fire, and ether to ether. These are the four masses of the world (sea, land, fire, air or ether). "Kinds" renders saecla, and in this passage it means the categories of stuff each body belongs to.

[DRAFT] (2.1115) The play on procudunt ("forge") and the repeated nouns (ignem ignes, aetheraque aether) are in the Latin. The English keeps "forge" for both fire and ether, so the repetition shows.

[DRAFT] (2.1117) "The creating nature of things" renders rerum natura creatrix, a variant of the poem's title phrase. It appears here with a feminine agent noun, which makes nature a maker.

[DRAFT] (2.1121) Nature "reins in" growth (refrenat) at a fixed point. This is the doctrine of 2.1105–1174 that every composite thing has a limit set by its own balance of intake and loss. Lucretius states it here before applying it to living things and then to the world.

[DRAFT] (2.1144) The argument turns from living things to the world as a whole. The "walls of the great world" (moenia mundi) echo the "flaming walls of the world" of 1.73, here "stormed" (expugnata) like a besieged city. The conclusion that this world will decay is the reading of the passage as the poet states it, and it belongs with 2.1150's claim that the earth is already exhausted.

[DRAFT] (2.1148) The clause ne quiquam ("in vain") hangs awkwardly after the "must" of 1146–1147. It means that this supply of food is bound to fail, since the veins cannot take in enough and nature does not provide enough. The English keeps the loose attachment, as the Latin has it.

[DRAFT] (2.1150) The "worn-out earth" (effeta tellus) is a mother past childbearing, in the image Lucretius uses for the earth. The claim is that the earth once produced all the kinds of animals and now produces only small ones. Its fuller form, the earth as mother of living things, comes in Book 5.

[DRAFT] (2.1154) Homer's golden chain (Iliad 8.19) is the rope Zeus claims he could let down from heaven to haul up gods and men. Lucretius uses it, with *funis* ("rope") where Homer has seira, to deny that heaven sent mortals down. Some readers add the Stoic image of a linked chain of causes, but the sentence itself does not say so.

[DRAFT] (2.1169) The reading followed is momen ("drift", "movement", from movimen), which gives "blames the drift of time". Some editors read nomen, giving "the name of the times", which is easier to explain but a less natural object for incusat. The translation takes the harder reading, and the note on it should keep that choice open.

[DRAFT] (2.1174) The word capulum ("bier", "coffin", cf. "go to the tomb") belongs to the language of burial, and so the plowman's father and the old vinedresser's own world are figured as dead and going to their funeral. The line is the poem's last in this stretch and brings the world's old age back to the single man who grumbles about it. This is the end of the book's argument: the old man who praises the past does not see that all things, himself and the earth included, are wearing out together.
