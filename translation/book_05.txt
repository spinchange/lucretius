    1  Who has the strength of heart to build a song worthy
    2  of the majesty of things and of these discoveries?
    3  Or who is so strong in words that he could shape praises
    4  to match the merits of the one who left us such prizes,
    5  brought forth and sought out by his own heart?
    6  No one, I think, who is made of a mortal body.
    7  For if we must speak as the known majesty of things
    8  itself demands, he was a god, a god, renowned Memmius, {{NOTE: Epicurus, again unnamed (cf. 1.66); the deification is deliberate and is defended at 5.49-54}}
    9  who first discovered that reasoning of life which
   10  is now called wisdom, and who by his art
   11  took life out of such great waves and such great darkness
   12  and set it in so calm a place and in so clear a light.
   13  For compare the godlike discoveries of others long ago.
   14  For Ceres is said to have established grain for mortals, and Liber
   15  the liquor of the vine-born drink;
   16  and yet life could go on without these things,
   17  as report says some nations live even now.
   18  But a good life was not possible without a pure heart;
   19  so with all the more right this man seems to us a god,
   20  from whom even now, spread abroad through great nations,
   21  the sweet consolations of life soothe our minds.
   22  But if you think the deeds of Hercules stand above his,
   23  you will be carried much farther from true reasoning.
   24  For what harm to us now would that great gaping mouth
   25  of the Nemean lion do, or the bristling Arcadian boar?
   26  And how much could they hinder us, the birds that haunt Stymphalus? {{NOTE: the Labors of Hercules follow: the Nemean lion, the Erymanthian boar, the Stymphalian birds, the Cretan bull, the Lernaean hydra, Geryon, the mares of Diomedes, the serpent of the Hesperides}}
   27  Finally, what could the bull of Crete do, or the plague of Lerna,
   28  the hydra, palisaded round with venomous snakes?
   29  Or what the triple-breasted strength of three-bodied Geryon,
   30  and the horses of Diomedes, breathing fire from their nostrils
   31  near Thrace and the Bistonian lands and Ismarus,
   32  and, guarding the gleaming golden apples of the Hesperides,
   33  fierce, with savage glare, the serpent of enormous body
   34  coiled round the tree's trunk? What harm, finally, would it do
   35  there by the Atlantic shore and the stern stretches of the sea,
   36  where no one of ours goes and no barbarian dares?
   37  And the other monsters of this kind that have been destroyed,
   38  if they had not been conquered, what harm, pray, would they do alive?
   39  None, I think: for even now the earth swarms, to the full,
   40  with wild beasts, and is filled with trembling terror
   41  through groves and great mountains and deep forests,
   42  places which for the most part it is in our power to avoid.
   43  But unless the heart is purged, what battles then
   44  and dangers must work their way into us against our will!
   45  How great then are the sharp cares of desire that tear
   46  a troubled man apart, and how great, too, the fears!
   47  Or pride, filth, and wantonness, what of them? How great
   48  the disasters they bring about! What of luxury and sloth?
   49  Therefore the man who has subdued all these and from the mind
   50  driven them out with words, not arms, will it not be fitting
   51  that this man be deemed worthy of a place among the gods?
   52  Especially since he was used to speak, well and divinely,
   53  many sayings about the immortal gods themselves {{NOTE: the source prints "iam mortalibus"; read as immortalibus, "about the immortal gods"}}
   54  and to lay open in his words the whole nature of things.
   55  Treading in his footsteps, while I follow out his reasonings
   56  and teach in my words by what covenant all things
   57  are created, and how they must endure in it,
   58  and cannot annul the strong laws of time,
   59  in which kind, first of all, the nature of the mind was found
   60  to consist, first, of a body that had birth,
   61  and not able to endure unharmed through long ages,
   62  but that images are used to deceive the mind in sleep
   63  when we seem to see one whom life has left;
   64  for the rest, the order of my reasoning has brought me now to this,
   65  that I must give an account of how the world consists
   66  of a mortal body and at the same time had a birth;
   67  and in what ways that coming-together of matter
   68  founded earth, sky, sea, stars, sun,
   69  and the globe of the moon; then what living things
   70  arose from the earth, and which were at no time born;
   71  and in what way the human race, with varied speech,
   72  began to deal with one another through the names of things;
   73  and in what ways that fear of the gods worked its way into
   74  our hearts, which over the round of the earth keeps holy
   75  shrines, lakes, groves, altars, and images of the gods. {{NOTE: simulacra here are statues of the gods; "images" kept per the register, with a play on the Book 4 sense}}
   76  Further, the courses of the sun and the wanderings of the moon,
   77  I shall explain by what force steering nature bends them,
   78  so that we may not think these, between heaven and earth,
   79  run their everlasting courses freely, of their own accord,
   80  obliging the growth of crops and of living things,
   81  nor suppose they are rolled round by some reasoning of the gods.
   82  For those who have learned well that the gods lead a life without care, {{NOTE: 5.82-90 recur verbatim at 6.58-66; 5.88-90 repeat 1.75-77 and the English repeats those lines}}
   83  if yet meanwhile they wonder by what principle
   84  each thing can be carried on, above all in those things
   85  that are seen overhead in the shores of the ether,
   86  are carried back again into the old religions
   87  and take on harsh masters, whom the wretches believe
   88  can do all things, ignorant of what can come to be
   89  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded,
   90  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep.
   91  For the rest, not to keep you longer with promises,
   92  first of all look on the seas and lands and sky;
   93  their threefold nature, their three bodies, Memmius,
   94  three appearances so unlike, three such weavings,
   95  a single day will give to destruction, and, upheld through many years,
   96  the mass and machinery of the world will fall.
   97  Nor does it escape my mind how new and strange a thing
   98  it strikes the mind as, the coming destruction of heaven and earth,
   99  and how hard it is for me to prove it in words;
  100  as happens when you bring to the ears a thing unheard of before
  101  yet cannot put it under the sight of the eyes
  102  nor lay it in the hands, by which the paved road of belief
  103  leads nearest into the human heart and the precincts of the mind.
  104  But still I will speak out. Perhaps the fact itself will give belief
  105  to my words, and you will see, when violent earthquakes arise,
  106  all things shaken to pieces in a little time.
  107  May steering fortune turn this far away from us,
  108  and may reasoning rather than the event itself persuade
  109  that all things can be overcome and collapse with a horrible-sounding crash.
  110  Before I approach this subject, to pour out pronouncements
  111  more holy and with far more certain reasoning than
  112  the Pythia, who speaks from the tripod and laurel of Phoebus, {{NOTE: the priestess of Apollo's oracle at Delphi; repeats 1.739}}
  113  I shall set out for you many consolations in learned words,
  114  so that, reined in by religion, you may not think
  115  that the lands and sun and sky, sea, stars, moon,
  116  must remain eternal, being of divine body,
  117  and so suppose it right that, like the Giants, {{NOTE: the Giants who made war on the Olympian gods and were punished}}
  118  all those should pay the penalty for monstrous crime
  119  who by their reasoning shake the walls of the world
  120  and would put out the glorious sun of heaven,
  121  branding immortal things with mortal speech;
  122  although these are so very far removed from divine power
  123  and so unworthy to be counted among the gods
  124  that they may rather be thought to give us a notion
  125  of what is removed from vital motion and sense.
  126  For indeed it is not possible to think that with any body whatever
  127  the nature of the mind and judgment can exist.
  128  Just as a tree cannot be in the ether, nor clouds in the salt sea, {{NOTE: 5.128-141 closely repeat 3.784-797; the English should match its Book 3 lines where the Latin agrees}}
  129  nor can fish live in the fields,
  130  nor blood be in wood nor sap in stones,
  131  it is fixed and appointed where each thing may grow and be,
  132  so the nature of the mind cannot arise without a body
  133  alone, nor exist far from sinews and blood.
  134  For if it could, much sooner could the power of the mind itself
  135  be in the head or shoulders or the bottoms of the heels,
  136  and be used to be born in any part whatever,
  137  and still remain in the same man and the same vessel.
  138  But since even in our body it is fixed
  139  and seen appointed where the spirit and the mind
  140  can be and grow apart, so much the more must we deny
  141  that, wholly outside the body and the living form,
  142  they could endure in the crumbling clods of earth or in the sun's fire
  143  or in water or the high shores of the ether.
  144  Therefore these things do not exist endowed with divine sense,
  145  since they cannot be alive and quickened with the breath of life.

  146  Again, it is not possible for you to believe that the holy
  147  dwellings of the gods exist in any parts of the world.
  148  For the nature of the gods is thin, and far removed
  149  from our senses, and is scarcely seen by the thought of the mind;
  150  and since it slips away from the touch and stroke of hands,
  151  it must touch nothing that is tangible to us;
  152  for what may not itself be touched cannot touch.
  153  Therefore their dwellings too must also be unlike
  154  our dwellings, thin, in keeping with their bodies;
  155  and this I shall prove to you later in ample discourse. {{NOTE: the promised account of the gods' dwellings never appears in the poem as we have it}}
  156  Further, to say that for the sake of men they willed to prepare
  157  the glorious nature of the world, and that therefore
  158  it is fitting to praise the praiseworthy work of the gods,
  159  and to think that it will be eternal and immortal,
  160  and that it is unlawful for what by the gods' ancient reasoning
  161  was founded for the races of men to last through endless age
  162  ever to be shaken by any force from its seat,
  163  or harried with words and overturned from top to bottom,
  164  and to invent and add other things of this kind, Memmius,
  165  is folly. For what profit could our gratitude
  166  bestow upon immortal and blessed beings,
  167  that for our sake they should set about doing anything?
  168  Or what new thing could, so long after, lure beings at peace before
  169  to desire to change their former life?
  170  For it seems that one should rejoice in new things
  171  whom the old things harm; but one who met with nothing grievous
  172  in the time gone by, while he spent his life in beauty,
  173  what could kindle in such a one a love of novelty?
  174  Or what harm would it have been to us never to have been created?
  175  Did life, I suppose, lie in darkness and sorrow
  176  until the generative origin of things dawned?
  177  For whoever has been born must want to remain
  178  in life, so long as soft pleasure holds him;
  179  but for one who never tasted the love of life
  180  and was never in the count, what harm is it not to have been created?
  181  Further, the pattern for creating things, and the very
  182  notion of men, from where was it first planted in the gods, {{NOTE: notities: the Epicurean "preconception" (prolepsis); the gods could not have known what men were before men existed}}
  183  so that they knew and saw in their mind what they wished to make,
  184  or in what way was the power of the beginnings ever known, {{NOTE: principia, distinct from primordia; rendered "beginnings" here pending a register entry}}
  185  and what they could do with their order changed among themselves,
  186  if nature herself did not give a likeness of creating?
  187  For so many first-beginnings of things, in so many ways,
  188  driven by blows from infinite time till now,
  189  and roused by their own weights, have been used to move
  190  and to come together in every way and to try out everything
  191  that they could create by meeting with one another,
  192  that it is no wonder if they have also fallen into such arrangements
  193  and come into such courses
  194  as those by which this sum of things is now carried on, renewing itself.
  195  But even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  196  this I would dare, from the very order of heaven,
  197  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  198  that in no way has the nature of things been prepared
  199  for us by divine power: so great is the fault with which it stands endowed.
  200  First, of all that the vast sweep of heaven covers,
  201  a greedy portion the mountains and the forests of wild beasts
  202  have taken; rocks hold it, and vast marshes,
  203  and the sea, which keeps the shores of the lands wide apart.
  204  Then, further, nearly two parts the burning heat
  205  and the unceasing fall of frost take away from mortals.
  206  What is left of plowland, even that nature by her own force
  207  would choke with brambles, did human force not resist,
  208  used for life's sake to groan over the strong mattock
  209  and to cut through the earth with the plow pressed down.
  210  If we did not turn the fertile clods with the plowshare,
  211  and work the soil of the earth, and call them forth to birth,
  212  of their own accord they could not come out into the clear air.
  213  And even so, at times, when things won with great labor
  214  are now in leaf throughout the lands and all in flower,
  215  either the sun in the upper air scorches them with excessive heat,
  216  or sudden rains destroy them, and chill frosts,
  217  and blasts of wind harry them with violent whirlwind.
  218  Further, the race of wild beasts, bristling with horror,
  219  hostile to the human kind on land and sea,
  220  why does nature feed and increase it? Why do the seasons bring
  221  diseases? Why does untimely death go wandering?
  222  Then again the child, like a sailor cast up by the savage waves,
  223  lies naked on the ground, speechless, in want of every
  224  help for life, as soon as into the shining shores of light
  225  nature has poured him out with throes from his mother's womb,
  226  and he fills the place with mournful wailing, as is right
  227  for one who has so much of evil left to pass through in life.
  228  But the various flocks grow up, and herds, and wild beasts,
  229  and they have no need of rattles, nor must any of them be given
  230  the coaxing, broken baby-talk of a kindly nurse,
  231  nor do they look for different clothes to suit the season of the sky;
  232  finally, they need no arms, no high walls,
  233  to guard their own, since for all of them all things abundantly
  234  the earth herself brings forth, and nature, artificer of things.
  235  First, since the body of the earth, and moisture,
  236  and the light breaths of air, and the hot vapors,
  237  of which this sum of things is seen to consist,
  238  all consist of a body that is born and mortal,
  239  the whole nature of the world must be thought to be of the same.
  240  For indeed, things whose parts and members we see
  241  to be of a body that is born and of mortal shapes,
  242  these same we see to be, as a rule, mortal
  243  and born as well. Therefore, since I see the greatest
  244  members and parts of the world consumed and born again,
  245  we may know that heaven too, and likewise earth, have had
  246  some time of beginning, and will have their destruction.
  247  In these matters, do not think that I have taken too much
  248  for granted, in assuming earth and fire to be mortal,
  249  and in not doubting that moisture and the airs perish,
  250  and in saying that the same are born and grow again.
  251  First, no small part of the earth, scorched through
  252  by constant suns, beaten by the force of many feet,
  253  breathes out a haze of dust and flying clouds,
  254  which strong winds scatter through all the air. {{NOTE: the source prints "aereaëre", evidently a corruption of aere, "air"}}
  255  Part of the clods too is called back to flood
  256  by rains, and rivers graze and gnaw their banks.
  257  Further, whatever feeds and increases another, in its share
  258  is given back; and since beyond doubt it is seen
  259  that the mother of all is also the common tomb of things,
  260  therefore the earth, you see, is drawn upon and, increased, grows back.

261  For the rest, that the sea, the rivers, the springs always brim
  262  with new moisture, and that the waters flow without end,
  263  there is no need of words: the great downrush of waters
  264  declares it on every side. But whatever water comes first
  265  is taken off, and so on the whole the moisture never overflows,
  266  partly because the strong winds sweeping the levels of the sea
  267  diminish it, and the sun of the upper air, unweaving it with his rays,
  268  partly because it is spread out beneath, through all the lands;
  269  for the brine is strained out, and the matter of moisture
  270  oozes back, and all of it gathers at the heads of the rivers,
  271  and from there flows over the lands in a column of sweet water
  272  where once a channel was cut and carried the waves down with liquid foot.
  273  Now therefore I shall speak of air, which in its whole body
  274  is changed in numberless ways with every single hour.
  275  For always whatever flows off from things, all of it
  276  is carried into the great sea of air; and unless air in turn
  277  gave bodies back to things and renewed them as they flow,
  278  all things would by now have been dissolved and turned to air.
  279  Air therefore never ceases to be born from things and to fall
  280  back into things, since it is certain that all things constantly flow.
  281  Likewise the generous fountain of liquid light, the sun of the upper air,
  282  floods the sky constantly with fresh brightness
  283  and at once supplies light with new light.
  284  For whatever brightness comes first is lost to it,
  285  wherever it falls. You may learn this from the fact
  286  that as soon as clouds begin to pass beneath the sun
  287  and, as it were, to break through between the rays of light,
  288  at once the lower part of those rays is all lost
  289  and the earth is shadowed wherever the storm clouds are carried;
  290  so that you may know things always need new brilliance,
  291  and each first cast of brightness perishes,
  292  and that in no other way can things be seen in the sun
  293  unless the very fountainhead of light supplied it without end.
  294  Indeed, even the lights of night, the lights that are of the earth,
  295  hanging lamps, and torches bright with flashing
  296  flares and rich with much murky smoke,
  297  hurry by a like principle, with heat as their servant,
  298  to supply new light; they press on to flicker with fire,
  299  press on, and the light leaves no place as if broken between:
  300  so hurriedly is its destruction, from all its fires,
  301  hastened on by the swift birth of flame.
  302  So therefore we must think that sun and moon and stars
  303  cast out their light from one fresh rising after another
  304  and always lose whatever flames come first,
  305  so that you may not perhaps believe these thrive inviolable.
  306  Again, do you not see that even stones are conquered by age,
  307  that high towers fall and rocks crumble,
  308  that the shrines of the gods and their images grow weary and crack,
  309  and that the holy divinity cannot stretch out the boundaries
  310  of fate, nor struggle against nature's covenants?
  311  Again, do we not see the monuments of men fall in ruin,
  312  demanding in turn, you would think, whether they too grow old, {{NOTE: text corrupt; Bailey obelizes the line; the rendering gives only the probable sense}}
  313  and flints torn from the high mountains rushing down,
  314  unable to bear and endure the strong forces of an age
  315  that is finite? For they would not fall, torn suddenly away,
  316  if from infinite time they had borne up against
  317  all the torments of age without breaking.
  318  Again, look now at this, which round and above
  319  holds the whole earth in its embrace: if it creates from itself
  320  all things, as some relate, and takes them back when they perish,
  321  the whole consists of a body that is born and mortal.
  322  For whatever increases and feeds other things from itself
  323  must be diminished, and renewed when it takes things back.
  324  Further, if there was no generative beginning
  325  of earth and heaven, and they have always been eternal,
  326  why, beyond the Theban war and the deaths of Troy,
  327  have not other poets also sung of other things?
  328  Where have so many deeds of men so often fallen, and why nowhere
  329  do they flower, grafted on eternal monuments of fame?
  330  But, as I think, the whole is new, and the nature
  331  of the world is recent, and not long ago took its beginnings.
  332  And so even now some arts are being polished,
  333  even now are growing; now much has been added
  334  to ships; lately players have given birth to melodious sounds;
  335  finally, this nature of things and its reasoning were found
  336  only lately, and I myself am now found the very first
  337  who can turn it into the speech of my fathers.
  338  But if perhaps you believe that all these same things were before,
  339  but that the generations of men perished in burning heat,
  340  or cities fell in some great upheaval of the world,
  341  or that after constant rains ravening rivers
  342  went out across the lands and buried the towns,
  343  all the more must you, conquered, confess
  344  that destruction of earth and heaven too will come;
  345  for when things were assailed by such diseases and such
  346  dangers, then, if a grimmer cause had pressed upon them,
  347  they would have spread disaster and great ruin far and wide.
  348  Nor in any other way do we seem to be mortal,
  349  except that we sicken, one with another, with the same diseases
  350  as those whom nature has removed from life.
  351  Further, whatever things endure eternal must of necessity {{NOTE: 5.351-363 repeat 3.806-818 nearly verbatim; conform to the Book 3 English}}
  352  either, because they are of solid body, repel blows
  353  and suffer nothing to pierce them that could break apart
  354  the close-packed parts within, as are the bodies
  355  of matter, whose nature we have shown before,
  356  or be able to last through all time because
  357  they are free of blows, as the void is,
  358  which stays untouched and suffers not a whit from a blow,
  359  or else because there is no supply of place around
  360  into which things might, as it were, part and be dissolved,
  361  as the sum of sums is eternal, and outside it
  362  there is no place into which things might leap apart, nor bodies
  363  that might fall upon it and dissolve it with a strong blow.
  364  But neither, as I have taught, is the nature of the world
  365  of solid body, since void is mixed in things,
  366  nor yet is it like the void; nor indeed are bodies lacking
  367  that might perhaps gather together out of the infinite
  368  and overthrow this sum of things with a violent whirlwind
  369  or bring upon it some other disaster of danger,
  370  nor further is the nature of place and the space of the deep
  371  lacking, into which the walls of the world could be scattered,
  372  or they can be struck by any other force and perish.
  373  The door of death, therefore, is not shut to the sky,
  374  nor to sun and earth, nor to the deep waters of the sea,
  375  but stands open and looks toward them with its vast and monstrous gape.
  376  Therefore you must confess that these same things
  377  were born; for things that are of mortal body
  378  could not, from infinite time until now,
  379  have scorned the strong forces of immeasurable age.

  380  Finally, since the greatest members of the world
  381  fight so fiercely among themselves, stirred by a war in no way holy, {{NOTE: pium bellum is the Roman term for a war duly declared with the proper rites; this war among the elements is anything but that}}
  382  do you not see that some end of their long contest
  383  can be granted them, either when the sun and all heat
  384  have drunk up all the moisture and prevailed?
  385  This they strive to do, and have not yet carried out their attempt;
  386  so much do the rivers supply, and they threaten besides
  387  to flood all things from the deep gulf of the sea:
  388  in vain, since the winds that sweep the levels of the sea
  389  diminish it, and so does the sun in the ether, unweaving it with its rays,
  390  and they trust that they can dry all things up
  391  before the water can reach the end of its undertaking.
  392  Breathing such war in an equal contest,
  393  they strive to settle great matters between them,
  394  though once, meanwhile, fire has been the stronger,
  395  and once, as the story goes, water reigned over the fields. {{NOTE: the flood of Deucalion, which matches the burning under Phaethon that follows}}
  396  For fire prevailed and, ranging around, burned up much,
  397  when the ravening force of the sun's horses swept Phaethon {{NOTE: Phaethon, son of the Sun, who drove his father's chariot and lost control of it; Jupiter struck him down}}
  398  off the road through the whole ether and over all the lands.
  399  But the father almighty, roused then with bitter anger,
  400  with a sudden stroke of the thunderbolt dashed great-hearted
  401  Phaethon from the horses to the earth, and the Sun, meeting him
  402  as he fell, caught up the eternal lamp of the world,
  403  and brought back the scattered horses and yoked them, trembling,
  404  then, steering them on their own road, restored all things,
  405  as, to be sure, the old poets of the Greeks have sung.
  406  But this is driven very far away from true reasoning.
  407  For fire can prevail when more bodies of matter
  408  have risen up out of the infinite;
  409  then its forces fall, overcome by some principle,
  410  or else things perish, burned up by the scorching air.
  411  Water likewise once rose up and began to prevail,
  412  as the story goes, when it overwhelmed the lives of men with its waves;
  413  then, when that force was turned aside by some principle and withdrew,
  414  whatever of it had risen up out of the infinite,
  415  the rains stopped and the rivers lessened their force.
  416  But by what means that gathering of matter
  417  founded the earth and the sky and the depths of the sea,
  418  the courses of the sun and the moon, I shall set out in order.
  419  For certainly not by design did the first-beginnings of things {{NOTE: 5.419-431 largely repeat 1.1021-1028; reconcile verbatim with the Book 1 rendering}}
  420  place themselves each in its order by keen intelligence,
  421  nor indeed did they bargain what motions each would give;
  422  but because many first-beginnings of things in many ways,
  423  struck by blows from infinite time till now,
  424  and driven by their own weights, have been used to travel
  425  and to come together in every way and try out everything
  426  that they could create by meeting with each other,
  427  therefore it comes about that, spread abroad through a great age,
  428  by trying every kind of union and motion
  429  at last those come together which, suddenly thrown together,
  430  often become the origins of great things,
  431  of earth and sea and sky and the race of living things.
  432  At that time neither could the sun's wheel be seen with its ample light
  433  flying on high, nor the stars of the great world,
  434  nor sea nor sky nor, finally, earth nor air,
  435  nor could anything be seen like the things we know,
  436  but a kind of strange storm and a mass, risen up.
  437  Then parts began to fly apart from one another, and like things
  438  to join with like, and to mark off the world,

Notes (DRAFT)

[DRAFT] (5.8) "Graius homo" is again left unnamed: the god is Epicurus (cf. 1.66), and Lucretius says "deus ille fuit, deus" outright. The deification is meant seriously within the poem's terms and is defended at 5.49-54, where the benefactor who frees men from passion "with words, not arms" is held worthy of the gods. The translation keeps the doubled "a god, a god."

[DRAFT] (5.14) Ceres (fruges) and Liber (liquoris vitigeni laticem) are the Roman grain-goddess and the wine-god, identified with Bacchus. They stand for the "divine" inventions of agriculture and viticulture that the poem ranks below Epicurus's discovery of how to live. The English "Liber" keeps the Latin cult-name.

[DRAFT] (5.22) Hercules, the great benefactor-hero of Greek and Roman tradition, is here measured against Epicurus. The twelve Labors that follow (5.24-36) are listed as monsters cleared from the earth, and the argument is that they are trivial next to the inner "monsters" of desire and fear (5.43-48).

[DRAFT] (5.26) The Labors run from the Nemean lion and the Erymanthian boar ("Arcadius sus") to the Stymphalian birds (Stymphala colentes), the Cretan bull, the Lernaean hydra, Geryon, the mares of Diomedes of Thrace, and the serpent guarding the Hesperides' golden apples. The list is not in the canonical order, and the Latin "Stymphala colentes" is more exactly "those dwelling at Stymphalus", which the English takes as the birds.

[DRAFT] (5.35) "Atlanteum litus" is the far western shore beyond the Pillars of Hercules, where the Hesperides' garden was located. "Noster … nec barbarus" means neither Roman nor foreigner ventures there, i.e. nobody at all.

[DRAFT] (5.53) The Perseus text reads "iam mortalibus e divis", which is nonsensical against "e divis" and is probably a reading of "immortalibus" (or a corruption close to it), so the sense is "about the immortal gods." The translation follows that sense and reads "ipsis immortalibus" as what Epicurus used to say "divinely" about the gods. Editors differ on the exact reading, so the translator should check against the apparatus.

[DRAFT] (5.55) "Cuius ... vestigia" picks up the unnamed Epicurus again (cf. 5.8 and 1.66), and "ingressus vestigia" echoes the proem's "ingredior ... vestigia" at 3.3-4, where Lucretius follows the Greek master's footsteps. The "reasonings" (rationes) are the Epicurean arguments of Books 1-3 that Lucretius now summarizes before turning to cosmology.

[DRAFT] (5.59) "Animi natura" is rendered "the nature of the mind", the subject of Book 3 (the mortality of animus and anima). The back-reference sums up Book 3's doctrine, and "simulacra ... in somnis" (5.62) refers to the Book 4 theory of dream-images, here kept as "images" per the register.

[DRAFT] (5.74) "Terrarum ... orbi" and the catalogue of shrines, lakes, groves, altars, and images (fana lacus lucos aras simulacraque) name the physical objects of Roman cult. The poet gives the origin of the fear of the gods (divom metus) as one of the topics of this book, which is taken up at 5.1161-1240.

[DRAFT] (5.82) These lines (5.82-90) recur at 6.58-66, and 5.88-90 repeat 1.75-77. The English repeats the earlier lines word for word, and "deos securum agere aevom" states the Epicurean doctrine that the gods live untroubled and do not govern the world, which is the point against "antiquas religiones".

[DRAFT] (5.86) "Religiones" here is the plural of the register term "religion", and the sense is the old cult-fears and observances that return when men cannot explain heavenly phenomena. The English keeps "religions" rather than "superstitions", as the register requires.

[DRAFT] (5.93) "Naturam triplicem, tria corpora" names sea, land, and sky, which the Epicureans (and Lucretius) treat as aggregates formed in time and so dissolvable. The "machina mundi" is the poem's image of the cosmos as a built structure, and "una dies" is the single day of its dissolution, which sets up the prediction of collapse in 5.95-96. A reader may hear in this a hint of modern cosmic mortality, but that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (5.102) "Via ... munita fidei" is the "paved road of belief": sight and touch are the most direct routes to conviction, and since the destruction of the world cannot be shown to either, Lucretius must rely on argument (dictis). The "templa mentis" are the regions or precincts of the mind (templa as in "Acherusia templa", never "temples").

[DRAFT] (5.107) "Fortuna gubernans" is "steering fortune", a parallel to "natura gubernans" at 5.77. This is a conventional prayer that the catastrophe not occur in the speaker's lifetime, and its note of piety sits oddly with the poem's denial of divine providence.

[DRAFT] (5.112) The Pythia is the priestess of Apollo (Phoebus) at Delphi who spoke from the tripod, with the laurel as the god's plant. The comparison is borrowed from Lucretius's own critique at 1.738-739 of Empedocles, whose verses he says are worthy of the oracle, but here the claim is that his own pronouncements are "more holy and more certain."

[DRAFT] (5.117) The Giants (Gigantum) were the earth-born sons of Gaia who attacked Olympus and were punished by Jupiter. The Latin "ritu par esse Gigantum" is the claim that men who deny the divinity of the heavenly bodies would be seen as being like the Giants, and this is probably a thrust at Stoic or traditional piety that treated sun and moon as gods.

[DRAFT] (5.121) "Inmortalia mortali sermone notantes" is the accusation that Epicureans label immortal things with a mortal tongue, which is the charge Lucretius sets up to refute. In his view, sun, moon, sea, and earth are not divine at all, so the charge fails.

[DRAFT] (5.128) These lines (5.128-141) closely repeat 3.784-797, the Book 3 argument that the mind cannot exist outside the body, and the English matches its Book 3 lines where the Latin agrees. The point is the Epicurean doctrine that each thing has its fixed place (certum ac dispositum), so mind (animi natura) needs the sinews and blood of a body.

[DRAFT] (5.140) "Anima atque animus" is rendered "the spirit and the mind", following the register: anima is the vital principle ("spirit") and animus the ruling mind. In Book 3 Lucretius treats them as two parts of one nature, and the line is a compressed reminder of that.

[DRAFT] (5.146-147) Epicurean doctrine compressed: the gods live in the intermundia, the spaces between worlds, not in any part of our world (in mundi partibus ullis). The point opposes the Stoic and popular view of a divinely inhabited cosmos; the argument runs from the gods' thin nature to the unlikeness of their dwellings (146-155).

[DRAFT] (5.148-149) "Thin" renders tenvis, "the thought of the mind" is animi mente, the register's merge of mens with animus in a non-technical phrase. On Epicurean doctrine the gods are perceived only by the mind, through very fine images, not by the senses.

[DRAFT] (5.155) The promise (quae tibi posterius largo sermone probabo) is not fulfilled in the poem as transmitted. The gods' dwellings are touched on at 3.18-24 but not proved at length. Whether this reflects the poem's unfinished state or a lost passage is disputed; the translation keeps the future tense as written.

[DRAFT] (5.156-164) One long sentence whose subject is the infinitive clause "to say that..." and whose predicate is "desiperest" (is folly) at 165. The target is the teleological view (Stoic in particular) that the world was made for men, and that it is impious to question its eternity. "Memmi" is the dedicatee, Gaius Memmius, addressed again here.

[DRAFT] (5.160) "Ratione vetusta" is rendered "ancient reasoning", with ratio in the sense of design or plan, a deliberate fit with the register's "reasoning". The line is difficult in construction: "quod... fundatum" is the object of the infinitives "sollicitare... vexare... evertere" in 162-163.

[DRAFT] (5.165-167) The argument from the gods' self-sufficiency, a core Epicurean tenet: beings blessed and immortal need nothing from men and cannot be put in their debt. Compare the denial of divine anger or favour at 2.646-651 (= 1.44-49), a passage whose authenticity is contested in the proem.

[DRAFT] (5.175-176) "Credo" is ironic ("I suppose"), mocking the supposition that life before creation lay in darkness. The Epicurean point is that non-existence involves no deprivation (cf. 3.830-869); the reader may also note "genitalis origo" (generative origin), echoing the "genitalia corpora" of the register.

[DRAFT] (5.181-182) "Notities" is the Epicurean preconception (Greek prolepsis), the mental pattern of a thing; Lucretius argues the gods could have had no such pattern of men before men existed. The translation gives "notion" for lack of an English technical term.

[DRAFT] (5.184) "Principiorum" is rendered "beginnings", a different word from "first-beginnings" (primordia), since the Latin differs. The term is a synonym for the same entities, here with "vis" (power) of the atoms, a point the register should record as a deliberate distinction.

[DRAFT] (5.186) The sense of the question that opens at 181 closes here: "if nature herself did not give a likeness of creating" (speciem creandi). Here "species" is rendered "likeness", in the register's sense of pattern or model.

[DRAFT] (5.187-194) The first-beginnings, by infinite chance combinations over infinite time, must have fallen on the arrangements that form this world. This is the same argument as 1.1021-1028 and 2.1067-1076 with the same wording of a random, trial-and-error process; a modern reader may hear a distant echo of selection, but that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (5.195-199) The poet claims he could refute design from the visible faults of heaven alone, even if the primordia were unknown. "Tanta stat praedita culpa" opens a catalogue of the world's defects (200-234), a standard Epicurean argument against providence.

[DRAFT] (5.204) "Duas partis" (two parts) is meant loosely: of the habitable earth remaining after mountains, seas and marshes, nearly two parts in three are lost to heat and frost, which is the poet's rhetorical estimate rather than a measurement.

[DRAFT] (5.206-212) The syntax is difficult and 207-212 are punctuated differently in editions. The translation follows the Perseus text: the sequence of potential clauses (would choke with brambles, could not come out) depends on 207 "ni vis humana resistat" and 210-212 as the further conditional "if we did not turn..." Note "ad ortus" at 211 as "to birth", the clods called forth to growth.

[DRAFT] (5.222-227) The child as a shipwrecked sailor is a famous passage of pessimism; the baby is cast up (proiectus) as if from the waves. "Luminis oras" (224) is the refrain "shining shores of light", first at 1.22 and here recalled in a bitter context. Lucretius is arguing from the evidence of a natural world not designed for human good.

[DRAFT] (5.235-239) The argument that the world is mortal begins: the four constituents (earth, moisture, air, fire) are each mortal, so the whole is. "Principio" opens a new section after the transition from 234. Note that "omnia nativo ac mortali corpore constant" rests on the doctrine that composite bodies are born and perish, only the first-beginnings being immortal.

[DRAFT] (5.240-246) The inference from the parts to the whole: what is composed of mortal, born parts is itself mortal. "Principiale aliquod tempus" is "some time of beginning", the world having a first moment and a final destruction, as argued at length in the rest of Book 5. The word "caeli" (heaven) names the sky or upper part of the world.

[DRAFT] (5.247-250) The poet anticipates an objection that he is begging the question in treating earth, fire, water and air as mortal. Here Lucretius is, without naming, contradicting Empedocles and other thinkers for whom the four elements were eternal roots; the "I said" (dixi) refers back to earlier discussion in Books 1 and 2.

[DRAFT] (5.254) The reading "aereaëre" is evidently corrupt in this printed text; the translation gives "air" (aere). Editors differ on the repair, and the printed form should not be taken as a manuscript reading.

[DRAFT] (5.259) "Omniparens... commune sepulcrum" (mother of all and common tomb) is the same formulation as 5.259 echoing 1.250-251 and the 2.998-1000 passage on the earth as the mother of creatures and their burial. The earth is both womb and grave.

[DRAFT] (5.261) Lucretius opens his argument against the sea's, the sky's, and the sun's permanence with the water cycle. The sea does not overflow because wind and sun draw it off (verrentes... deminuunt) and the earth filters the brine (percolatur... virus) so that fresh water rises at the river heads. The "unweaving" (retexens) is the translator's literal rendering of the sun's loosening of moisture.

[DRAFT] (5.269) "Matter of moisture" renders materies umoris, using the register's "matter" for what the physics treats as the water's stuff. A reader should not hear "matter" here as the technical first-bodies. It is the water itself, filtered clean and returning.

[DRAFT] (5.273) The text prints Aëra with a diaeresis (Greek accusative aēra). "Air" here is Lucretius's name for the surrounding fluid medium, the "great sea of air" (aeris in magnum... mare) into which things flow and from which they are renewed. This is the flux argument of Book 2 applied to the cosmos: every body sheds and receives, so none is fixed.

[DRAFT] (5.281) The sun is called a "fountain of liquid light" (liquidi fons luminis), the same image of light as a flowing stream that Lucretius uses in the Book 2 passage on the sun's rays. The point is that the sun is not a static body but a continual source, its light spent and replaced at once. The comparison to lamps and torches (5.294-301) argues that this replacement is what permanence looks like.

[DRAFT] (5.302) This is the conclusion for the heavenly bodies (solem lunam stellasque): sun, moon, and stars lose their fires continuously and so cannot be "inviolable" (inviolabilia). The denial that the heavens are immortal and divine is the central target of the book's opening sections. Epicurus is not named, but the argument is his.

[DRAFT] (5.308) "Images" here translates simulacra in the plain sense of statues of the gods, not the Book 4 films that drift from surfaces. A reader who knows the technical term should not read it back into this line. The point is that even the cult's own objects decay, and the "holy divinity" (sanctum numen) cannot extend fate's limits (fati... finis) or resist nature's covenants (naturae foedera).

[DRAFT] (5.312) The line is corrupt, and Bailey obelizes it (the Latin quaerere proporro, sibi cumque senescere credas, does not construe). The translation gives only the probable sense: that the monuments, as if questioning in turn, seem to ask whether they too age. This is a reconstruction of meaning, not a rendering of a secure text.

[DRAFT] (5.326) Lucretius's argument from the lack of earlier poetry is that if the world had always existed, the Theban war and Troy (bellum Thebanum et funera Troiae) would not be the oldest themes. The Theban cycle and the Trojan war are the two great epic subjects. He argues that the absence of earlier songs shows the world to be recent.

[DRAFT] (5.336) Lucretius claims to be the first to turn the Epicurean doctrine into Latin (in patrias... voces, "the speech of my fathers"). The claim is not strictly true, but it is a boast of priority within Latin verse. Note "reasoning" for ratio, as in the register: the doctrine's method as well as its content.

[DRAFT] (5.339) Here Lucretius sets out the Stoic and Empedoclean catastrophes: conflagration (torrenti... vapore, associated with Phaethon's myth and with Heraclitus and Stoic ekpyrosis) and flood. He turns them against themselves: if the world has survived such disasters, they show how fragile it is, and a worse one would end it.

[DRAFT] (5.351) These lines repeat 3.806-818 nearly verbatim, where they were used to show that the soul is mortal. Here the same classification of what can be eternal (solid body, void, or the infinite sum with nothing outside) is applied to the world. The English should conform to the Book 3 rendering; this is the trichotomy: solidity, the void, or having no external place for dissolution.

[DRAFT] (5.361) "Sum of sums" renders summarum summa, the whole of all that exists, the infinite universe. It is eternal because there is no outside from which it could be struck or into which it could fly apart.

[DRAFT] (5.373) The "door of death" (leti... ianua) is Lucretius's image for the openness of the world to destruction. The reader may feel the grim humor in the sky and sun and sea having no closed door against death; the gape (hiatu) is vast. This is the poem's usual way of making the argument felt and not merely stated.

[DRAFT] (5.381) The Romans called a war duly declared, with the fetial priests' rites observed, a *bellum pium* (pio ... bello); Lucretius turns the phrase around, so that "in no way holy" (nequaquam pio) says the elemental war is without any rite or sanction. The sense is "impious" as well as "undeclared."

[DRAFT] (5.395) The flood is not named here. The reader will probably think of Deucalion's flood, which the Greeks paired with Phaethon's conflagration as the two great catastrophes (umor ... regnarit in arvis). Lucretius gives it only the hedge "as the story goes" (ut fama est). The same phrase returns at 5.412, so the hedge is a refrain.

[DRAFT] (5.397) Phaethon, son of the Sun, borrowed his father's chariot and lost control of it, scorching the earth until Jupiter struck him down with a thunderbolt (Phaethonta ... rapax vis solis equorum). Lucretius tells the myth in full only to reject it at 5.406 as "driven very far away from true reasoning" (a vera ... ratione). He keeps the poets' version of the event but replaces its cause with a shift in the balance of matter.

[DRAFT] (5.419) Lines 419-431 closely repeat 1.1021-1028. That passage says the first-beginnings did not arrange themselves by design but, after trying every combination through endless time, fell into the patterns that make the world. The translation should therefore match its Book 1 rendering word for word wherever the Latin is the same (primordia rerum, ex infinito, plagis ponderibusque). Here "first-beginnings" is the fixed rendering of primordia.

[DRAFT] (5.421) "Nor did they bargain what motions each would give" (pepigere) uses the language of contract. The image is of the first-beginnings as parties to an agreement, which the poet denies. This is a negative counterpart to the later "covenants" of nature (foedera naturae).

[DRAFT] (5.440) Here the first-beginnings are called *principia* (omnigenis e principiis), a variant of primordia. The register's rule of distinct Latin for distinct English suggests keeping the variation visible. A rendering such as "beginnings of every kind," as the translation has it, can do that, with the Latin noted.

[DRAFT] (5.456) The pun on *elementa* ("elements") is the register's: the same word names the letters of the alphabet in Lucretius's analogy between atoms and letters (1.197, 2.688ff.). Here it means the smallest constituent bodies, "much smaller elements" than those of earth (multoque minoribus ... elementis). The doctrine is that the lighter, finer bodies are the ones that rise.

[DRAFT] (5.457) The earth's "open pores" (rara foramina) express the doctrine that even apparently solid things contain void, which is what lets the lighter bodies escape. The reader may associate this with Lucretius's earlier arguments for the inane (1.329ff.), though the verse does not repeat them.

[DRAFT] (5.478) The comparison of the heavenly bodies, which neither sink nor rise but turn in the middle region, to limbs that stay still while others move (in nobis quaedam ... membra) is Lucretius's own analogy. It sustains his picture of the world as a body with "members" (membra, 5.381, 5.439), and links the cosmic and the human.

[DRAFT] (5.495-497) The earth's settling is the last stage of the cosmogony: heavy matter sinks "like dregs" (ut faex) and the lighter elements are sorted out above it. The lines continue the sequence begun in 5.449 ff., where earth, sea, air and fire separate out by weight.

[DRAFT] (5.498-505) The ether (aether ignifer) is the outermost, finest layer of the cosmos. The poet pictures it as steady and untroubled, carrying the stars' fires, while the air below it is stormy (turbantibus, procellis).

[DRAFT] (5.507) The Pontus (Pontos) is the Black Sea. Its steady outflow through the Bosporus was a commonplace in ancient writing, and Lucretius uses it as a visible analogy for the ether's even flow (certo aestu, unum tenorem).

[DRAFT] (5.509) Nunc quae sit causa canamus (the cause of the motions of the stars) opens the astronomical section. Lucretius gives a list of possible causes, not one.

[DRAFT] (5.510-516) The first model is a rotating celestial sphere (magnus orbis) held at its poles by air, driven by a current above or below (alium supra… alium supter). The comparison is to a water-wheel (fluvios versare rotas), which turns when the current strikes its paddles.

[DRAFT] (5.521) The Latin summania templa is a textual puzzle. It may refer to Summanus, the Roman god of nocturnal thunder, so that the phrase means "nightly precincts of heaven," or it may be a corrupt or variant reading for "highest." The translation takes "nightly," following the Summanus reading.

[DRAFT] (5.526-533) This is the Epicurean doctrine of multiple explanations (plurisque… causas, plures causae). For celestial phenomena, which cannot be observed directly, any cause consistent with the appearances is admissible. Lucretius claims only that one of these causes must hold in our world (una… necessest), and that the surviving options are those that occur "in various worlds" (in variis mundis), a reference to the infinity of worlds.

[DRAFT] (5.534-538) The earth stays at rest because the air below it is "joined from the beginning" (ex ineunte aevo coniunctam) to its parts, so the earth is not a load placed on air. A modern reader may hear a distant echo of the idea that weight is relative to position, but that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (5.556-563) The comparison to the anima (vis animai) and the animus (animi vis) works as a proof by analogy: the body's weight does not burden the limbs, just as the air does not burden the earth. The register terms are anima = "spirit" and animus = "mind," and here the two are used almost interchangeably for the vital force.

[DRAFT] (5.564-573) The point about the size of the sun is Epicurean: the senses do not lie about the size of distant bodies (nostris sensibus esse videtur). The sun is as large as it appears, or very nearly so, and the argument is that fire loses almost nothing in apparent size over distance.

[DRAFT] (5.574) This line (perveniunt nostros ad sensus et loca fulgent) repeats 571 in the source and does not belong to the argument at this point. The translation keeps it, as the source prints it, so the files stay aligned line for line.

[DRAFT] (5.575) The notho lumine ("bastard light") is borrowed light, reflected from the sun. Lucretius leaves open whether the moon shines by reflected or by its own light, as part of the same multiple-explanations method.

[DRAFT] (5.596) This line repeats 584 in the source and does not fit its context, so editors treat it as a stray intrusion. The translation keeps it for alignment.

[DRAFT] (5.599) The elementa vaporis ("elements of heat") are the first-beginnings of fire. As in the register, elementa recalls the letters of the alphabet, which Lucretius elsewhere compares to atoms.

[DRAFT] (5.614-617) Lucretius gives two or more explanations for the sun's turning between the tropics, as his method requires where the evidence cannot decide. Capricorn (aegocerotis, "goat-horned") marks the winter turning-point and Cancer (canceris … metas) the summer one, the "goal-posts" being the turning-posts of a racecourse.

[DRAFT] (5.622) The "holy judgment" (sancta … sententia) is Democritus's, the atomist predecessor of Epicurus, whom Lucretius honors here even while borrowing his explanation only as one possibility. The theory is that bodies nearer the earth are less swept along by the heavenly whirl (turbine), so the sun lags behind the fixed stars and seems to move backward through the signs. The translation follows the Latin and does not clarify the mechanics.

[DRAFT] (5.637-645) The second explanation proposes alternating currents of air (aeraër, a doubled-vowel manuscript spelling of aër) that push the sun between the summer and winter signs. Lucretius supports it with the analogy of clouds moving in different directions at different heights (646-649). Neither this nor the Democritean theory is asserted over the other.

[DRAFT] (5.656) Matuta is the old Italian goddess of dawn, identified with the Greek Leucothea. Her name stands here for the dawn itself, in the same figurative way as Ceres and Bacchus later (742-743); Lucretius elsewhere permits such names provided one does not take them for gods (2.655-660).

[DRAFT] (5.663-665) "The story goes" (fama est) marks this as report, not observation: the legend that fires seen scattered on Mount Ida at daybreak gather into a single orb. It supports the claim that the sun's fire may be assembled anew each dawn from scattered seeds (semina … ardoris, "seeds of heat").

[DRAFT] (5.679) The Latin text prints "conseque" and the translation does not follow it letter for letter. The usual correction is consequiae ("consequences, sequels"), which gives the sense "in sequence" here; the reading is a conjecture because the manuscripts are corrupt.

[DRAFT] (5.687-688) "Node of the year" (anni nodus) is the equinox, the point where the ecliptic crosses the celestial equator and day and night are equal. The argument is that the sun's oblique path (obliquo … lumine, 693) through the zodiac (signiferi … orbis, 691) divides the sky into unequal arcs except at that point.

[DRAFT] (5.701-704) "They seem to speak the truth" (videantur dicere verum) most plausibly refers back to those who hold the sun is renewed daily from gathering fires (660-665); the pronoun is not stated in the Latin. The point is that the varying speed of gathering would account for the seasonal change in the sun's rising-point, so the doctrine would be borne out.

[DRAFT] (5.705-714) This begins the section on the moon's phases, again offered as a set of alternatives. First, the moon shines by reflected sunlight (solis radiis percussa), as those hold who picture it as a ball (pilai) moving beneath the sun. The reader may hear in this the correct modern explanation; the poem presents it only as one possibility among several, with no preference given.

[DRAFT] (5.727-730) The "Babylonian doctrine of the Chaldaeans" (Babylonica Chaldaeum doctrina) held that the moon is itself half-luminous and turns, as in the second ball theory (720-726), while the Greek astronomers (astrologorum artem) held that it is lit by the sun. Lucretius's point is that each side denies the other without a decisive reason, so both remain open; the Latin of 729-730 is compressed and the translation keeps it close.

[DRAFT] (5.731-736) The third alternative is that a new moon is created each month, in a fixed order, and destroyed. Lucretius defends it by the regularity of other recurring things, which prepares the procession of the seasons that follows.

[DRAFT] (5.737-747) The procession of the seasons is a personified set-piece in the manner of a Roman triumphal or religious procession. Venus and Cupid ("winged herald", Veneris praenuntius … pennatus), Zephyr, Flora, Heat, Ceres, the etesian winds, Bacchus (Euhius Euan, from the ritual cry euhoe), Autumn, Volturnus, Auster, Bruma and Hiemps appear in turn. The names are poetic ornament for natural events, not divine agents, as Lucretius insists elsewhere; Bruma and Hiemps are the winter solstice and winter, and Volturnus is a south-east wind. The passage concludes by returning to the moon (748-750) with the argument from regularity.

[DRAFT] (5.764) The cone is the earth's shadow, which narrows away from the sun (coni... umbras); in a lunar eclipse the moon passes through it. The "rigid" shadows are the dense, sharply bounded dark of the cone's core.

[DRAFT] (5.771) This line repeats 764 verbatim (menstrua dum rigidas coni perlabitur umbras). Editors treat it as a misplaced duplicate. It follows 770, where it breaks the syntax of the question, and the translation prints it anyway to keep the source order and numbering.

[DRAFT] (5.751) Lucretius gives several possible causes for eclipses and does not choose among them (pluribus e causis). This is the Epicurean method of multiple explanations: for phenomena that cannot be observed directly, every cause consistent with the appearances is allowed to stand. The lunar eclipse he offers is the earth's shadow, but he also allows an unseen dark body, the sun's own fading, or the moon's own light failing.

[DRAFT] (5.772) The long sentence recalls the astronomical account just given and announces the return to the youth of the world (mundi novitatem, 780). The "closing of eyes" (conivent) in 778 pictures the sun and moon as eyes that shut and open. It adds to the rhetorical conceit of the personified heavens without being a doctrinal claim.

[DRAFT] (5.781) Crerint is the contracted form of decreverint, "had decided," so the earth is shown choosing what to bear first and what to risk to the winds (incertis... ventis). The translation keeps the sense of decision. "The shining shores of light" (in luminis oras) is the register's fixed phrase for birth into daylight, and it recurs at 1.22, 1.170 and 1.179.

[DRAFT] (5.791) The "mortal generations" (mortalia saecla) are the animal species, human beings included. The earth bears plants first, then animals, as the body first grows hair and feathers (788-790). Lucretius's idea of a spontaneous earthly origin of living things belongs to the Epicurean tradition and is not his own invention.

[DRAFT] (5.795) "Mother" (maternum nomen) is a deliberate argument, not an ornament. The earth deserves the name because everything came from her (e terra... creata). Lucretius rejects the claim that animals fell from the sky or rose from the sea (793-794), and he returns to the title at 821-822.

[DRAFT] (5.808) The "wombs" (uteri) are rooted growths in the soil that held the first infants, and the earth then nursed them with a milk-like juice (sucum... consimilem lactis). The picture is a literal analogy to human birth and lactation (813-815). A modern reader may hear a faint resemblance to later ideas of spontaneous generation, but that is the reader's association and not the poet's.

[DRAFT] (5.825) The source prints two spellings of aerias run together (aeriasqueaëriasque). The sense, "and with them the birds of the air," is not in doubt, and the translation follows it.

[DRAFT] (5.828) Lines 828-836 are Lucretius's argument that the world ages and changes (mutat... mundi naturam totius aetas). The earth ceased to bear as a woman past childbearing does (827). The idea that the world itself grows old and weaker is distinctly Lucretian here: the whole cosmos has a life cycle, and the earth's present inability to produce what it once did (quod potuit nequeat) follows from that. Elsewhere, at 2.1150-1174, he says the same of the world's decline.

[DRAFT] (5.839) The Latin of this line is corrupt in the manuscripts (androgynem, interutras necutrumque utrimque remotum). The sense, a hermaphrodite creature of both sexes and of neither, is clear, and the translation follows the reading that gives it. The androgyne belongs with the other monsters (portenta) that the earth tried to make and could not sustain.

[DRAFT] (5.846) The failed monsters (monstra ac portenta) are the poem's account of why only some kinds survive. They die because they cannot feed, grow up, or breed (847-848), since nature "frightened off their growth" (natura absterruit auctum). A modern reader may hear natural selection in this, and in 855-877 where only protected kinds survive. That is the reader's association, since Lucretius is not describing change in species over time.

[DRAFT] (5.859) The source misprints tuta as "tutaTA." The line says that cunning, courage, or speed has kept each surviving kind safe from the start of its life (ex ineunte aevo). The translation reads tuta.

[DRAFT] (5.867) Lucretius addresses Memmius directly here (Memmi) and again in the second person. Memmius, the dedicatee, was a Roman aristocrat and politician, and the poem is addressed to him throughout. The creatures "handed over to our protection" (tutelae tradita) are the animals that survive by their usefulness to humans (dogs, horses, sheep, cattle).

[DRAFT] (5.878) The passage argues from growth rates and constitution: composite beasts cannot exist because their parts would mature, age, eat and desire differently. Centaurs (Centauri) are half man, half horse; "alienigenis membris" ("limbs from alien stocks") names the mismatch of origins on which the whole argument turns.

[DRAFT] (5.884) The horse is "in flower" (floret) at three while the boy still seeks the breast (ubera mammarum in somnis lactantia quaeret), so the two halves of a Centaur could not keep pace with each other. The verb "flower" is used of both horse and boy, and returns for the human at 888 (florenta iuventas).

[DRAFT] (5.890) "Veterino semine equorum" ("seed of draft horses") uses the technical "seed" for animal generation, not the atomic "seeds of things" (semina rerum). It is the same word on a different level, and the context makes clear that it is the biological sense here.

[DRAFT] (5.892) Scylla is the sea monster of the Odyssey, with a woman's upper body and a girdle of dogs. "Semimarinis" ("half of the sea") describes her lower half, and she is the second of Lucretius's examples of composite creatures.

[DRAFT] (5.897) The translator's query about Venus is answered by the register, which keeps the name "Venus" for sexual desire throughout (nec simili Venere ardescunt). The metonymy is common in Latin, and the same use recurs at 962. The English does not gloss it as "desire", so the divine name stays visible.

[DRAFT] (5.905) The Chimaera is the Homeric fire-breather: lion in front, serpent behind, and in the middle a she-goat (Greek *chimaira*). Lucretius says only "media ipsa, Chimaera" ("in the middle herself, the Chimaera"), and the pun on the goat is not carried by the English "herself". The point of the argument is that a lion's flesh would burn in its own fire like all other flesh (flamma ... torrere atque urere).

[DRAFT] (5.907) "Tellure nova caeloque recenti" ("when the earth was new and the sky fresh") refers to the newness of the world, which was the usual ground for the claim that early earth could produce monsters. Lucretius answers that "newness" is only a name (novitatis nomine inani), and then adds the fantasy of golden rivers and jewelled trees as absurd instances. The reading "suesse" at 912 is the correct form of the corrupted printing noted by the translator.

[DRAFT] (5.916) "Semina rerum" ("seeds of things") is the register's fixed term for the first-beginnings, here used in the argument that, even with many seeds present in the new earth, nothing shows that mixed creatures could arise. The proof is by analogy: plants today, which the same earth bears, cannot be intertwined with one another (non ... inter se possunt complexa creari).

[DRAFT] (5.924) "Foedere naturae certo" ("by nature's fixed covenant") is the first appearance in this segment of the register term "covenant" (foedus), the legal word for a treaty. It names the lawlike limits within which each kind arises, and the phrase recurs in the poem's accounts of what can and cannot be (cf. 1.584 on fixed limits for every thing).

[DRAFT] (5.925) Here begins the account of early humans, the "primitive" or "hard" race that the verse sets against the softness of later life. It is a Lucretian anthropology, not a golden age: the humans are tougher because the earth that bore them was "dura" ("hard"), and the tone of the whole passage is of toil and fear (miseris mortalibus, 944).

[DRAFT] (5.932) "Volgivago ... more ferarum" ("in the roaming manner of wild beasts") begins a sequence of negations (nec ... nec ... neque) that describes what early humans lacked: ploughing, planting, pruning, fire, clothes, common good, laws. The reader is meant to feel the accumulation of absence.

[DRAFT] (5.941) "Arbita" are the fruit of the strawberry tree (Arbutus unedo), which ripens red in late autumn and winter, as the line says. Lucretius treats it as the food of early humans, and it returns at 965 as a payment for sex.

[DRAFT] (5.948) The "templa nympharum" ("woodland precincts of the nymphs") are sacred places only in the loose sense of haunts; the register's rule that *templa* means regions or precincts, not "temples", applies. The nymphs are the spirits of springs and woods in Roman belief, and the passage is the first mention of anything like religion in the human story.

[DRAFT] (5.950) The repetition "umida saxa, umida saxa" ("the wet rocks, the wet rocks") is Lucretius's own, a deliberate echo that makes the line pick up the previous one and slow the rhythm, as water drips. The English keeps the doubling.

[DRAFT] (5.958) "Commune bonum" ("a common good") and "legibus" ("laws") mark what is missing before the origin of society, which Lucretius takes up later in the book (from 1011). The reader will meet again the contrast between the state of nature and the social contract; the verse does not use the later vocabulary, and any such association is the reader's own.

[DRAFT] (5.962) The three modes of union (mutual desire, violence, price) are laid out with *vel ... vel ... vel* ("either ... or ... or"). The cost of acorns, arbute berries and pears recalls the earlier line on the foods (939-942).

[DRAFT] (5.970) "Saetigeris ... subus" ("bristly boars") is a simile of sleeping like beasts: the people lie naked on the ground and cover themselves with leaves (foliis ac frondibus). It sits with 984-987, where wild beasts drive them from their beds.

[DRAFT] (5.973) The passage on night and day (973-981) answers the fear that the sun might not return. Lucretius says early humans did not wonder at it because they had seen the alternation since childhood (a parvis ... consuerant); the argument is from habit, and it anticipates his later account of how wonder at the heavens gave rise to fear of the gods.

[DRAFT] (5.976) "Rosea face sol" ("the sun with rosy torch") is a poetic image of the dawn sun, borrowed from epic and tragedy, where the sun or Dawn carries a torch. It is one of the few places in the passage where the diction rises toward epic.

[DRAFT] (5.997) The placeholder asks what "vermina" are. The word is rare: it seems to mean sharp internal pains, griping of the bowels (from vermen, a twisting), and the translation takes it so ("gripings"). Some editors and readers take it as "worms" infesting the wounds, which would fit the foul sores (ulcera tetra, 995) but not the "savage" (saeva) pains.

[DRAFT] (5.1006) The placeholder asks about ratio. Here "improba navigii ratio" means the technique or art of seafaring, not Epicurean "reasoning" as in the register's usual rendering. The translation uses "craft", a deliberate departure from the fixed term, since the sense is practical skill.

[DRAFT] (5.1011) The lacuna after 1012 is marked in the text. The transmitted "cognita sunt" (1013) has lost its subject, and the sense probably concerned the bonds of marriage or the first knowledge of family ties. The translation prints the lacuna unnumbered and supplies nothing. The sentence is therefore broken at that point, and "were known" has no subject in the English.

[DRAFT] (5.1017) "Venus inminuit viris" is the goddess standing for sexual love, which Lucretius says sapped the primitive strength (viris). Here Venus is a force in the human story rather than the divine patron of the proem. In the translation the name is kept and the sense left to the context.

[DRAFT] (5.1025) "Foedera" (covenants) is the register's term for nature's covenants, but here it means the human compacts of mutual non-aggression made among neighbours (1019-1020). This is the beginning of Epicurean social contract. Lucretius says it did not hold universally, only for "a good and great part" (bona magnaque pars), and the translation keeps "covenants" so the vocabulary connects to the later political account (5.1136 ff.).

[DRAFT] (5.1028) This opens the argument on the origin of language, which is directed against the view that one man gave things their names. "Natura" and "utilitas" (need) together cause speech: nature supplies the capacity for varied sounds, need shapes the names. The parallel with children pointing (1030-1032) is offered as evidence of how it happens, not as an analogy for its own sake.

[DRAFT] (5.1041) The "someone" who "distributed names" (nomina distribuisse) is a Greek-philosophical target, probably the doctrine, found in Plato's Cratylus and in Stoic thought, of a name-giver (nomothetes). The attack is Epicurean: language arose naturally from the earliest humans' feelings and needs, not from the invention of a single wise man. Lucretius does not name the opponent.

[DRAFT] (5.1047) The placeholder asks about notities. In Epicurean terms it is the "preconception" (Greek prolepsis), a general concept stored in the mind from repeated experience of things. Lucretius's argument is that the supposed first name-giver could not have had any notion of the usefulness of speech unless others had already been using it, since such a notion is formed by experience.

[DRAFT] (5.1063) The placeholder concerns the Molossian dog. It was a large breed from Molossia in Epirus, prized in antiquity as a guard and hunting dog, and a stock example of fierce, powerful dogs. The translation keeps the ethnic adjective as Lucretius has it.

[DRAFT] (5.1075) "Pinnigeri Amoris" is the winged Cupid (Love) personified as the source of the stallion's desire. The "spurs" (calcaribus) are the goad of passion, an image drawn from riding. The Latin uses the mythological figure for an animal drive, and the translation keeps the personification.

[DRAFT] (5.1091) This line turns from language to fire, answering an objection Memmius (the addressee) might silently raise: how did humans get fire? The sentence that follows gives lightning (fulmen) and friction of branches as the two possible sources (1101, "quorum utrumque"), without deciding between them, which is typical of Lucretius's method of multiple explanations. The same fire is then said to have been put to use in cooking, taught by the sun (1102-1104).

[DRAFT] (5.1105) The subject of this passage is the same as the preceding one: the kings and talented men who lead the change from the primitive way of life to cities and property. "Fire" (igni) points back to the discovery of fire and cooking earlier in the book's history of civilization.

[DRAFT] (5.1108) The account of early kingship (reges) is a hypothetical sociology, not a record: Lucretius gives kings a citadel as "defense and refuge" (praesidium perfugiumque), a pair of terms he will reuse for the fearful human mind at 5.1186, where the same word (perfugium) names the refuge of handing everything to the gods.

[DRAFT] (5.1113) The invention of gold (aurum repertum) reverses the old standard of "looks and strength" (facies, vires) with wealth. Here the Epicurean doctrine that natural needs are small begins to surface, and it is stated outright at 1117-1119 ("to live sparingly with a level mind," vivere parce aequo animo), which echoes Epicurus' teaching that natural wealth is limited and easily obtained.

[DRAFT] (5.1117) "True reasoning" (vera ratione) is the register's fixed rendering of ratio as the Epicurean method; the line states the school's ethical doctrine that frugality is itself riches, in a compressed form that a reader might take for a proverb.

[DRAFT] (5.1125) The thunderbolt simile (quasi fulmen) is an image of envy (invidia) striking down the powerful, and it prepares the poem's later treatment of the thunderbolt as a natural event misread as divine punishment (5.1218-1221). The Latin syntax is loose here, with the subject (invidia) held back to the next line, and the English keeps that delay. "Tartarus" (Tartara) is the lowest region of the underworld, used here as a figure for ruin and not as a place Lucretius believes in.

[DRAFT] (5.1135) The line (nec magis id nunc est neque erit mox quam fuit ante) is a remark on how little the habit of "wisdom from another's mouth" has changed, and it closes the digression on ambition. Some editors suspect the line is out of place, but the translation follows the transmitted text without comment on the point.

[DRAFT] (5.1136) With "the kings slain" (regibus occisis) Lucretius turns to the fall of monarchy and the rise of magistrates and law. This is a historical sketch in the manner of contemporary social-contract thought, with no named event; Roman readers might hear in it the expulsion of the Tarquins, but the poet does not say so.

[DRAFT] (5.1155) "Common covenants of peace" (communia foedera pacis) belongs to the register's family of foedera, the Epicurean idea that justice is a compact made for mutual advantage and not a thing existing in nature. Epicurus' maxims on justice as a pact not to harm or be harmed lie behind it.

[DRAFT] (5.1161) Here begins the account of the origin of religion. Lucretius's explanation (causa) is psychological and anthropological: early men experienced dreams and waking visions of the gods and drew false conclusions, which accords with Epicurean doctrine that the gods exist as images but have no concern for the world. The "images" (simulacra) are not named in this passage, but the "forms" (facies) seen in sleep and waking are the same phenomenon treated in Book 4.

[DRAFT] (5.1175) The reasoning that early men "gave them eternal life" (aeternamque dabant vitam) because the visions kept returning corresponds to the Epicurean account of the gods as supplied by a ceaseless stream of images (subpeditabatur). The reader should note that Lucretius accepts the gods' blessed immortality as true elsewhere, and what he faults is the inference from it to divine governance of the world.

[DRAFT] (5.1183) Lucretius gives a second source of belief: ignorance of causes (nec poterant ... cognoscere causis) for the regularity of the heavens. This is the standard Epicurean diagnosis, and the poem argues throughout that the heavens work by natural causes, which Book 5 goes on to explain (5.509 ff.).

[DRAFT] (5.1190) Line 1190 is textually troubled in the manuscripts, and editors have tried to repair or bracket parts of the catalogue of heavenly phenomena in 1189-1193. The translation follows the Latin as printed in the source, repeating "the moon" and "night" as the Latin does.

[DRAFT] (5.1194) The apostrophe "O unhappy human race" (O genus infelix humanum) is the poet's own cry against the consequences of the error. It may be set beside the denunciation of religion at 1.101 ("tantum religio potuit suadere malorum"), where the same charge is made.

[DRAFT] (5.1198) The note on veiled head: the Roman rite required the worshipper to cover the head with a fold of the toga (velatum caput) when sacrificing, as opposed to the Greek practice of sacrificing bareheaded. Lucretius sets "piety" (pietas) against this show and defines the true kind as a mind at peace (pacata mente) in the line that follows.

[DRAFT] (5.1204) The description of fear arising on looking at the heavens (cum suspicimus ... caelestia ... templa) begins a passage that makes the sky the occasion of religious dread. "Walls of the world" (moenia mundi, 1213) is the register's fixed rendering, as at 1.73, where they are "flaming"; here the question is whether they can last, and the Epicurean answer, given at length elsewhere in Book 5, is that every world is born and will perish. A reader may hear in the worry about the world's end an anticipation of later cosmology, but that is the reader's association and not the poet's.

[DRAFT] (5.1211) "Poverty of reasoning" (rationis egestas) names the cause of fear: the mind has no explanation to hand. The line carries the poem's central claim that terror follows from ignorance, and the same idea underlies the refrain at 1.146-148, where the "face and reasoning of nature" (naturae species ratioque) is what scatters it.

[DRAFT] (5.1226) The storm-at-sea example with the "commander of a fleet" (induperatorem classis) uses an archaic form of imperator, as in Ennius, and probably suggests a Roman general with legions (legionibus) and war elephants (elephantis), such as those of Pyrrhus or Hannibal. The point is that even the mighty pray in vain (nequiquam) and drown anyway.

[DRAFT] (5.1234) The note on fasces: the fasces (fascis) were bundles of rods with an axe (securis) carried by lictors before Roman magistrates as emblems of their authority to flog and execute. Lucretius shows the "hidden force" (vis abdita) crushing even these signs of power, which recalls the fall of kings and thrones at 1136-1140.

[DRAFT] (5.1236) The earthquake (tellus ... vacillat) is the last of the three illustrations of terror, after thunder and storm. Lucretius's explanation of earthquakes is given in Book 6; here the phenomenon serves only as a reason for the mortal habit of assigning command (gubernent) to gods.

[DRAFT] (5.1241) The metals' discovery by forest fire is Lucretius's own rationalist account; the sequence (copper and bronze, gold, silver, lead, then iron) follows the order of use in the Epicurean history of culture, which assumes that human skills arise from chance and necessity and not from divine gift. "Potestas" for lead and silver is the sense of their "power" or effective property, and the translation keeps "weight" and "power" literally.

[DRAFT] (5.1250) The hunting aside is compressed: Lucretius says that trapping with a pit and fire (fovea atque igni) is an older method than the organized hunt with nets and dogs, which supports fire as a possible origin of the forest blaze. It is offered as one of several alternative causes, in the Epicurean manner of "multiple explanations" (seu… sive… sive).

[DRAFT] (5.1252) "Quicquid id est, qua cumque e causa" closes the list of possible causes without choosing among them, a regular Epicurean method for phenomena that cannot be settled by observation. The doctrine, not the particular cause, carries the argument.

[DRAFT] (5.1257) The English renders "concreta" as "hardened" and keeps "bronze" for "aes", since the Latin word covers both copper and bronze. Reading "aeris" as "of bronze" follows the register, though a reader should know that Lucretius does not distinguish the two metals.

[DRAFT] (5.1261) "Lacunarum… vestigia": the "lacunae" here are the hollows or pits in the ground, an ordinary Latin sense, and have nothing to do with a gap in the text. The metal took the shape of the cavity it had run into, and this suggested casting.

[DRAFT] (5.1275) The reversal of esteem between bronze and gold is a Roman observation of the poet's own day, and "nunc" marks it as contemporary. It leads to the general lesson of 1276-1280 on how the value of things shifts with time.

[DRAFT] (5.1282) Memmius is addressed here by name for the first time in this segment; the second-person address and the claim that the reader can work it out "per te" reflect the poet's habit of drawing the dedicatee into the argument.

[DRAFT] (5.1283) The list of "ancient weapons" (hands, nails, teeth, stones, branches, fire) belongs to the Epicurean primitive stage of humanity before metal. The order bronze before iron in 1286-1288 matches the Three Age pattern known to Hesiod and later to modern archaeology, though a modern reader should take that as the reader's association and not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (5.1302) The "Lucanian oxen" (boves Lucas) are elephants, so called because Romans first met them in Lucania in the army of Pyrrhus (280 BC). "Poeni" are the Carthaginians, who used elephants against Rome in the Punic Wars, and "anguimanus" (snake-handed) describes the trunk. The English keeps "Lucanian oxen" as Lucretius's own coinage and does not substitute "elephants".

[DRAFT] (5.1308) The catalogue of animals in war (bulls, boars, lions) is Lucretius's argument that such inventions are doomed because the beasts turn on their own side. The pattern of "ne quiquam" (in vain) recurs at 1271, 1313, and 1332, which marks each failed experiment.

[DRAFT] (5.1319) The manuscripts give "patebant" (lay open), which does not suit the sense; most editors read "petebant", "made for", as in the English. The reading is an emendation, and the translation follows it.

[DRAFT] (5.1327) Lines 1327 and 1328 are two forms of one line, one with "suo… sanguine" and the other with "in se fracta", and both appear in the manuscripts. Editors keep one and bracket or delete the other; the translation prints both, as the source does, so the files stay aligned.

[DRAFT] (5.1341) Lines 1341-1349 are often suspected as a later addition or unrevised alternative draft, since the poet's doubt that men foresaw the harm sits awkwardly with the preceding account. The translation prints them as given.

[DRAFT] (5.1348) "In variis mundis varia ratione creatis" invokes the Epicurean doctrine of an infinite number of worlds (mundi), here used to say that such a war might have happened on some other world as well. A modern reader may hear an anticipation of later plurality-of-worlds thinking, but that is the reader's association, not Lucretius's claim. The register renders "mundus" as "world".

[DRAFT] (5.1354) The manuscripts have a doubled word, "coegit coëgit", a scribal doubling of a single form; the translation reads "coegit". The sentence says that wool-working was first a man's task by nature's compulsion, before it passed to women.

[DRAFT] (5.1357) "Agricolae… severi" refers to the stern farmers who reproached the men for the work and so assigned wool to women while taking on the hard labor themselves; the passage is Lucretius's own aetiology of the division of labor and is not found in other Epicurean sources.

[DRAFT] (5.1362) The Latin order is *rerum ... natura creatrix*, with *primum* between them, so *rerum* can go with *natura* (the title formula, which the register fixes as "the nature of things") or with *creatrix* ("creator of things"). The English takes the second reading and drops the formula. Check the renderings at the lines you cite (1.629, 2.1117), which I have not seen, and decide whether this is a deliberate exception, or whether "the nature of things herself, the creator" would keep the formula.

[DRAFT] (5.1387) *Dius* can mean "divine, godlike" and also "bright, of the open air", as in *sub divo*, "under the open sky". The English takes the open-air sense; "godlike leisure" would hint at the Epicurean picture of the gods' ease, which is not obviously in play here.

[DRAFT] (5.1388-1389) This is the first appearance of a refrain that returns at 5.1454-1455, with *eruit* ("draws out, digs out") here and *erigit* ("raises") there. The English matches at 1388 and 1454, and varies only the verb, as the Latin does. The phrase is *luminis oras* without *dias*, so it is not the full refrain "the shining shores of light" (1.22 and elsewhere); keep "the shores of light" without "shining".

[DRAFT] (5.1392-1396) These lines reuse 2.29-33, with changes: *saepe itaque* for *cum tamen*, *corpora habebant* for *corpus habemus*, *ridebat* for *adridet*, and *pingebant* for *conspergunt*; the person and tense shift from "we" to "they". Only the unchanged Latin (*inter se prostrati in gramine molli / propter aquae rivom sub ramis arboris altae*, and *anni tempora ... viridantis floribus herbas*) should match the Book 2 English word for word. I have not seen the Book 2 English, so check it. The reuse makes the first men's simple pleasure the same as ours, which is the point of 1409-1411.

[DRAFT] (5.1408) *Vigiles* are those who stay awake (cf. *vigilantibus*, 1405). "Watchmen" suggests an office, so "the wakeful" may be safer here. The same word is used of the sun and moon at 1436 (*vigiles mundi*), and the English should either keep that echo in both places or drop it in both.

[DRAFT] (5.1409) *Genus* looks like the subject of *didicere*: "and the race (of men now) has learned to keep the measure", set against the *silvestre genus* of 1411 and *hominum genus* at 1430. "In its kinds" in the English seems to take it otherwise; please recheck. Also, *extra numerum* (1401, "out of time") and *numerum servare* (1409, "keep the measure") are a deliberate pair, so the English should use the same word for *numerum* in both.

[DRAFT] (5.1411) *Terrigenae* (also 1427), "the earth-born", are the first humans, whom Book 5 earlier describes as brought forth by the earth. The word is not in the register; consider adding "earth-born" for *terrigenae* so that 1411 and 1427 stay identical.

[DRAFT] (5.1419-1422) *Quam reor* marks the story of the first wearer of a pelt, killed by ambush while the pelt was torn apart in the fight, as Lucretius's own inference rather than a received myth. The English keeps the conjecture visible with "I believe".

[DRAFT] (5.1429) *Plebeia* ("common", the cloth of the plebs) sets purple and gold against plain cloth. The same contrast appears in 2.34-36, just after the lines reused at 1392-1396, where purple coverlets (*ostro rubenti*) are weighed against a *plebeia vestis* as cures for fever. "Common" here and in Book 2 should be rendered alike.

[DRAFT] (5.1432-1433) The compressed doctrine is that natural pleasure has a limit (*quae sit habendi finis ... quoad crescat vera voluptas*). Beyond that limit, more possessions add only empty cares (*curis inanibus*, 1431), not more pleasure.

[DRAFT] (5.1436) *Templum* is a marked-off region of the sky, originally the augur's space for observation, so "precinct", as for *Acherusia templa* (1.120), is right. The sun and moon are *vigiles* here, echoing the human *vigiles* of 1408; see the note there.

[DRAFT] (5.1444) The Latin as supplied prints "poetaepoëtae", two spellings run together, apparently a source artifact for *poetae*. The English is unaffected, but check the line against `latin/`.

[DRAFT] (5.1445) *Elementa* here are the letters of the alphabet, so the line says that writing was invented not long before the poets began to record deeds. "Elements" is kept per the register, which also uses the word for atoms (compare the letter analogy at 1.197), and the double sense is the point.

[DRAFT] (5.1446-1447) The claim that earlier times lie beyond our view (*respicere aetas nostra nequit*) matches the argument at 5.324-327 that no poets sing of anything before the Theban War and the fall of Troy. *Nisi qua ratio vestigia monstrat* names the method of the whole account: inference from traces by reasoning.

[DRAFT] (5.1451) *Daedala* here qualifies the finished work (*signa polita*), not a maker, so it cannot be "artificer" as at 1.7 (*daedala tellus*) and 5.234. The register should record it as a second sense, "artful" or "skilfully wrought", with the same root, Daedalus the craftsman. The English currently has "artful", which fits.

[DRAFT] (5.1452-1453) *Progredientis* is genitive and agrees with *mentis*, so the mind itself is "advancing step by step". The English "as men advanced" shifts the subject slightly. The Latin verb is singular (*docuit*), with *usus* and *experientia* as its subjects.

[DRAFT] (5.1454-1455) These lines repeat 5.1388-1389 (see the note there), with *erigit* for *eruit*. The English at 1454 should stay identical to 1388, with "raises" rendering the changed verb.
