BOOK 1

Invocation of Venus and the praise of Epicurus; nothing comes from nothing
and nothing returns to nothing; unseen bodies, and the void; the properties
and accidents of things; the first-beginnings are solid, eternal and without
parts; against Heraclitus, Empedocles and Anaxagoras; the universe has no
end in matter or in space.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

This translation is plain, exact modern English free verse, kept line for
line with the Latin of the Perseus text, which follows Bailey's Oxford text
in orthography and numbering, so that any English line can be checked
against its original. It follows the printed text throughout. Where the text
prints lines out of numerical order (15 before 14, 156-158 before 155, 1086
before 1085) the translation keeps the printed order and the printed
numbers. Where lines are lost it prints an unnumbered gap and supplies
nothing: the line marked 860a, the eight lines missing after 1093, the torn
right-hand ends of 1068-1073, and the suspected gap after 1013. Where
editors delete or emend, the notes say so and the translation keeps the
printed word (the stray repeat at 769, the "body" of the sea at 271,
"Aeolia" at 721), with one exception: at 520 it reads vacaret, "stood
empty", with nearly every editor, since the printed vocaret gives no sense.

Lucretius writes a physics without a technical vocabulary and makes one as
he goes, complaining twice in this book of "the poverty of the language"
(139, 832). The translation does the same and keeps the terms rigid.
Primordia, his commonest name for the ultimate bodies, is "first-beginnings"
every time, and his shorter principia is "beginnings", so that the two Latin
words stay apart; the other names he lists at 58-61 are kept distinct too
(matter, generative bodies, seeds of things, first bodies). The word "atom"
never appears in the verse, because Lucretius never uses it; the notes say
it once. Inane is "void", never "vacuum". Concilium, the combination of
first bodies that makes a thing, is "union"; simplicitas, their being
without parts, is "singleness"; coniuncta and eventa are "conjoined" and
"accidents". Ratio, the hardest word in the poem, is "reasoning" where it is
the doctrine or the method, "by what principle" where it is instrumental,
and "order" in the "highest order of heaven" at 54. Religio is "religion",
the plain word; the proem's attack has no force if it is softened to
"superstition". Animus is "mind", anima "spirit", never "soul"; mens beside
animus is "thought". Simulacra are "images", the word Book 4 will make
technical; elementa is "elements" even where it means letters, because the
analogy between first-beginnings and letters (197, 823-829, 912-914) rests
on the one word.

Lucretius repeats himself on purpose. Lines 146-148 close the proem and
return at the head of three later books; 670-671 return three times; 75-77
come back as 594-596; 926-950 become the proem of Book 4; 738-739 return in
Book 5. Where the Latin repeats, the English repeats its own earlier lines
word for word, and the lines fixed in this book are binding on the rest of
the poem. The same goes for the formulas of transition ("For the rest",
"Come now", "as I showed you above, a little while before") and for the
connectives. This is a poem that argues, and its "for", "further", "again",
"finally" and "therefore" are its skeleton; the English keeps every one,
which makes it more insistent than modern verse is used to being, and that
insistence is Lucretius.

The book's shape is this. A hymn to Venus as the generative power of nature
(1-43), with a prayer for peace in a bad time for Rome, sits beside a
statement that the gods take no part in our affairs (44-49), and the notes
record the editors' unease. Epicurus is praised, unnamed (62-79), and the
charge of impiety is turned back on religion by the sacrifice of Iphianassa
(80-101). The physics then proceeds in steps, each announced: nothing from
nothing (146-214), nothing to nothing (215-264), unseen bodies (265-328),
the void (329-417), body and void as the only two natures and everything
else as their property or accident (418-482), the solidity, eternity and
least parts of the first-beginnings (483-634). The polemic against
Heraclitus, the four-element theorists and Anaxagoras (635-920) is the
liveliest stretch, and the hardest to follow without its connectives. After
the digression on the poet's own method (921-950) the book ends with the
infinity of space and matter (951-1051) and the ridicule of a world with a
center (1052-1117), broken by the two worst gaps in the text.

    1  Mother of Aeneas' race, pleasure[1] of men and gods,
    2  nourishing Venus, who beneath the gliding signs of heaven
    3  fill with life the ship-bearing sea, the grain-bearing lands,
    4  and throng them, since through you every kind of living thing
    5  is conceived and, risen, looks upon the light of the sun:
    6  you, goddess, you the winds flee, you the clouds of heaven flee,
    7  you and your coming; for you the artificer[2] earth sends up
    8  her sweet flowers, for you the levels of the sea laugh,
    9  and the sky, made calm, shines with light poured wide.
   10  For as soon as the face of the spring day stands revealed
   11  and the fertile breath of the west wind, unbarred, grows strong,
   12  first the birds of the air, goddess, announce you and your
   13  coming in, their hearts struck through by your power.
   15  Then wild beasts and herds go bounding over the glad pastures[3]
   14  and swim the rushing rivers: so, captured by your charm,
   16  each follows you eagerly wherever you go on to lead.
   17  Then through seas and mountains and sweeping rivers,
   18  through the leafy homes of birds and the greening plains,
   19  striking seductive love into the breasts of all,
   20  you make them eagerly propagate their generations, kind by kind.
   21  Since you alone steer the nature of things,
   22  and without you nothing rises into the shining shores of light[4],
   23  and nothing comes to be glad, nothing lovely,
   24  I long for you as my partner in writing these verses
   25  which I attempt to set down on the nature of things
   26  for our son of the Memmii[5], whom you, goddess, at every season
   27  have willed to excel, adorned with every gift.
   28  All the more, goddess, give my words a lasting charm.
   29  Bring it about that meanwhile the savage works of war
   30  over all seas and lands be lulled and lie at rest;
   31  for you alone can help mortals with tranquil peace,
   32  since Mars[6], mighty in arms, who rules the savage works
   33  of war, often throws himself back into your lap,
   34  conquered by the eternal wound of love,
   35  and so, gazing up, his rounded neck laid back,
   36  feeds on love his hungry eyes, gaping at you, goddess,
   37  and the breath of him, reclining, hangs upon your lips.
   38  Goddess, as he lies back upon your holy body,
   39  pour yourself around and over him, and from your lips
   40  pour out sweet speech, asking calm peace, glorious one, for the Romans;
   41  for neither can we, in a bad time for our country, do this work
   42  with an untroubled mind, nor can the famous offspring of Memmius
   43  in such a pass be wanting to the common safety.
   44  For the whole nature of the gods must, of itself, by necessity[7]
   45  enjoy immortal life in utmost peace,
   46  removed from our affairs and separated far away;
   47  for free from every pain, free from dangers,
   48  strong in its own resources, needing nothing of ours,
   49  it is neither won by services nor touched by anger.
   50  For the rest, lend ears set free, and a keen mind
   51  withdrawn from cares, to true reasoning[8],
   52  so that my gifts, laid out for you with faithful care,
   53  you do not, before they are understood, scorn and leave behind.
   54  For I shall begin to set out for you the highest order
   55  of heaven and the gods, and unfold the first-beginnings[9] of things,
   56  from which nature creates all things, makes them grow, and feeds them,
   57  and into which, when they perish, that same nature dissolves them,
   58  which we, in giving our account, are used to calling matter
   59  and the generative bodies of things, and to name
   60  seeds of things, and to use for these same things the term
   61  first bodies, because from them, as first, all things are.
   62  When human life lay foully before the eyes,
   63  crushed on the earth beneath the weight of religion[10],
   64  which showed its head from the regions of heaven,
   65  looming over mortals with horrible aspect,
   66  first a man of Greece[11] dared to lift mortal eyes
   67  against it, and first to take a stand against it;
   68  him neither the fame of gods nor thunderbolts nor heaven
   69  with its threatening growl held down; rather they goaded
   70  the keen courage of his mind the more, so that he longed
   71  to be first to break the tight-barred gates of nature.
   72  And so the living force of his mind prevailed, and far
   73  beyond the flaming walls of the world[12] he went out
   74  and traveled the whole immensity in thought and mind[13],
   75  from where he brings back to us, a victor, what can come to be
   76  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded,
   77  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep[14].
   78  Therefore religion in its turn is thrown beneath our feet
   79  and trampled; the victory lifts us level with heaven.
   80  In these matters I fear this: that you might think
   81  you enter on the impious elements of reasoning and set foot
   82  on the road of crime. Rather, the opposite: more often it is
   83  religion that has borne the criminal and impious deeds.
   84  As at Aulis, where the altar of the Virgin of the Crossways[15]
   85  was foully defiled with the blood of Iphianassa
   86  by the chosen leaders of the Danaans, the first of men.
   87  As soon as the ribbon, bound about her maiden hair,
   88  streamed down in equal lengths on either cheek,
   89  and she saw her father standing sorrowful before the altar,
   90  and beside him the attendants hiding the knife,
   91  and the citizens shedding tears at the sight of her,
   92  mute with fear, her knees giving way, she sank to the ground.
   93  Nor could it help the poor girl at such a moment
   94  that she had been the first to give the king the name of father;
   95  for lifted by men's hands and trembling she was led
   96  to the altar, not so that, when the solemn rite was done,
   97  she might be escorted by the ringing wedding-hymn,
   98  but so that, pure, impurely[16], at the very season of marriage,
   99  she might fall, a grieving victim, slaughtered by her father,
  100  that a happy and favorable departure be granted the fleet.
  101  So much evil could religion prompt.[17]
  102  You yourself, at any moment now, overcome by the prophets'
  103  terror-speaking words, will look to desert us.
  104  For indeed how many dreams they can even now invent for you,
  105  dreams that could overturn the reasonings of your life
  106  and trouble all your fortunes with fear!
  107  And with reason: for if men saw a sure end
  108  to their troubles, they would by some means find strength
  109  to stand against the religions and the threats of prophets.
  110  As it is, there is no way to stand against them, no power,
  111  since eternal punishments must be feared in death.
  112  For it is not known what the nature of the spirit[18] is,
  113  whether it is born, or instead works its way into us at birth,
  114  and whether it perishes together with us, torn apart by death,
  115  or visits the darkness of Orcus[19] and his vast pools,
  116  or by divine ordinance slips itself into other beasts,
  117  as our Ennius[20] sang, who first brought down
  118  from lovely Helicon a crown of unfading leaf
  119  to shine in fame through the Italian nations of men;
  120  and yet besides, that the precincts of Acheron exist
  121  Ennius sets forth, declaring it in eternal verses,
  122  where neither our spirits nor our bodies endure,
  123  but certain images[21], pale in strange ways;
  124  from there, he recalls, the likeness of ever-flourishing Homer
  125  rose up before him and began to shed salt tears
  126  and to unfold in words the nature of things.
  127  Therefore, while we must give a good account of things
  128  above, by what principle the courses of sun and moon
  129  come about, and by what force each thing is carried on
  130  upon the earth, then above all with keen reasoning
  131  we must see of what the spirit and the nature of the mind consist,
  132  and what thing it is that meets us and terrifies our minds
  133  when we are awake but stricken with sickness, or buried in sleep,
  134  so that we seem to see and hear face to face
  135  those whose bones, after death, the earth embraces.
  136  Nor does it escape my mind that the obscure findings of the Greeks
  137  are hard to set in light in Latin verses,
  138  especially since much must be done with new words
  139  because of the poverty of the language[22] and the newness of the things;
  140  but still your worth, and the hoped-for pleasure
  141  of sweet friendship, urge me to bear any labor,
  142  and lead me to keep watch through the calm nights,
  143  searching with what words and with what song at last
  144  I may spread clear lights before your mind
  145  by which you can see deep into hidden things.

  146  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness[23]
  147  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
  148  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
  149  Its first principle will take its starting-point for us from here:
  150  that nothing is ever begotten from nothing by divine will[24].
  151  Indeed, fear so holds all mortals in its grip
  152  because they behold many things happening on earth and in the sky
  153  the causes of whose workings they can in no way see,
  154  and they suppose them to be done by divine power.
  156  For these reasons, when we have seen that nothing can be created[25]
  157  from nothing, then we shall more rightly discern from there
  158  what we are seeking: both from what each thing can be created
  155  and in what manner all things come about without the labor of gods.
  159  For if things came to be from nothing, out of all things
  160  every kind could be born; nothing would need a seed.
  161  First, men could rise out of the sea, and out of the earth
  162  the scaly race, and birds could burst out of the sky;
  163  herds and other cattle, every kind of wild beast,
  164  born at random, would occupy the tilled land and the wild.
  165  Nor would the same fruits stay constant on the trees,
  166  but would change; all trees could bear all fruits.
  167  For where there were no generative bodies for each thing,
  168  how could there be a fixed mother for things?
  169  But as it is, since each thing is created from fixed seeds,
  170  it is born and comes forth into the shores of light
  171  from that place where the matter of each and its first bodies are;
  172  and for this reason all things cannot be begotten from all,
  173  because in fixed things there dwells a separate power.
  174  Further, why do we see the rose poured forth in spring, grain in the heat,
  175  vines when autumn urges them on,
  176  if not because, when fixed seeds of things at their own time
  177  have flowed together, whatever is created is disclosed
  178  while the seasons are at hand, and the living earth
  179  brings tender things safely forth into the shores of light?
  180  But if they came from nothing, they would spring up suddenly
  181  at uncertain intervals and in alien parts of the year,
  182  since there would be no first-beginnings that could be kept
  183  from generative union by an unfavorable season.
  184  Nor again, for things to grow, would there be need of time
  185  for the meeting of seed, if they could grow out of nothing;
  186  for little infants would suddenly become young men,
  187  and trees, risen from the earth, would leap up all at once.
  188  It is plain that none of this happens, since all things
  189  grow little by little, as is fitting, from fixed seed,
  190  and as they grow they keep their kind; so that you can know
  191  that each thing grows large and is nourished from its own matter.
  192  To this is added that without the year's fixed rains
  193  the earth could not send up its gladdening produce,
  194  nor again could the nature of living things, kept apart from food,
  195  propagate its kind and preserve its life;
  196  so that you should rather think that many bodies are common
  197  to many things, as we see elements[26] are to words,
  198  than that anything could exist without beginnings.
  199  Again, why could nature not produce men so huge
  200  that they could cross the sea on foot, wading through its shallows,
  201  and tear great mountains apart with their hands,
  202  and in living outlast many generations of the living,
  203  if not because fixed matter has been assigned to things
  204  for their begetting, from which it stands what can arise?
  205  Therefore it must be confessed that nothing can come from nothing,
  206  since things need seed, from which each, once created,
  207  can be brought forth into the tender breezes of the air.
  208  Finally, since we see that tilled places surpass
  209  the untilled, and yield better produce to our hands,
  210  there are, it is clear, first-beginnings of things in the earth
  211  which we, turning the fertile clods with the plowshare
  212  and working the soil of the earth, rouse to birth;
  213  but if there were none, you would see each thing, without our labor,
  214  come of its own accord to be much better.
  215  To this is added that nature dissolves each thing back
  216  into its own bodies, and does not destroy things into nothing.
  217  For if anything were mortal in all its parts,
  218  each thing would suddenly perish, snatched from our eyes;
  219  for there would be no need of any force that could bring about
  220  division among its parts and undo their bonds.
  221  As it is, since each thing is made of eternal seed,
  222  until a force has met it that can shatter things with a blow
  223  or pierce within through the voids[27] and dissolve them,
  224  nature does not allow the destruction of anything to be seen.
  225  Further, whatever things time removes through age,
  226  if it destroys them utterly, consuming all their matter,
  227  from where does Venus lead the race of living things, kind by kind,
  228  back into the light of life? Or, once led back, from where does the artificer earth
  229  nourish and increase them, kind by kind, providing food?
  230  From where do native springs and rivers from far outside
  231  keep the sea supplied? From where does the ether feed the stars?
  232  For all things that are of mortal body
  233  infinite time and the days gone by must have consumed.
  234  But if in that space, in that time gone by, there have been
  235  those things from which this sum of things is made up, renewed,
  236  they are surely endowed with an immortal nature.
  237  Therefore things cannot, any of them, return to nothing.
  238  Again, the same force and cause would destroy all things
  239  indiscriminately, unless eternal matter held them,
  240  more or less entangled in its bonds with itself;
  241  for a touch would surely be cause enough of death,
  242  since there would be nothing of eternal body whose
  243  fabric any force would have to unweave.
  244  But as it is, since the bonds of the beginnings with one another
  245  are unlike, and matter is eternal,
  246  things remain with body unharmed until a force
  247  meets them found keen enough for the texture of each.
  248  Therefore no thing returns to nothing, but all things
  249  by division return to the bodies of matter.
  250  Finally, the rains perish when father ether[28]
  251  has flung them headlong into the lap of mother earth;
  252  but shining crops spring up, and branches grow green
  253  on the trees; the trees themselves grow and are weighed down with fruit.
  254  From this, in turn, our race and the race of wild beasts are fed,
  255  from this we see glad cities flower with children
  256  and leafy woods on every side sing with new birds,
  257  from this the cattle, weary and fat, through the glad pastures lay down
  258  their bodies, and the white milky liquid
  259  flows from the swollen udders; from this the new offspring
  260  on unsteady limbs frisk through the tender grass
  261  and play, their young minds made drunk with unmixed milk[29].
  262  Therefore none of the things that are seen perish utterly,
  263  since nature remakes one thing from another, and does not allow
  264  anything to be born unless helped by another's death.
  265  Come now: since I have taught that things cannot be created
  266  from nothing, nor, once born, be called back to nothing,
  267  yet lest perhaps you begin to distrust my words
  268  because the first-beginnings of things cannot be seen with the eyes,
  269  hear further of bodies which you yourself must
  270  confess are among things and yet cannot be seen.
  271  First, the roused force of the wind lashes the body of the sea[30]
  272  and overturns huge ships and scatters the clouds,
  273  at times, running across the plains in a swift whirlwind,
  274  it strews them with great trees, and the mountaintops
  275  it harries with forest-breaking[31] blasts: so with a fierce
  276  roar the sea rages, and storms with a threatening growl.
  277  Therefore winds, beyond doubt, are unseen bodies
  278  that sweep the sea, the lands, and finally the clouds of heaven,
  279  and harry them and snatch them up in a sudden whirlwind;
  280  nor do they flow or spread their havoc in any other way
  281  than when the soft nature of water is suddenly carried
  282  in an overflowing stream, swollen with abundant rains
  283  by a great downrush of water from high mountains,
  284  hurling together broken pieces of forests and whole trees,
  285  nor can strong bridges bear the sudden force
  286  of the oncoming water: so, turbid with great rain,
  287  the river runs against the piers with mighty strength,
  288  makes havoc with a great roar, and rolls beneath its waves
  289  great stones, and dashes down whatever stands against its flood.
  290  So therefore the blasts of wind too must be carried,
  291  which, when like a strong river they have leaned
  292  toward any quarter, drive things before them and dash them down
  293  with repeated onsets, at times in a twisting eddy
  294  seize them and carry them off in a swift, spinning whirlwind.
  295  Therefore, again and again, winds are unseen bodies,
  296  since in their deeds and ways they are found to rival
  297  great rivers, which are of visible body.
  298  Then again, we perceive the various odors of things
  299  and yet never see them coming to our nostrils,
  300  nor do we look upon hot heats, nor can we take in cold
  301  with our eyes, nor are we used to seeing voices;
  302  yet all these must consist of bodily
  303  nature, since they can strike upon the senses;
  304  for to touch and be touched no thing can, unless it is body.
  305  Again, clothes hung up on the wave-breaking shore
  306  grow damp, and the same, spread out in the sun, grow dry.
  307  But neither how the moisture of water settled in them
  308  has been seen, nor again how it fled before the heat.
  309  Therefore the moisture is dispersed into small parts
  310  which the eyes can in no way see.
  311  Indeed, as many years of the sun come round again,
  312  a ring on the finger grows thin beneath from wearing,
  313  the falling of the drip hollows a stone, the curved iron
  314  plowshare secretly wastes away in the fields,
  315  and the stone pavements of the roads we see already
  316  worn by the feet of the crowd; then, beside the gates, bronze[32]
  317  statues show their right hands thinned
  318  by the touch of those who greet them, often, and of those passing by.
  319  These things, then, we see are diminished, since they are worn away.
  320  But what bodies depart from them at any given time
  321  envious nature has shut off from the power of sight.

  322  Lastly, whatever the passing days and nature add to things
  323  little by little, compelling them to grow in due measure,
  324  no sharpness of the eyes, however it strains, can observe;
  325  nor again, in whatever grows old with age and wasting,
  326  nor in rocks that overhang the sea, eaten through by the gnawing salt,
  327  could you discern what they lose at each moment.
  328  Therefore nature carries on her work through unseen bodies.
  329  And yet not all things are held packed tight on every side
  330  by the nature of body; for there is void[33] in things.
  331  To have learned this will be of use to you in many matters,
  332  and will not let you wander, doubting and always asking
  333  about the sum of things, and distrusting my words.
  334  Therefore there is place, untouchable, void and empty.
  335  For if there were not, by no means could things
  336  move at all; for what stands out as the office[34] of body,
  337  to obstruct and stand in the way, would at every moment be present
  338  in all things; and so nothing could go forward,
  339  since nothing would make a beginning of giving way.
  340  But as it is, through seas and lands and the heights of heaven,
  341  many things, in many ways, by varied principle, moving
  342  we see before our eyes, which, if there were no void,
  343  would not so much be robbed of their restless motion and lack it
  344  as never have been brought to birth at all by any means,
  345  since matter, packed tight on every side, would have lain at rest.
  346  Further, however solid things may be thought to be,
  347  you may yet see from this that they are of loose and porous body.
  348  In rocks and caves the liquid moisture of the waters
  349  seeps through, and all things weep with plentiful drops.
  350  Food spreads itself through the whole body of living things;
  351  trees grow and pour out their fruit in season,
  352  because from the deepest roots, into the whole tree,
  353  through trunks and through branches, all their food is spread.
  354  Voices pass through walls, and through the closed rooms of houses
  355  fly across; stiffening cold seeps through to the bones.
  356  And unless there were void spaces through which each of the bodies
  357  could pass, you would see this happen by no means at all.
  358  Finally, why do we see some things outweigh other things
  359  in weight, though they are no larger in shape?
  360  For if there is as much in a ball of wool as there is
  361  of body in lead, it is fitting that they weigh just as much,
  362  since it is the office of body to press all things downward,
  363  while the nature of void, on the contrary, stays without weight.
  364  Therefore what is just as large and yet is seen to be lighter
  365  plainly declares that there is more void in it;
  366  but the heavier, on the contrary, proclaims there is more body
  367  in it, and that it holds much less of the empty within.
  368  There is therefore, plainly, that which with keen reasoning
  369  we are seeking, mixed in with things: what we call void.
  370  In these matters, so that it may not lead you from the truth,
  371  I am compelled to forestall what certain men[35] invent.
  372  They say the waters give way to the scaly creatures as they thrust
  373  and open liquid paths, because the fish leave places behind them
  374  into which the yielding waves can flow together;
  375  so too, they say, other things can move among themselves
  376  and change their place, though all things are full.
  377  All this, of course, has been accepted on false reasoning.
  378  For where, in the end, will the scaly creatures be able to go forward
  379  unless the waters give them room? And again, where will the waves
  380  be able to withdraw, when the fish cannot go on?
  381  Therefore either all bodies must be deprived of motion,
  382  or we must say that void is mixed in with things,
  383  from which each thing may take its first start of moving.
  384  Lastly, if two broad bodies, after colliding,
  385  spring swiftly apart, then surely the air must take possession
  386  of all the void that comes to be between the bodies.
  387  That air, again, though it flows in with currents speeding round,
  388  still will not be able at one moment to fill the whole
  389  space; for it must occupy first one and then
  390  the next place, before the whole can be possessed.
  391  But if someone perhaps, when the bodies have sprung apart,
  392  thinks this happens because the air condenses itself,
  393  he is wrong; for then an emptiness comes to be that was not there before,
  394  and likewise an emptiness is filled that stood there before,
  395  nor can air be condensed in such a way,
  396  nor, if it could, could it, I think, without void
  397  draw itself into itself and gather its parts together.
  398  Therefore, however long you may delay with objections,
  399  you must still confess that there is void in things.
  400  And many more, besides, I could set before you, recounting
  401  arguments, to scrape together belief for my words.
  402  But for a keen mind these small tracks
  403  are enough, through which you yourself can come to know the rest.
  404  For as hounds very often, of a mountain-ranging beast,
  405  find out with their noses the resting places hidden in leaves,
  406  once they have set themselves on the sure tracks of its path,
  407  so you yourself, on your own, will be able to see one thing from another
  408  in such matters, and to work your way into all
  409  the unseen lurking places and drag the truth out of them.
  410  But if you are sluggish, or shrink back a little from the matter,
  411  this I can promise you plainly, Memmius:
  412  such generous draughts, drawn from great springs,
  413  will my sweet tongue pour out from my rich heart
  414  that I fear slow old age will first creep through
  415  our limbs and loosen in us the barriers of life
  416  before, in verses, on any single matter, the whole store
  417  of arguments has been sent through your ears.
  418  But now, to resume and weave to its end in words the work begun:
  419  all nature, then, as it is in itself, has been founded
  420  on two things; for there are bodies and there is void,
  421  in which these are set and through which they move this way and that.
  422  For that body exists by itself is declared by the sense
  423  common to all[36]; and unless trust in this stands first and firm,
  424  there will be nothing to appeal to concerning hidden things
  425  by which we could confirm anything by reasoning of the mind.
  426  Then again, place and space, which we call void,
  427  if it did not exist, bodies could not be set anywhere
  428  at all, nor move in any direction this way and that;
  429  as I showed you above, a little while before.
  430  Further, there is nothing that you could say is, from all
  431  body disjoined, and set apart from the void,
  432  to be found as a third nature, as it were, in the count.
  433  For whatever will exist must itself be something,
  434  of bulk large or small, so long as it exists;
  435  and if it has touch, however light and slight,
  436  it will add to the count of body and join its sum;
  437  but if it is untouchable, unable on any side
  438  to stop anything passing through it on its way,
  439  then this, of course, will be the empty, which we call void.
  440  Again, whatever will exist by itself will either act
  441  or must itself be acted upon by other things acting,
  442  or be such that things can exist and be carried on in it.
  443  But nothing can act or be acted upon without body,
  444  nor again provide place except the void and empty.
  445  Therefore besides void and bodies no third nature
  446  by itself can be left in the number of things,
  447  neither one that falls at any time under our senses
  448  nor one that anyone can grasp by reasoning of the mind.
  449  For whatever things have a name, you will find them either conjoined[37]
  450  to these two things, or you will see that they are their accidents.
  451  A conjoined property is that which can nowhere, without a deadly
  452  division, be disjoined and parted from the thing,
  453  as weight is to stones, heat to fire, wetness to water,
  454  touch to all bodies, untouchability to void.
  455  Slavery, on the other hand, poverty and riches,
  456  freedom, war, concord, and the other things at whose
  457  coming and going nature remains unharmed,
  458  these we are used, rightly, to calling accidents.
  459  Time[38] likewise does not exist by itself, but from things themselves
  460  our sense takes in what has been carried through in time gone by,
  461  then what thing is at hand, and what will follow after;
  462  nor must it be admitted that anyone senses time by itself,
  463  apart from the motion of things and their calm rest.
  464  Finally, when they say the abduction of Tyndareus' daughter[39]
  465  and the conquest of the Trojan-born nations in war "are", we must take care
  466  that they do not force us to admit these things exist by themselves,
  467  since the generations of men whose accidents these were
  468  have been carried off already by an age gone past recall;
  469  for one thing will be said to be an accident of the lands[40], another
  470  of the regions themselves, whatever has been done.
  471  Finally, if there had been no matter of things,
  472  and no place and space, in which each thing is carried on,
  473  never would the fire, kindled by love for the beauty of Tyndareus' daughter,
  474  glowing beneath the Phrygian breast of Alexander,
  475  have lit the famous contests of savage war,
  476  nor would the wooden horse, unknown to the Trojans, have set Pergamum
  477  ablaze by its night-time delivery of the sons of the Greeks;
  478  so that you may see clearly that all deeds done, from the ground up,
  479  do not exist or have being by themselves as body does,
  480  nor are spoken of on the same principle as void,
  481  but rather that you may justly call them accidents
  482  of body and of place, in which each thing is carried on.

  483  Further, bodies are partly the first-beginnings of things,
  484  partly those that are formed from a union of beginnings[41].
  485  But those that are the first-beginnings of things no force
  486  can quench; for it is by solid body that they prevail.
  487  And yet it seems hard to believe that anything
  488  among things can be found to be of solid body.
  489  For the thunderbolt of heaven passes through the walls of houses,
  490  as do shouting and voices; iron grows white-hot in fire,
  491  and rocks leap apart in fierce and seething heat;
  492  when the hardness of gold is shaken loose and dissolves in heat,
  493  then the ice of bronze[42], conquered by flame, melts away;
  494  heat seeps through silver, and so does penetrating cold,
  495  since, gripping cups in hand as is the custom, we have felt both
  496  when the dew of water was poured in from above.
  497  To such a degree there seems to be nothing solid in things.
  498  But since true reasoning and the nature of things nonetheless
  499  compel us, attend, while in a few verses we make it plain
  500  that there are things which consist of solid and eternal body,
  501  which we teach are the seeds of things and their first-beginnings,
  502  from which the whole sum of things now stands created.
  503  To begin with, since the twofold nature of two things,
  504  far unlike each other, has been found to exist,
  505  of body and of place, in which each thing is carried on,
  506  each of necessity must exist by itself and pure.
  507  For wherever there is empty space, which we call void,
  508  there body is not; and further, wherever body
  509  holds its place, there empty void by no means exists.
  510  The first bodies, therefore, are solid and without void.
  511  Further, since there is void in things that have been born,
  512  solid matter must stand around it;
  513  nor can anything be proved by true reasoning
  514  to hide void in its body and to hold it within,
  515  unless you grant that what encloses it stands solid.
  516  And that, further, can be nothing but a union
  517  of matter, such as could enclose the void in things.
  518  Matter, therefore, which consists of solid body,
  519  can be eternal, while all else is dissolved.
  520  Then again, if there were nothing that stood empty[43] as void,
  521  all would be solid; unless, on the other hand, there were definite bodies
  522  to fill up whatever places they occupied,
  523  all the space there is would stand as empty void.
  524  Body, therefore, is plainly marked off from void
  525  in alternation, since neither does the wholly full exist
  526  nor, again, the wholly empty; there are, then, definite bodies
  527  that can mark off empty space from full.
  528  These can neither be dissolved when struck by blows from outside
  529  nor, again, be unravelled by being pierced to the core,
  530  nor, assailed in any other way, can they be made to give;
  531  as I showed you above, a little while before.[44]
  532  For nothing, it seems, can be crushed without void,
  533  nor broken, nor split in two by cutting,
  534  nor take in moisture, nor likewise seeping cold,
  535  nor penetrating fire, by which all things are undone.
  536  And the more void each thing holds within it,
  537  the more it gives way when assailed to the core by these.
  538  Therefore if the first bodies are solid and without void,
  539  as I have taught, they must be eternal.
  540  Further, unless matter had been eternal,
  541  before now all things would have gone back utterly to nothing,
  542  and from nothing would have been reborn whatever we see.
  543  But since I taught above that nothing can be created
  544  from nothing, nor what has been born be called back to nothing,
  545  the first-beginnings must be of immortal body,
  546  into which all things can be dissolved at their last hour,
  547  so that matter may be at hand for the renewal of things.
  548  The first-beginnings, therefore, are of solid singleness[45],
  549  nor in any other way could they have been kept through the ages
  550  to renew things, from time already infinite.
  551  Finally, if nature had set no limit
  552  to the breaking of things, by now the bodies of matter
  553  would have been so worn down by the breaking of past ages
  554  that nothing conceived from them, from a fixed time on,
  555  could reach the furthest goal of its age.
  556  For we see that anything can be dissolved more quickly
  557  than made again; therefore whatever the long span of days,
  558  the infinite age of all the time gone before,
  559  had broken till now, confounding and dissolving it,
  560  could never be repaired in the time remaining.
  561  But as it is, a fixed limit to breaking, plainly,
  562  remains assigned, since we see each thing made again,
  563  and at the same time fixed seasons, kind by kind, stand set
  564  for things, in which they can reach the flower of their age.
  565  Add to this that, although the bodies of matter
  566  are most solid, an account can still be given of all things
  567  that come to be soft, air, water, earth, vapors,
  568  how they come to be and by what force each is carried on,
  569  since void is mixed, once and for all, in things.
  570  But if, on the contrary, the first-beginnings of things were soft,
  571  how hard flints and iron could be created
  572  no account could be given; for utterly all
  573  nature will lack a beginning for its foundation.
  574  They are, therefore, strong in solid singleness,
  575  and by a denser union of them all things
  576  can be packed tight and show forth sturdy strength.
  577  Further, if no limit has been set to the breaking
  578  of bodies, still, out of eternal time, certain bodies
  579  must even now survive in things,
  580  such as have not yet been assailed by any danger.
  581  But since they are endowed with a fragile nature,
  582  it is inconsistent that they could have lasted through eternal time,
  583  harried through the ages by numberless blows.
  584  Finally, since a limit is set for things, kind by kind,
  585  to their growing and their holding on to life,
  586  and since what each can do by nature's covenants[46],
  587  and what, further, each cannot, stands decreed,
  588  nor is anything changed, but all things are so constant
  589  that the varied birds, all of them in their order,
  590  show the markings of their kind upon their bodies,
  591  they must surely also have a body of unchangeable
  592  matter; for if the first-beginnings of things
  593  could be overcome in some way and be changed,
  594  it would then be uncertain, too, what can come to be
  595  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded,[47]
  596  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep,
  597  nor could the generations, kind by kind, so often repeat
  598  the nature, the manners, the diet, and the movements of their parents.

  599  Then further, since there is in each case an outermost point[48]
  600  of that body which our senses can no longer
  601  make out, that point, beyond doubt, stands without parts
  602  and consists of a least nature, and was never
  603  separate by itself, nor will it have the power to be hereafter,
  604  since it is itself a part of another, the first and single part;
  605  then other parts and others, alike, in their order,
  606  in close-packed column fill out the nature of the body;
  607  and since these cannot stand by themselves, they must
  608  cling where they can by no means be torn away.
  609  The first-beginnings, therefore, are of solid singleness,
  610  and they cohere tightly, packed with least parts,
  611  not combined out of a gathering of those parts,
  612  but rather strong in eternal singleness;
  613  from them nothing can be torn away, nothing now diminished,
  614  so nature allows, keeping them in reserve as seeds for things.
  615  Further, unless there is a least, the very smallest
  616  bodies will consist of infinite parts,
  617  since indeed the half of a half will always have
  618  its own half, and nothing will set a limit.
  619  Therefore between the sum of things and the least of things
  620  there will be nothing to tell them apart; for however utterly
  621  infinite the whole sum may be, still the smallest things there are
  622  will consist of infinite parts just the same.
  623  And since true reasoning cries out against this and denies
  624  that the mind can believe it, you must yield and admit
  625  that there are things which stand endowed with no parts now
  626  and consist of a least nature. And since these exist,
  627  you must admit that those, too, are solid and eternal.
  628  Finally, if to break all things up into least parts
  629  had been the custom of the nature of things, the creator[49],
  630  that same nature could restore nothing from them now,
  631  because what is furnished with no parts
  632  cannot have the things that generative matter
  633  must have: various connections, weights, blows,
  634  collisions, motions, by which each thing is carried on.
  635  Therefore those who have thought that the matter of things is
  636  fire, and that the sum of things consists of fire alone,
  637  seem to have slipped greatly from true reasoning.
  638  Their leader, Heraclitus[50], is the first to enter the battle,
  639  bright in fame for his dark speech, more among the empty-headed
  640  than among the serious Greeks who seek the truth;
  641  for fools admire and love above all those things
  642  which they see lurking under twisted words,
  643  and they set up as true whatever can prettily touch
  644  the ears and is painted over with a charming sound.
  645  For why should things be so various, I ask,
  646  if they are created out of fire, one and pure?
  647  For it would avail nothing for hot fire to be made dense
  648  or made rare, if the parts of fire had the same
  649  nature which fire as a whole has as well.
  650  For the heat would be fiercer with its parts drawn together,
  651  and fainter, again, with them scattered and thrown apart.
  652  More than this there is nothing you might suppose could happen
  653  under such conditions, far less that so great a variety
  654  of things could arise from fires made dense and rare.
  655  This too: if they were to grant void mixed in with things,
  656  fires could be made dense and be left rare;
  657  but because they see many things that would run against them
  658  and shrink from leaving pure void in things,
  659  while they fear the steep way, they lose the true road,
  660  nor again do they see that, with void taken from things,
  661  all things are made dense, and out of all things one
  662  body is made, which could send nothing swiftly from itself,
  663  as heat-bringing fire throws out light and warmth,
  664  so that you may see it is not made of close-packed parts.
  665  But if perhaps they believe that by some other principle
  666  fires can be quenched in combining and change their body,
  667  if, that is, they will not in any part hold back from doing this,
  668  then beyond doubt the heat will perish utterly into nothing,
  669  all of it, and from nothing will come whatever is created;
  670  for whatever is changed and passes out of its own bounds,[51]
  671  this at once is the death of what it was before.
  672  Therefore something must survive in them unharmed,
  673  so that all things do not return utterly to nothing for you,
  674  and the store of things, reborn from nothing, grow strong.
  675  Now then, since there are certain bodies, utterly sure,
  676  which keep their nature always the same,
  677  by whose going away or coming in and change of order
  678  things change their nature and bodies are transformed,
  679  you may know that these bodies of things are not of fire.
  680  For it would make no difference for some to withdraw and go away
  681  and others to be added, and some to change their order,
  682  if all of them still kept the nature of heat;
  683  for whatever they created would in every way be fire.
  684  But in truth, as I think, it is so: there are certain bodies whose
  685  collisions[52], motions, order, position, shapes
  686  make fires, and, their order changed, change
  687  their nature, and they are not like fire, nor like any
  688  other thing which could send bodies
  689  to the senses and by contact touch our touch.
  690  Further, to say that all things are fire, and that no
  691  true thing exists in the number of things except fire,
  692  as this same man does, seems utter madness.
  693  For he himself, starting from the senses, fights against the senses
  694  and shakes them, on which all belief depends,
  695  by which this fire he names became known to him;
  696  for he believes that the senses truly know fire,
  697  but does not believe the rest, which are no less clear.
  698  And this seems to me as empty as it is mad;
  699  for to what shall we appeal? What can be surer for us
  700  than the senses[53] themselves, by which to mark true and false?
  701  Further, why should anyone rather do away with all things
  702  and wish to leave the nature of heat alone,
  703  than deny that fires exist and still leave some other nature?
  704  For it seems equal madness to say either.
  705  Therefore those who have thought that the matter of things is[54]
  706  fire, and that the sum of things can consist of fire,
  707  and those who have set up air[55] as the beginning
  708  for begetting things, or whoever have thought that water
  709  by itself shapes things, or that earth creates
  710  all things and is turned into the natures of all things,
  711  seem to have strayed greatly and far from the truth.
  712  Add also those who double the first-beginnings of things,
  713  joining air to fire and earth to liquid,
  714  and those who think that out of four things all things can
  715  grow forth, out of fire, earth, and breath[56], and rain.
  716  Of these, first among them is Empedocles[57] of Acragas,
  717  whom the island bore within the three-cornered shores of its lands,
  718  around which, flowing with great windings, the Ionian
  719  sea sprinkles salt spray from its gray-green waves,
  720  and with its narrow strait the rushing sea divides with its waves
  721  the shores of Aeolia's[58] lands from the island's borders.
  722  Here is vast Charybdis, and here the rumblings of Etna
  723  threaten that the angers of its flames are gathering again,
  724  so that its force may once more vomit fires burst from its jaws
  725  and carry again to the sky the lightning-flashes of its flame.
  726  And while this great region seems in many ways a wonder
  727  to the races of men, and is said to be worth the seeing,
  728  rich in good things, fortified with a great force of men,
  729  yet it seems to have had in it nothing more glorious than this man,
  730  nothing more holy, more marvelous, or more dear.
  731  Indeed the songs of his divine breast
  732  cry aloud and set forth his glorious discoveries,
  733  so that he scarcely seems born of human stock.
  734  Yet he, and those we spoke of above, who are inferior
  735  remarkably in many ways and much the lesser,
  736  though they discovered many things well and as if divinely,
  737  and gave answers as from the inner shrine of the heart
  738  more holy and with far surer reasoning than
  739  the Pythia[59], who speaks from the tripod and the laurel of Phoebus,
  740  yet over the beginnings of things they came to ruin,
  741  and there, great as they were, they fell heavily with a great fall.
  742  First, because they set up motions with void taken from things,
  743  and yet leave things soft and rare,
  744  air, sun, fire, earths, animals, crops,
  745  and still do not mix void into their body;
  746  next, because they will make no limit at all to the cutting
  747  of bodies, and set no halt to their breaking,
  748  and nothing at all to exist in things as a least,
  749  when we see that the outermost point of each thing
  750  is that which to our senses seems to be least,
  751  so that you may infer from this that in things you cannot see
  752  the outermost point they have stands as a least.
  753  Add to this as well that, since they make the first-beginnings of things
  754  soft, things which we see to be born
  755  and to be of mortal body, it follows that utterly
  756  the sum of things must by now return to nothing,
  757  and the store of things, reborn from nothing, grow strong;
  758  and how far each of these stands from the truth you will see now.
  759  Next, they are in many ways hostile and a poison
  760  to one another; therefore either, meeting, they will perish,
  761  or they will so scatter as, when a storm is gathered,
  762  we see thunderbolts scatter, and rains and winds.

  763  Finally, if all things are created out of four things,
  764  and all things are dissolved back again into those things,
  765  why can those any more be called the first-beginnings of things
  766  than things, the other way round, be thought to be theirs?
  767  For they are born by turns, and change their color
  768  and their whole nature among themselves from all time.
  769  we see the thunderbolts scatter, and the rains and winds.[60]
  770  But if perhaps you think the body of fire and of earth
  771  come together, and the airy breezes and the moisture of liquid,
  772  so that in their union nothing changes their nature,
  773  no thing can be created for you out of them,
  774  not a living thing, not one with a lifeless body, like a tree;
  775  for each thing, in the meeting of the varied heap,
  776  will show its own nature, and air will be seen, mixed,
  777  to remain together with earth and with a certain moisture.
  778  But the first-beginnings, in the begetting of things, must
  779  bring to bear a nature secret and unseen,
  780  so that nothing stands out to fight against and obstruct
  781  whatever is created from being properly what it is.
  782  Further, they start again from heaven and its fires,
  783  and make fire first turn itself into the breezes
  784  of air, from this the rain be born, and earth be created
  785  out of rain, and from earth all things return in reverse,
  786  moisture first, after it air, then heat,
  787  and these never cease to change among themselves, to travel
  788  from heaven to earth, from earth to the stars of the world.
  789  This the first-beginnings must by no means do.
  790  For something unchangeable must survive,
  791  so that things are not all brought back utterly to nothing;
  792  for whatever is changed and passes out of its own bounds,
  793  this at once is the death of what it was before.
  794  Therefore, since the things we spoke of a little before
  795  come into change, they must consist
  796  of others, which can nowhere be converted,
  797  so that all things do not return utterly to nothing for you;
  798  why not rather posit certain bodies endowed with such
  799  a nature that, if perhaps they have created fire,
  800  the same, with a few taken away and a few added,
  801  their order and motion changed, can make the breezes of air,
  802  and so all things be changed, some into others?
  803  'But the plain fact shows openly,' you say, 'that into the breezes
  804  of air all things grow up out of the earth and are fed;
  805  and unless the season, at a favorable time, is generous
  806  with rains, so that the trees sway under the melting of the clouds,
  807  and the sun for its part warms them and gives its heat,
  808  crops, trees, and living things could not grow.'
  809  Of course; and unless dry food and soft moisture
  810  helped us, our body would waste, and then all life too
  811  would be loosed from all our sinews and bones;
  812  for beyond doubt we are helped and nourished
  813  by certain things, and other things in turn by certain others.
  814  No wonder: because many first-beginnings, common in many ways
  815  to many things, are mixed together in things,
  816  for that reason varied things are nourished by varied things.
  817  And it often matters greatly, for the same first-beginnings,
  818  with what and in what position they are held together,
  819  and what motions they give and receive among themselves;
  820  for the same make up heaven, sea, lands, rivers, sun,
  821  the same make crops, trees, living things,
  822  but they move mixed with different things and in different ways.
  823  Indeed, everywhere in these very verses of ours
  824  you see many elements common to many words,
  825  though you must still admit that the verses and the words
  826  differ from one another both in sense and in the sound they make.
  827  So much can elements do by a change of order alone;
  828  but those that are the first-beginnings of things can bring
  829  more to bear, from which varied things can each be created.
  830  Now let us also examine the homoeomeria[61] of Anaxagoras,
  831  as the Greeks call it, which to name in our own tongue
  832  the poverty of our native speech does not allow us;
  833  but still the thing itself is easy to set out in words.
  834  To begin with, when he speaks of the homoeomeria of things,
  835  he means, you see, that bones from tiny and minute
  836  bones, and that from tiny and minute
  837  bits of flesh, flesh is born, and blood is created
  838  from many drops of blood coming together among themselves,
  839  and he thinks gold can be formed out of specks
  840  of gold, and earth grow together out of little earths,
  841  fires out of fires, moisture be made of moistures,
  842  and the rest he imagines and supposes on a like principle.
  843  Nor yet that there is void in things in any part
  844  does he grant, nor that there is an end to the cutting of bodies.
  845  Therefore in both parts of his reasoning he seems to me
  846  to go astray just as those did of whom we spoke above.
  847  Add that he imagines first-beginnings far too weak;
  848  if first-beginnings are what stand endowed with a nature
  849  like that of things themselves, and labor just as they do
  850  and perish, and nothing reins them back from destruction.
  851  For which of them will hold out under heavy pressure
  852  so as to escape death, beneath the very teeth of death?
  853  Fire, or moisture, or air? Which of these? Blood or bones?
  854  None, I think, when every thing will be, utterly and equally,
  855  as mortal as those things we see plainly
  856  before our eyes perish, overcome by some force.
  857  But that things cannot fall back into nothing, nor again
  858  grow out of nothing, I call to witness the things proved before.
  859  Further, since food increases the body and nourishes it,
  860  we may know that veins and blood and bones
 860a  * * *[62]
  861  or if they say that all foods are of mixed body
  862  and have in them small bodies of sinews,
  863  and bones, and veins altogether, and parts of gore,
  864  it will follow that all food, both the dry and the liquid itself,
  865  must be thought to consist of things of foreign kind,
  866  of bones and sinews and serum and blood mixed together.
  867  Further, whatever bodies grow up out of the earth,
  868  if they are in the earth, the earth must consist
  869  of things of foreign kind, which rise up out of the earth.
  870  Transfer the argument again; you may use the very same words:
  871  if flame lies hidden in wood, and smoke and ash,
  872  wood must consist of things of foreign kind;
  873  further, that whatever bodies the earth feeds and increases
  874  consist of things of foreign kind, which rise out of the wood.[63]

  875  Here there is left a certain slender chance of hiding,
  876  which Anaxagoras takes up for himself, holding that every
  877  thing lies hidden, mixed in among all things, but that one thing
  878  appears, the one of which the most parts are mixed in
  879  and lie more ready to hand and set in the forefront.
  880  But this is driven far away from true reasoning;
  881  for it would then follow that grain too, when by the menacing
  882  strength of the stone it is crushed, would often send out some sign
  883  of blood, or of some other thing that is nourished in our body.
  884  When we grind them stone upon stone, it would be fitting for gore
  885  by a like principle to ooze from grasses too, and often,
  886  and for them to send out juices, sweet drops of a savor like
  887  the savor of milk in the udder of the wool-bearing ewe;
  888  and, of course, that when clods of earth are often crumbled,
  889  kinds of grasses and grains and leaves should be seen
  890  parceled out through the earth, lying hidden in tiny bits;
  891  and finally, that ash and smoke should be seen in wood
  892  when it was snapped across, and tiny fires lying hidden.
  893  Since the plain fact shows that none of these things happens,
  894  you may know that things are not so mixed in things,
  895  but rather that seeds, mixed in many ways, must lie hidden
  896  in things, seeds common to many things.
  897  "But often on great mountains," you say, "it happens that the tall
  898  trees' neighboring tops are rubbed at their summits
  899  against each other, as the strong south winds force them to it,
  900  until a flower of flame springs up and they blaze."
  901  Indeed so; and yet fire is not implanted in the wood,
  902  but there are many seeds of burning, which, through the rubbing,
  903  when they have flowed together, create fires in the forests.
  904  For if flame, already made, were hidden in the forests,
  905  the fires could not be concealed for any length of time;
  906  they would consume the forests everywhere, burn up the groves.
  907  Now do you see, then, what we said a little before,
  908  that it often matters very much, for these same first-beginnings,
  909  with what and in what position they are held together,
  910  and what motions they give and receive among themselves,
  911  and that these same, a little changed among themselves, create
  912  fires and wood? Just as the words themselves, too,
  913  differ by elements a little changed among themselves,
  914  when we mark wood and fires[64] with distinct sounds.
  915  Finally, if you think that whatever you see in things plain to view
  916  cannot come to be unless you imagine the bodies of matter
  917  endowed with a nature like theirs,
  918  on this reasoning the first-beginnings of things perish for you:
  919  it will come about that they guffaw, shaken with trembling laughter[65],
  920  and wet their faces and cheeks with salt tears.
  921  Come now, for the rest: learn, and hear more clearly.
  922  Nor does it escape my mind how dark these things are; but with a sharp
  923  thyrsus[66] the great hope of praise has struck my heart
  924  and at the same time has driven into my breast sweet love
  925  of the Muses, inspired by which now, with vigorous mind,
  926  I range through the pathless places of the Pierides[67], trodden before
  927  by no one's foot. It is a joy to come to untouched springs
  928  and drink from them, a joy to pluck new flowers
  929  and to seek for my head a crown of distinction from there
  930  where the Muses have veiled no one's temples before;
  931  first because I teach of great things, and go on
  932  to free the mind from the tight-tied knots of religion,
  933  then because on a dark matter I compose such lucid
  934  verses, touching all of it with the Muses' charm.
  935  For this too seems not to be without good reason;
  936  but just as physicians, with foul wormwood for children,
  937  when they try to give it, first touch the rims round the cups
  938  with the sweet and golden liquid of honey,
  939  so that the unforeseeing age of children may be fooled
  940  as far as the lips, and meanwhile drink down the bitter
  941  juice of wormwood, and, deceived, may not be undone,
  942  but rather, restored by such means, may grow strong,
  943  so I now, since this reasoning often seems
  944  rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd
  945  shrinks back from it, have wished, in sweet-speaking
  946  Pierian song, to set out for you our reasoning
  947  and, as it were, to touch it with the Muses' sweet honey,
  948  if by such means I might perhaps hold your mind
  949  on our verses, while you see deep into the whole
  950  nature of things, the shape in which it stands composed.
  951  But since I have taught that the most solid bodies of matter
  952  fly about, unconquered, forever through the ages,
  953  come now, whether there is some limit to their sum
  954  or not, let us unfold; likewise, what has been found to be void,
  955  or place and space, in which all things are carried on,
  956  let us see clearly whether the whole of it is bounded utterly
  957  or lies open, immeasurable and vast and deep.
  958  All that exists, then, is bounded along none of its ways;
  959  for then it would have to have an outermost point.
  960  Further, it seems that nothing can have an outermost point
  961  unless there is something beyond to bound it, so that there seems to be
  962  a point past which the nature of our sense cannot follow.
  963  As it is, since we must admit there is nothing outside the sum,
  964  it has no outermost point; it lacks, therefore, end and measure.
  965  Nor does it matter in which of its regions you stand;
  966  so true it is that, whatever place each one takes, on all
  967  sides alike one leaves the whole as infinite as before.
  968  Further, if now we should set down as bounded
  969  all the space that exists, if someone should run out to its shores,
  970  last of all, at the very edge, and hurl a flying spear[68],
  971  would you rather that, whirled by strong force,
  972  it goes where it was sent and flies far off,
  973  or do you judge that something can stop it and stand in its way?
  974  For you must admit and adopt one or the other.
  975  Either one shuts off your escape, and forces you to grant
  976  that the whole, exempt from any end, lies open.
  977  For whether there is something that blocks it and brings it about
  978  that it does not reach where it was sent and lodge itself at its end,
  979  or whether it is carried on outside, it has not set out from the end.
  980  In this way I will follow, and, wherever you place
  981  the outermost shores, I will ask: what in the end becomes of the spear?
  982  It will come about that no end can stand anywhere,
  983  and the scope for flight will always draw out the escape.
  984  Further, if all the space of the whole sum
  985  were shut in on every side and stood within fixed shores
  986  and were bounded, by now the store of matter
  987  would, by its solid weights, have flowed from all sides to the bottom,
  988  nor could anything be carried on beneath the canopy of the sky,
  989  nor would there be a sky at all, nor the light of the sun,
  990  since all matter would lie heaped up,
  991  settling down from infinite time already past.
  992  But as it is, plainly, no rest is given to the bodies
  993  of the beginnings, because there is no bottom at all
  994  where they might, as it were, flow together and take up their seats.
  995  Always, in ceaseless motion, all things are carried on
  996  in every part, and from beneath are furnished,
  997  out of the infinite, the swift bodies of matter.
  998  Finally, before our eyes one thing is seen to bound another;
  999  air walls off the hills, and mountains the air,
 1000  land bounds the sea, and the sea in turn bounds all lands;
 1001  but the whole, indeed, there is nothing outside to bound.

 1002  There is, therefore, a nature of place and a space of the deep
 1003  such that bright thunderbolts could not run through it in their course,
 1004  gliding on through an everlasting tract of age,
 1005  nor bring it about, by going, that any less remains to go;
 1006  so widely everywhere lies open a huge room for things,
 1007  its boundaries taken away, in every direction on all sides.
 1008  Further, nature herself prevents the sum of things from being able
 1009  to provide itself a limit, since she compels body to be bounded
 1010  by void, and what is void, in turn, to be bounded by body,
 1011  so that by this alternation she renders the whole infinite;
 1012  or else, too, if the other did not bound it, one of the two
 1013  would still stretch out without measure in its single nature,[69]
 1014  and neither sea nor earth nor the shining precincts of heaven
 1015  nor the mortal race nor the holy bodies of the gods
 1016  could hold their ground for the brief space of an hour;
 1017  for, driven apart from its union, the store of matter
 1018  would be carried, set loose, through the great void,
 1019  or rather it would never have come together and created
 1020  anything, since, scattered, it could not have been driven together.
 1021  For certainly the first-beginnings of things did not by design[70]
 1022  set themselves each in its order with a shrewd mind,
 1023  nor, surely, did they bargain what motions each should give;
 1024  but because, many and changed in many ways, throughout the whole
 1025  they are harried from infinite time, struck on by blows,
 1026  by trying every kind of motion and union
 1027  they come at last into such arrangements
 1028  as those by which this sum of things has been created and stands,
 1029  and, preserved as well through many great years
 1030  once it has been cast into fitting motions,
 1031  it brings about that the rivers replenish the greedy sea
 1032  with the large waves of their stream, and that the earth, warmed
 1033  by the sun's heat, renews her young, and the race of living things,
 1034  sent up, flourishes, and the fires of the gliding ether live.
 1035  This they could by no means do unless a store of matter
 1036  could rise up from the infinite,
 1037  from which they are used to making good, in time, each thing lost.
 1038  For just as the nature of living things, robbed of food,
 1039  wastes away, losing its body, so all things must
 1040  dissolve as soon as matter has failed to come in supply,
 1041  turned away by some cause from its road.
 1042  Nor can blows from outside, from every side, preserve
 1043  the whole sum, whatever has been gathered into union.
 1044  For they can strike often and hold back a part
 1045  until others come and the sum can be filled up;
 1046  yet at times they are forced to rebound, and at the same time
 1047  to grant the beginnings of things space and time for flight,
 1048  so that they can be carried away, free from the union.
 1049  Therefore, again and again, many must rise up;
 1050  and yet, so that even the blows themselves can be in supply,
 1051  there is need of an infinite force of matter on every side.
 1052  In these matters, Memmius, flee far from believing
 1053  what they say: that all things press toward the middle[71] of the sum,
 1054  and that for this reason the nature of the world stands without any
 1055  blows from outside, and top and bottom cannot be loosed
 1056  in any direction, because all things have pressed toward the middle
 1057  (if you believe that anything can stand upon itself),
 1058  and that the heavy things beneath the earth all press
 1059  upward and rest against the earth, set there upside down,
 1060  like the images of things we now see through water;
 1061  and contend by like reasoning that creatures wander upside down
 1062  and cannot fall back out of the earth into the regions of the sky
 1063  below, any more than our bodies could
 1064  of their own accord fly up into the precincts of heaven;
 1065  that when they see the sun, we behold the stars of night,
 1066  and that they share the seasons of heaven turn by turn with us
 1067  and pass nights equal to our days.
 1068  But an empty . . . these things for fools . . .[72]
 1069  since they hold it embraced . . .
 1070  For there can be no middle . . .
 1071  infinite; nor in any way, even if there were a middle now,
 1072  could anything stand still there . . .
 1073  than by any other principle far . . .
 1074  For all place and space, which we call void,
 1075  must, through the middle and through what is not the middle, give way
 1076  equally to weights, wherever their motions carry them.
 1077  Nor is there any place where bodies, once they have come there,
 1078  can lose the force of weight and stand still in the void;
 1079  nor must what is void hold up anything at all,
 1080  but must go on giving way, as its nature seeks.
 1081  Things cannot, therefore, on such a principle be held
 1082  in union, overcome by desire for the middle.
 1083  Further, since they imagine that not all bodies
 1084  press toward the middle, but only those of earth and liquid,
 1086  the moisture of the sea and the great waters from the mountains,[73]
 1085  and those that are held, as it were, in an earthy body,
 1087  but on the contrary teach that the thin breezes of air
 1088  and hot fires together are carried away from the middle,
 1089  and that for this reason the whole ether flickers with stars all round
 1090  and the flame of the sun is fed through the blue of heaven,
 1091  because heat, fleeing from the middle, gathers all of itself there,
 1092  and that the topmost boughs of trees could not put forth leaves at all
 1093  unless from the earth, little by little, food for each . . .[74]

Notes

[1] (1.1) "Mother of Aeneas' race" (Aeneadum genetrix): Venus, mother of
    Aeneas, is the ancestress of the Romans, so the poem opens on a Roman
    patron goddess. "Pleasure" (voluptas) is the word Epicurus's school used
    for the highest good; the epithet carries doctrine as well as charm.

[2] (1.7) "the artificer earth" (daedala tellus): daedala, the word for
    cunning workmanship, is rendered "artificer" wherever Lucretius applies
    it to nature (1.228, and "nature, artificer of things" at 5.234).

[3] (1.14-15) The source prints 15 before 14, an editorial transposition
    that lets the beasts bound over the pastures before they swim the
    rivers. The translation keeps the printed order and the printed numbers,
    here and at every transposition in the poem.

[4] (1.22) "the shining shores of light" (dias in luminis oras): Lucretius's
    phrase for birth into the daylight, repeated through the poem. Where the
    Latin has luminis oras without the adjective (1.170, 1.179, 2.577,
    2.617, 5.224, 5.781, 5.1389, 5.1455) the English is "the shores of
    light"; 1.22 is the only line with dias.

[5] (1.26) "our son of the Memmii" (Memmiadae nostro): Gaius Memmius, a
    Roman noble, praetor in 58 BC and the patron of Catullus as well, is the
    poem's addressee; "you" throughout the poem is Memmius. He is "the
    famous offspring of Memmius" at 1.42 and is named at 1.411 and 1.1052.

[6] (1.32) "Mars" is Mavors in the Latin, the old form of the god's name;
    the translation uses the familiar one. The war god lying conquered in
    Venus's lap lets the poet ask for peace from the one power that can calm
    him.

[7] (1.44-49) These six lines on the untroubled nature of the gods recur
    word for word at 2.646-651, where they belong to the argument. Many
    editors bracket them here as an intrusion from Book 2, since they sit
    oddly after a prayer to Venus for help; the source prints them, and so
    does the translation.

[8] (1.51) "true reasoning" (veram ad rationem): ratio standing alone is
    Epicurus's doctrine and method, and is "reasoning" throughout. Where it
    governs heaven, as in "the highest order of heaven" (summa caeli
    ratione, 54), it is the system of the world, "order"; where it is
    instrumental (qua ratione, 77, 128) it is "by what principle".

[9] (1.55-61) "the first-beginnings of things" (rerum primordia) is
    Lucretius's commonest name for what the Greek atomists called atoms, a
    word he never uses and the verse never supplies. He gives his cluster of
    names at once: "matter" (materies), "the generative bodies of things"
    (genitalia corpora), "seeds of things" (semina rerum), "first bodies"
    (corpora prima); "which we are used to calling" (suemus) marks them as
    the school's own Latin. Each is kept distinct throughout, and his
    shorter synonym principia is "beginnings" (see 1.484).

[10] (1.63) "religion" (religione): the plain word, not "superstition".
     Religio in Lucretius is the fear of gods who watch and punish, and the
     attack that follows falls on that; the proem's force depends on not
     softening the word.

[11] (1.66) "a man of Greece" (Graius homo) is Epicurus (341-270 BC),
     unnamed here and named in the whole poem only once, at 3.1042. "First
     ... and first" (primum ... primusque) makes him the hero of the poem's
     revolt.

[12] (1.73) "the flaming walls of the world" (flammantia moenia mundi): the
     fiery outer shell of our world, which in Epicurus's physics is one of
     countless worlds in an unbounded void. Epicurus's mind passes out
     through it and ranges "the whole immensity" (omne immensum). The same
     walls are imagined flying apart at 1.1102.

[13] (1.74) "in thought and mind" (mente animoque): animus is "mind"
     throughout the poem, and mens, where it stands beside animus as here,
     is "thought"; anima, the vital principle, is "spirit" (1.112). Book 3
     sets out the distinction.

[14] (1.77) "its boundary-stone set deep" (alte terminus haerens): the
     terminus was the stone sunk at the edge of a Roman field, sacred and
     not to be moved. The line, with 75-76, is repeated at 1.594-596.

[15] (1.84-86) "the Virgin of the Crossways" (Triviai virginis) is Diana,
     called Trivia as goddess of the three-way crossroads. Iphianassa is
     Iphigenia; Homer gives Agamemnon a daughter Iphianassa (Iliad 9.145),
     and Lucretius uses the older name. At Aulis the Greek fleet bound for
     Troy was held by contrary winds until Agamemnon sacrificed her. "The
     Danaans" (Danaum) are the Greeks.

[16] (1.98) "pure, impurely" (casta inceste): the girl is chaste and the act
     is a pollution. The whole scene turns marriage into slaughter: the
     woolen band (infula, 87) laid on her hair is the band of a sacrificial
     victim, and the escort she might have had was the wedding hymn
     (Hymenaeo, 97).

[17] (1.101) "So much evil could religion prompt" (tantum religio potuit
     suadere malorum): the most famous line of the poem, and its thesis in
     brief.

[18] (1.112) "the nature of the spirit" (natura animai): anima, the vital
     principle, is "spirit" throughout, never "soul". Lines 112-116 list the
     questions about it that fear exploits (is it born, does it die with us,
     does it go down to the underworld or pass into other creatures), and
     Book 3 answers them.

[19] (1.115) Orcus is the Roman name of the underworld and of its god; "the
     precincts of Acheron" (Acherusia templa, 120) is the same place named
     from its Greek river. Templa are regions, of the sky or of the earth,
     not temples.

[20] (1.117-126) Ennius (239-169 BC), author of the Annales, is the founder
     of Latin epic and Lucretius's predecessor in the hexameter; the "crown
     from Helicon", the Muses' mountain in Boeotia, is Greek poetry brought
     into Latin. In the proem of the Annales Homer appeared to Ennius in a
     dream and told him that his spirit had passed into him; so Ennius
     taught both the passage of spirits into other bodies (116) and the
     underworld of 120-126, the two beliefs this book sets out to destroy.

[21] (1.123) "images" (simulacra): the word Book 4 will make the name of the
     thin films thrown off by things, by which we see; here it is the ghosts
     of Ennius's underworld. The English keeps "images" for simulacra in
     every sense.

[22] (1.136-139) "the poverty of the language and the newness of the things"
     (egestatem linguae et rerum novitatem): the complaint that Latin has no
     words for Greek philosophy; Lucretius makes it again at 1.832 and
     3.260. His new words are the reason the translation fixes its own (see
     the translator's commentary).

[23] (1.146-148) The first of Lucretius's refrains: these three lines recur
     word for word at 2.59-61, 3.91-93 and 6.39-41, and the English repeats
     its own lines at each place. "The face and reasoning of nature"
     (naturae species ratioque) is nature's appearance and the account that
     explains it.

[24] (1.150) "nothing is ever begotten from nothing by divine will" (nullam
     rem e nihilo gigni divinitus umquam): the first principle of the
     physics. Divinitus, "by divine agency", carries the point: what is
     denied is creation from nothing by gods, who are invoked only where
     causes cannot be seen (151-154).

[25] (1.155-158) The source prints 156-158 before 155, an editorial
     transposition that makes 155 complete the "both ... and" of 158 (et
     unde ... et quo modo). The translation keeps the printed order and the
     numbers.

[26] (1.197) "as we see elements are to words" (ut verbis elementa videmus):
     elementa is the Latin for both the letters of the alphabet and the
     elements of things, and Lucretius's recurring analogy between first-
     beginnings and letters rests on the one word (1.823-829, 1.912-914,
     2.688-699, 2.1013-1022). The English keeps "elements" for both senses
     so that the analogy stays visible; at 1.81 the same word is the
     "elements", the rudiments, of a doctrine.

[27] (1.223) "through the voids" (per inania): the plural is the void inside
     compound things, the empty room between their first bodies. The void is
     argued for from 1.329.

[28] (1.250-251) "father ether ... the lap of mother earth" (pater aether
     ... in gremium matris terrai): the old pairing of Sky and Earth as
     parents, which Lucretius keeps as a figure inside a naturalistic
     argument; he develops it at 2.991-1001.

[29] (1.261) "made drunk with unmixed milk" (lacte mero): merum is wine
     taken without water, as Romans did not normally drink it; the young
     animals are tipsy on milk.

[30] (1.271) "the body of the sea" (corpus): so the source reads; many
     editors emend to pontum, "the sea". The translation keeps the printed
     word and takes it as the sea's body, since the argument is about
     bodies.

[31] (1.275) "forest-breaking blasts" (silvifragis flabris), like "the wave-
     breaking shore" (fluctifrago litore, 305), is a compound adjective of
     the kind Ennius coined for epic; Lucretius makes a few of his own,
     mostly for the violence of nature, and the English keeps them as
     compounds.

[32] (1.316-318) The bronze statues beside the city gates have their right
     hands worn thin by those who greet them: Romans touched or kissed the
     hand of a statue in passing, a salute to the god or the man it showed.

[33] (1.330) "there is void in things" (est in rebus inane): inane is "void"
     throughout, never "vacuum", though Lucretius also uses vacuum as its
     synonym (1.509, 1.523). "Place, untouchable, void and empty" (334) and
     "place and space" (locus ac spatium, 426) are other names for the same
     thing; the void is only room that offers no resistance.

[34] (1.336) "the office of body" (officium corporis) is "to obstruct and
     stand in the way" (officere atque obstare): the play of words makes the
     point that a body's one proper function is to be in the way, so that in
     a world without void nothing could begin to move. The "office" of body
     is to press downward at 1.362.

[35] (1.371) "certain men" (quidam) are not named. The doctrine is that of
     the Aristotelians and the Stoics, who denied void inside the world and
     explained motion by mutual replacement: the fish moves into the water
     that moves into the place the fish has left. Lucretius's answer is that
     the water has nowhere to go first.

[36] (1.422-423) "the sense common to all" (communis sensus) is the
     sensation everyone shares, not "common sense" in the modern meaning.
     That sensation is the first ground of trust (prima fides) is Epicurus's
     rule of truth: without it nothing about hidden things could be
     confirmed by reasoning. Lucretius defends the senses at length at
     4.469-521.

[37] (1.449-450) "conjoined" (coniuncta) and "accidents" (eventa) are
     Lucretius's Latin for Epicurus's permanent properties (symbebekota) and
     accidents (symptomata): what a thing cannot lose "without a deadly
     division" (sine permitiali discidio, 451), and what can come and go
     while the thing remains. The two English words are kept throughout.

[38] (1.459-463) Time has no existence of its own but is sensed from the
     motion and rest of things; it is an accident of accidents, as the
     school put it.

[39] (1.464-477) "Tyndareus' daughter" (Tyndaridem) is Helen; "Alexander"
     (474) is Paris, son of Priam, and "Phrygian" is Trojan; "Pergamum"
     (Pergama, 476) is the citadel of Troy. "Wooden" (durateus) is Homer's
     own word for the horse (Odyssey 8.493), taken into Latin letter for
     letter. The Trojan War is the example of events that have no being of
     their own.

[40] (1.469-470) "an accident of the lands ... of the regions themselves"
     (aliud terris, aliud regionibus ipsis): the deeds were accidents of the
     matter that took part in them, the lands and the people, and of the
     space in which they happened; neither the matter nor the place is the
     deed.

[41] (1.484) "a union of beginnings" (concilio ... principiorum): principia
     is Lucretius's shorter synonym for primordia, and the English keeps the
     two Latin words apart as "beginnings" and "first-beginnings" (so also
     1.740, 1.993, 1.1047); it is the same doctrine under a second name.
     "Union" (concilium) is the fixed word for the combination of first
     bodies that makes a thing (also 1.517, 1.1043).

[42] (1.493-496) "the ice of bronze" (glacies aeris) is Lucretius's own
     figure: bronze as a frozen thing that melts in fire. "As is the custom"
     (rite, 495) is the Roman way of holding the cup in the hand while the
     water is poured in from above, so that heat and cold are felt through
     the silver.

[43] (1.520) "nothing that stood empty as void": the source prints vocaret,
     "called", which gives no sense; nearly all editors read vacaret, "stood
     empty", and here the translation follows the emendation rather than the
     printed word.

[44] (1.531) "as I showed you above, a little while before" (id quod iam
     supra tibi paulo ostendimus ante) repeats 1.429 word for word; there it
     looked back to 1.335-345, here to 1.503-527. The English repeats its
     own line.

[45] (1.548) "of solid singleness" (solida simplicitate): simplicitas is the
     first-beginnings' being single, without parts that could be separated;
     "singleness" is kept for it (1.574, 1.609, 1.612).

[46] (1.586) "nature's covenants" (foedera naturai): foedus is a treaty, and
     the figure of a binding compact that fixes what each thing can do is
     Lucretius's own; it recurs at 2.302, 5.310 and 5.924, and "stands
     decreed" (sancitum, 587) is the language of law. The English keeps
     "covenants" throughout.

[47] (1.594-596) These lines repeat 1.75-77, the proem's account of what
     Epicurus brought back, now not a discovery but a premise. The English
     repeats its own lines.

[48] (1.599-608) The least parts: the "outermost point" (extremum cacumen)
     that the eye can just make out on a visible body has no parts and is "a
     least nature" (minima natura); by analogy the first-beginnings, too,
     are made of least parts that exist only as parts and never separately
     (Epicurus, Letter to Herodotus 56-59). The regress of 615-622, that
     without a least the smallest thing would have as many parts as the
     whole, is the argument's other half, and the point returns against
     Empedocles at 749-752.

[49] (1.629) "the nature of things, the creator" (rerum natura creatrix):
     nature as maker, a phrase Lucretius uses again at 2.1117 and 5.1362. It
     is a figure for the workings of first-beginnings and void, not a
     goddess.

[50] (1.638-639) Heraclitus of Ephesus (about 500 BC), who made fire the
     stuff of all things, was called "the Obscure" in antiquity for his
     riddling style, and 639 plays on it: "bright" in fame (clarus) for
     "dark" speech (obscuram). "The empty-headed" (inanis) is the same word
     as "the void" (inane), so his admirers are hollow men. "Is the first to
     enter the battle" (init proelia primus) casts the survey of rival
     schools as a campaign.

[51] (1.670-671) The refrain of change as death: these two lines recur at
     1.792-793, 2.753-754 and 3.519-520, verbatim in the English as in the
     Latin. Whatever changes so as to leave its own bounds (suis finibus)
     has ceased to be what it was; so the first-beginnings themselves must
     never change.

[52] (1.684-685) "collisions, motions, order, position, shapes" (concursus
     motus ordo positura figurae): the things by which the same first bodies
     make different things. The list, with variations, is one of Lucretius's
     refrains (1.633-634, 2.726, 2.1021). Here it answers Heraclitus: fire
     itself is one such arrangement.

[53] (1.693-700) The senses are the first measure of truth, and Heraclitus,
     "starting from the senses, fights against the senses": he trusts sight
     when it shows him fire and distrusts it for everything else. This is
     Epicurus's rule of truth again (1.422-425), argued out at 4.469-521.

[54] (1.705-706) These lines repeat 1.635-636, with "can consist"
     (consistere posse) for "consists of fire alone" (consistere solo);
     Lucretius reopens the polemic in his own earlier words so as to extend
     it to the other single elements.

[55] (1.707-713) Air as the beginning of things is the doctrine of
     Anaximenes of Miletus, water that of Thales; for earth alone no thinker
     is known, and Aristotle remarks that no one made earth the sole
     principle. "Those who double the first-beginnings" (712) are the pairs
     the ancient handbooks assign to Oenopides of Chios (fire and air) and
     Xenophanes (earth and water).

[56] (1.715) "breath" (anima): here the element air, in the list of
     Empedocles' four, not the spirit of 1.112. "Spirit" is kept for the
     vital principle only, and this non-technical sense is noted wherever it
     occurs (6.130 and after).

[57] (1.716-725) Empedocles of Acragas (now Agrigento) in Sicily, of the
     fifth century BC, taught that all things are mixtures of four "roots",
     fire, air, earth and water, and wrote his physics in hexameters, which
     is why Lucretius praises him as a poet in 731-733 while refuting him.
     Sicily is "three-cornered" (triquetris), its old name Trinacria; the
     strait is the Strait of Messina, with the whirlpool Charybdis of
     Odyssey 12, and Etna is described between eruptions.

[58] (1.721) "the shores of Aeolia's lands" (Aeoliae terrarum oras): so the
     source reads, though the Aeolian Islands lie north of Sicily and the
     mainland across the strait is Italy, which Munro's emendation Italiae
     names. The translation keeps the printed reading; the mainland is meant
     either way.

[59] (1.738-739) The Pythia was Apollo's priestess at Delphi, who prophesied
     seated on the tripod and, as Lucretius has it, from the laurel,
     Apollo's tree. To rank these thinkers' answers above hers is a stroke
     against religion in passing. The two lines recur at 5.111-112.

[60] (1.769) This line repeats 1.762 word for word and does not fit here;
     editors generally delete it as a copyist's stray repetition. The source
     prints it, and the translation keeps it in place.

[61] (1.830-832) "Homoeomeria" (homoeomerian), Greek for "likeness of
     parts", is the Aristotelian name for the doctrine of Anaxagoras of
     Clazomenae (about 500-428 BC) that each kind of stuff is made of tiny
     portions of the same stuff: bone of little bones, gold of specks of
     gold. Lucretius leaves the word Greek, blaming "the poverty of our
     native speech" (patrii sermonis egestas) as at 1.139, and the
     translation leaves it Greek too.

[62] (1.860a) The source marks a line lost after 860: the sentence that
     began "since food increases the body and nourishes it" has lost its
     conclusion. The translation prints the gap and supplies nothing; "or if
     they say" (sive, 861) answers an alternative that is no longer there.

[63] (1.873-874) The sense of these two lines is disputed, and editors
     suspect corruption or a lost line: the earth's produce is said to rise
     "out of the wood" (lignis), where the argument wants the earth. The
     translation renders the text as printed.

[64] (1.909-914) Lines 909-910 repeat 818-819 word for word. The clinching
     example turns on Latin letters: ligna, "wood", and ignes, "fires", are
     made of nearly the same letters in a different order, as fire and wood
     are made of the same first-beginnings in a different arrangement.
     English "wood" and "fires" cannot show it; the note has to.

[65] (1.915-920) If like could come only from like, the first-beginnings of
     a laughing or weeping creature would themselves laugh and weep.
     Lucretius returns to the joke at 2.976-990, where he argues that
     sentient things are made of bodies without sense.

[66] (1.922-925) The thyrsus is the ivy-wound wand carried by Bacchus and
     his followers, whose touch brought on the god's frenzy; the hope of
     fame strikes the poet with it, so his inspiration is Bacchic before it
     is the Muses'.

[67] (1.926-950) These lines return almost word for word as the proem of
     Book 4 (4.1-25), and the English there repeats the English here. "The
     Pierides" are the Muses, from Pieria below Mount Olympus; the untrodden
     places and untouched springs are the poet's claim to a new kind of
     poem. The honey on the rim of the children's cup of wormwood
     (absinthia, 936-942) is the image of his method: the bitter medicine is
     the doctrine, the honey is the verse.

[68] (1.968-983) The thrown spear: run to the supposed edge of space and
     throw; either the spear flies on, and there is space beyond, or
     something stops it, and that something is beyond. Simplicius attributes
     the argument to Archytas of Tarentum, the Pythagorean of the fourth
     century BC, with an outstretched hand in place of the spear; Lucretius
     gives it its classic form.

[69] (1.1013) The sentence breaks here, and many editors posit a lost line
     or lines after 1013. The sense can be followed, since the condition of
     1012 is answered by 1014; the translation prints the text as it stands
     and supplies nothing.

[70] (1.1021-1028) The first-beginnings did not take their places "by
     design" (consilio) or "bargain" (pepigere) over their motions: the
     ordered world is the arrangement that happened to hold, out of every
     arrangement tried through infinite time. Book 5 (5.837-877) applies the
     same reasoning to the first living creatures, and a modern reader will
     hear natural selection in it; that is the reader's association, not the
     poet's claim.

[71] (1.1052-1067) "That all things press toward the middle of the sum" (in
     medium summae ... omnia niti) is the doctrine of a finite spherical
     world held together by the tendency of everything toward its center,
     Aristotle's and above all the Stoics'; Lucretius names no one. With it
     goes the belief in the antipodes (1058-1067), people on the far side of
     the earth standing "upside down" (suppa, an old word) relative to us
     and seeing the sun when we see the stars. In an infinite universe there
     is no middle, so both fall together.

[72] (1.1068-1073) The right-hand ends of these six lines are lost: a leaf
     of the ancestor of our manuscripts was damaged, and only the line
     openings survive. The translation prints the gaps as ". . ." and
     supplies nothing; the drift is that the "middle" is an empty notion of
     fools and that an infinite space has none.

[73] (1.1085-1086) The source prints 1086 before 1085, an editorial
     transposition; the translation keeps the printed order and the numbers.

[74] (1.1093) Eight lines are lost after 1093 (1094-1101), and the text
     resumes in mid-sentence at 1102. The translation prints eight
     unnumbered gaps and supplies nothing. The lost lines finished the
     opponents' view and began Lucretius's reply: that if their world had a
     center and no matter beyond it, it would fly apart.

[75] (1.1102) "the walls of the world" (moenia mundi): the walls Epicurus
     passed through at 1.73, imagined here as flying apart "in the manner of
     winged flames" (volucri ritu flammarum) if the sum of matter were
     finite.

[76] (1.1117) "so things will kindle lights for things" (ita res accendent
     lumina rebus): the book ends on the image with which its argument
     began, the light of understanding against "blind night" (caeca nox,
     1115-1116), as at 1.144-148; "unseen" in "unseen first-beginnings"
     (primordia caeca, 1110) is the same adjective.
