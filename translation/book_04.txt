  447  But if by chance a hand placed beneath one eye
  448  presses it from below, by a certain feeling it comes about
  449  that all things we look at seem, as we look, to become double:
  450  double the lights of the lamps, flowering with flames,
  451  and through the whole house the furniture doubled in pairs,
  452  and men with double faces and twin bodies.
  453  Again, when sleep has bound our limbs in sweet slumber
  454  and the whole body lies in deepest rest,
  455  yet we seem to ourselves to be awake and to move
  456  our limbs, and in the blind darkness of the night
  457  we think we see the sun and the light of day,
  458  and, in a closed room, sky, sea, rivers, mountains
  459  we seem to change, and to cross the plains on foot,
  460  and to hear sounds, when the stern silences of the night
  461  hold fast on every side, and to give answers, saying nothing.
  462  We see many other wonders of this kind,
  463  all seeking, as it were, to violate our trust in the senses,
  464  in vain, since the greatest part of them deceive
  465  because of the mind's suppositions, which we add ourselves,
  466  so that things not seen by the senses pass for seen;
  467  for nothing is harder than to separate things plain
  468  from doubtful ones, which the mind straightway adds of itself.
  469  Again, if anyone thinks that nothing can be known, he does not know
  470  whether even that can be known, since he admits he knows nothing.
  471  Against this man, then, I will forbear to argue the case,
  472  who has set himself with his head where his footprints should be.
  473  And yet, granting that he knows even this, still I would ask
  474  this very thing: since he has seen nothing true in things before,
  475  from where does he know what it is to know and, in turn, not to know,
  476  what thing has created the notion of the true and the false,
  477  and what thing has proved the doubtful to differ from the certain?
  478  You will find that from the senses first was created
  479  the notion of the true, and that the senses cannot be refuted.
  480  For that must be found to deserve greater trust
  481  which can of its own accord overcome false things with true.
  482  Further, what ought to be held in greater trust than the senses?
  483  Or will reasoning, sprung from a false sense, have the power
  484  to speak against them, when it has sprung wholly from the senses?
  485  And unless they are true, all reasoning too becomes false.
  486  Or will the eyes be able to correct the ears, or the ears
  487  the touch? Or again will the mouth's taste accuse this touch,
  488  or will the nostrils confute it, or the eyes refute it?
  489  It is not so, I think. For to each a separate power
  490  has been allotted, each has its own force, and so it must be
  491  that what is soft, and cold or hot, is perceived apart, {{NOTE: text uncertain at 4.491: the transmitted "videre" ("to see") sits oddly with touch; editors emend or transpose; rendered by sense}}
  492  and that the various colors of things are perceived apart,
  493  and whatever things are joined to colors.
  494  Apart, likewise, the mouth's taste has its power, apart smells
  495  are born, apart sounds. And so it must be
  496  that one sense cannot convict another.
  497  Nor, further, will they be able to correct themselves,
  498  since equal trust must always be placed in them.
  499  Therefore what has seemed to them at any time is true.

  500  And if reasoning cannot untie the cause
  501  why things that were square close at hand are seen far off
  502  as round, still it is better, if you are at a loss for reasoning,
  503  to give faulty causes for each of the two shapes
  504  than to let things plain to see slip from your hands somewhere
  505  and violate the first trust and tear up all
  506  the foundations on which life and safety rest.
  507  For not only would all reasoning fall in ruin; life itself
  508  would collapse at once, unless you dared to trust the senses
  509  and avoid steep places and the other things that are
  510  to be fled in this kind, and follow what is their opposite.
  511  That whole store of words, then, is empty for you,
  512  which is drawn up and made ready against the senses.
  513  Finally, as in a building, if the first rule is crooked,
  514  and if the square is false and slips from its straight lines,
  515  and if the level limps a hair's breadth at any point,
  516  everything must be made faulty and askew,
  517  crooked, sagging, leaning forward, leaning back, the roof out of true,
  518  so that some parts seem already to want to fall, and do fall,
  519  all of it betrayed by those first false judgments;
  520  so then your reasoning of things must be crooked
  521  and false, whatever reasoning has risen from false senses.
  522  Now, by what means each of the other senses perceives
  523  its own object, the reasoning left is by no means rugged.
  524  First of all, sound and every voice are heard when, working their way
  525  into the ears, they have struck the sense with their body.
  526  For we must admit that voice too, and sound, are bodily,
  527  since they are able to strike upon the senses.
  528  Further, the voice often scrapes the throat, and shouting
  529  makes the windpipe rougher as it goes out,
  530  since, rising in a greater crowd through a narrow way,
  531  the first-beginnings of voices have begun to go out.
  532  Clearly the door of the mouth too is scraped when it is filled.
  533  There is no doubt, then, that voices and words consist
  534  of bodily beginnings, since they are able to do harm. {{NOTE: principia, "beginnings", Lucretius's alternative term for the first-beginnings (primordia, 4.531)}}
  535  Nor, likewise, does it escape you what body is lost, and what
  536  is drawn from men's sinews and their very strength
  537  by unbroken speech carried on to the shadow of black night
  538  from the rising brightness of the dawn,
  539  above all if it is poured out with the loudest shouting.
  540  Therefore the voice must be bodily,
  541  since one who talks much loses part of his body.
  542  Roughness of voice, again, comes from roughness
  543  of the beginnings, and smoothness likewise is made by smoothness;
  544  nor do the first-beginnings pierce the ears with like shape
  545  when the trumpet bellows deep with its low-pressed growl
  546  and the barbarous horn, roused, booms back its hoarse drone, {{NOTE: the source prints barbita ("lyre"); most editors read barbara, the "barbarous" Phrygian horn, which the sense requires and is rendered here}}
  547  and when the Daulian birds, born in the gardens of Helicon, {{NOTE: nightingales, after the myth of Procne and Philomela set at Daulis; the line is textually disputed (Bailey reads swans from the torrents of Helicon)}}
  548  raise their liquid lament with mourning voice.
  549  So when we press these voices out from deep within our body
  550  and send them out straight through the mouth,
  551  the nimble tongue, artificer of sinews, articulates them, {{NOTE: daedala, "artificer", as in the artificer earth (1.7); most texts read verborum, "artificer of words", for the source's nervorum}}
  552  and the shaping of the lips gives them form in its turn.
  553  When the space is not long from which each voice
  554  sets out to arrive, the words themselves too must
  555  be heard plainly and distinguished syllable by syllable;
  556  for the voice keeps its shaping and keeps its form.
  557  But if the space set between is longer than is fit,
  558  the words must be confused passing through much air
  559  and the voice thrown into disorder as it flies through the air.
  560  So it comes about that you can perceive a sound but cannot
  561  make out what the meaning of the words is;
  562  so confused and tangled does the voice arrive.
  563  Further, a single word often rouses the ears
  564  of all in a crowd, sent out from a crier's mouth.
  565  One voice, then, suddenly scatters into many voices,
  566  since it divides itself among separate ears,
  567  stamping on the words their form and clear sound.
  568  But the part of the voices that does not fall on the ears themselves
  569  is carried past and perishes, spread out in vain through the breezes.
  570  Part, dashed against solid places, is thrown back, returns
  571  a sound, and at times deceives us with the echo of a word.
  572  When you see this well, you can give an account
  573  to yourself and to others of how, through lonely places,
  574  rocks give back the matching forms of words in order,
  575  when we search among the shadowed mountains for straying comrades
  576  and call them, scattered, with a great voice.
  577  I have seen places give back even six or seven voices
  578  when you threw out one: so the hills themselves, to hills
  579  beating the words back, repeated the sayings as they returned.
  580  These places, the neighbors fancy, goat-footed Satyrs
  581  and Nymphs hold, and they say there are Fauns,
  582  by whose night-wandering din and jesting play
  583  they declare the speechless silences are commonly broken,
  584  and sounds of strings arise, and the sweet laments
  585  that the pipe pours out when struck by the players' fingers;
  586  and that the race of farmers hears it far and wide when Pan,
  587  shaking the pine coverings of his half-beast head,
  588  often runs his curved lip over the open reeds,
  589  so that the pipe will not cease pouring out its woodland music.
  590  Other marvels and portents of this kind they tell,
  591  so that they may not be thought to hold lonely places
  592  deserted even by the gods. So they toss out wonders in their talk,
  593  or they are led by some other reason, as the whole
  594  human race is all too greedy for listening ears.
  595  For the rest, it is no wonder by what principle,
  596  through places where the eyes cannot make out things in plain view,
  597  voices come through these places and assail the ears;
  598  we often see a conversation through closed doors as well;
  599  no doubt because a voice can pass unharmed through the winding
  600  passages of things, while images refuse;
  601  for they are torn apart unless they swim through straight passages,
  602  such as those of glass, through which every appearance flies.
  603  Further, a voice is divided in every direction,
  604  since voices are born one from another, once a single one
  605  has sprung forth and leapt apart into many, as a spark of fire
  606  often spreads itself out into its own fires.
  607  Therefore places hidden away behind are filled with voices,
  608  which all around are astir and roused with sound.
  609  But images all travel along straight paths
  610  once they are sent out; and so no one can see
  611  over a wall, but can take in voices from beyond it.
  612  And yet this very voice too, while it passes through the closed rooms
  613  of houses, is blunted and enters the ears confused,
  614  and we seem to hear a sound rather than words.

  615  As for how we perceive flavor, the tongue and palate
  616  hold a little more reasoning in them, and more labor.
  617  To begin with, we perceive flavor in the mouth when, chewing,
  618  we press it out of food, as with a sponge full of water,
  619  if someone should start to squeeze it in the hand and dry it.
  620  Then all we press out is spread through the palate's passages
  621  and through the winding pores of the loose-woven tongue.
  622  So when the bodies of the oozing flavor are smooth,
  623  they touch sweetly, and sweetly handle all
  624  the moist precincts sweating round about the tongue;
  625  but on the contrary they prick the sense and tear it as they come,
  626  the more so the more each is filled with roughness.
  627  Next, the pleasure from flavor lies within the bounds of the palate;
  628  but when it has plunged down through the throat,
  629  there is no pleasure, while it is all being spread into the limbs;
  630  nor does it matter at all on what food the body is fed,
  631  so long as what you take you can digest and spread
  632  through the limbs, and keep the stomach's full and even tenor.
  633  Now, so we may see why one food suits some, another others,
  634  I shall explain, and why what is harsh and bitter to some
  635  can yet seem to others exceedingly sweet,
  636  and why in these things there is such distance and difference
  637  that what is food to some is to others sharp poison;
  638  for indeed there is a snake which, touched by a man's spittle,
  639  perishes and destroys itself by gnawing at itself.
  640  Further, hellebore is sharp poison to us,
  641  but to goats and to quails it adds fat.
  642  So that you can learn by what means this comes about,
  643  first it is fitting to remember what we said before,
  644  that seeds are held mixed in things in many ways.
  645  Further, all living creatures whatever that take food,
  646  just as they are unlike outwardly, and, kind by kind,
  647  the outer contour of their limbs confines them,
  648  so too they consist of seeds and vary in their shape.
  649  Again, since the seeds differ, it must be that the intervals
  650  and the paths, which we call pores, differ too
  651  in all the limbs, and in the mouth and the palate itself.
  652  Therefore some must be smaller and some larger,
  653  for some creatures they must be three-cornered, for others square,
  654  many round, some many-angled in many ways.
  655  For as the order of the shapes and their motions demand,
  656  so the shapes of the pores must differ,
  657  and the paths vary, according as the texture confines them.
  658  So when what is sweet to some becomes bitter to others,
  659  for the one to whom it is sweet the smoothest bodies must
  660  enter the passages of the palate caressingly,
  661  but for those to whom the same thing is bitter within,
  662  rough and hooked bodies, doubtless, make their way into the throat.
  663  Now it is easy from these things to understand each case.
  664  For indeed when fever has risen in someone, the bile prevailing,
  665  or the force of disease has been roused by some other means,
  666  then the whole body is thrown into disorder, and all
  667  the positions of the beginnings are changed;
  668  so it comes about that bodies which before suited the sense
  669  now do not suit it, and others are more fitted,
  670  which, making their way in, can beget a bitter sense;
  671  for both kinds are mixed together in the flavor of honey,
  672  a thing we have often shown you above already.
  673  Come now, how the touch of odor strikes the nostrils
  674  I shall set out. First, there must be many things
  675  from which a varied stream of odors rolls flowing,
  676  and we must suppose they flow and are sent out and scattered everywhere;
  677  but one odor is more fitted to some creatures, another to others,
  678  because of their unlike forms. And so through the air
  679  bees are drawn, however far, by the odor of honey,
  680  and vultures by carcasses; then, where the cloven hoof of beasts
  681  has carried its steps, the far-reaching force of hounds
  682  leads on, and the human smell is scented from far away
  683  by the savior of the citadel of Romulus' sons, the white goose. {{NOTE: the sacred geese of Juno on the Capitol, whose cackling woke the defenders against the Gauls' night attack (Livy 5.47)}}
  684  So one scent given to some, another to others, leads each
  685  to its own pasture, and makes it spring back from foul poison,
  686  and in this way the generations of wild beasts are preserved.
  687  This odor itself, then, whatever it is that provokes the nostrils,
  688  is such that one can be sent farther than another;
  689  but still none of them is carried as far
  690  as sound, as voice, not to speak now of the things
  691  that strike the sight of the eyes and provoke vision.
  692  For it comes wandering and slowly, and perishes on the way,
  693  little by little, easily torn apart into the breezes of air;
  694  first, because it is barely sent out from deep within the thing;
  695  for that odors flow and withdraw from the depths of things
  696  is shown by this, that all things seem to smell more when broken,
  697  when crushed, when dissolved by fire.
  698  Next, you may see that it is made of larger
  699  beginnings than voice, since through walls of stone
  700  it does not pass, where voice and sound are commonly carried.
  701  Therefore you will see as well that what smells is not so easy
  702  to track down, in what region it is placed;
  703  for the blow cools as it lingers through the air,
  704  and the messengers of things do not run down hot to the sense.
  705  And so dogs often go astray and search for the tracks.
  706  Nor yet is this found only in odors and in the class
  707  of flavors; likewise the appearances and colors of things
  708  do not all so suit the senses of all
  709  that some are not harsher to some to look at.
  710  Indeed, the cock, who with clapping wings drives out the night
  711  and is wont to call the dawn with ringing voice,
  712  ravening lions are not able to stand against
  713  and look upon: so instantly do they remember flight. {{NOTE: a common ancient belief, also in Pliny, Natural History 8.52 and 10.47}}
  714  Doubtless because there are in the body of cocks certain
  715  seeds which, when they are sent into the eyes of lions,
  716  bore into the pupils and give sharp pain,
  717  so that, fierce as they are, they cannot hold out against them,
  718  although these things cannot harm our sight at all,
  719  either because they do not get in, or because, once they do,
  720  a free way out of the eyes is given them, so that by lingering
  721  they cannot harm the eyes in any part.
  722  Come now, hear what things move the mind, and from where
  723  the things that come into the mind come, learn in a few words.
  724  First, I say this: that images of things wander,
  725  many, in many ways, in all directions on every side,
  726  thin images, which easily join with one another in the air
  727  when they meet, like a spider's web or a leaf of gold.
  728  For indeed these are much thinner in texture
  729  than those that take hold of the eyes and provoke vision,
  730  since these pass through the loose openings of the body and stir
  731  the thin nature of the mind within, and provoke sense.
  732  And so we see Centaurs and the limbs of Scyllas
  733  and the dog-faces of Cerberus, and images of those
  734  whose bones, after death, the earth embraces; {{NOTE: repeats 1.135 verbatim in the Latin; the English repeats its own line}}
  735  since images of every kind are carried everywhere,
  736  some that come to be of their own accord in the air itself,
  737  some that withdraw from all manner of things,
  738  and those that are made by combining the shapes of these.
  739  For certainly no image of a Centaur comes from a living one,
  740  since no such nature of living thing ever existed;
  741  but when by chance the images of horse and man meet,
  742  they easily cling together at once, as we said before,
  743  because of their subtle nature and thin texture.
  744  Other things of this kind are created by the same principle.
  745  And since these move nimbly with the utmost lightness,
  746  as I showed before, any one subtle image easily
  747  moves our mind with a single blow;
  748  for the mind is thin, and wonderfully mobile itself.
  749  That these things happen as I tell, you may easily learn from this:
  750  insofar as the one is like the other, what we see with the mind
  751  and with the eyes, it must come about by a like principle.
  752  Now, then, since I have shown that I see lions, say,
  753  by means of images, whatever images provoke the eyes,
  754  you may know that the mind is moved by a like principle
  755  by images of lions and the rest, which it sees just as
  756  well as the eyes, except that it perceives thinner ones.
  757  Nor by any other principle, when sleep has spread out the limbs,
  758  does the thought of the mind keep watch, except that the same images
  759  provoke our minds as when we are awake,
  760  so much so that we seem surely to see one
  761  whom, life left behind, death and the earth now possess.
  762  Nature compels this to happen because all
  763  the senses of the body, blocked, rest throughout the limbs,
  764  and cannot refute the false with things that are true.
  765  Further, memory lies idle and languishes in sleep,
  766  and does not protest that this one, in the grip of death and doom
  767  long since, is not the one the mind believes it sees alive.
  768  For the rest, it is no wonder that images move
  769  and throw their arms in rhythm, and their other limbs;
  770  for it happens that in dreams an image seems to do this.
  771  For indeed, when the first perishes and a second is born after
  772  in another posture, the earlier seems to have changed its gesture.
  773  Of course we must suppose that this happens by a swift principle:
  774  so great is the mobility, and so great the abundance of things,
  775  and so great, in any one moment we can perceive,
  776  the abundance of tiny parts, that it can supply them. {{NOTE: particulae, Lucretius's diminutive, used here only; the tiny parts are the images themselves, not the first-beginnings}}

  777  And many questions arise in these matters, and much we must
  778  make clear, if we wish to set out the matter plainly.
  779  It is asked first of all why, whatever has come into anyone's
  780  fancy, the mind thinks of that very thing at once.
  781  Do the images keep watch upon our will,
  782  and does an image meet us the moment we wish,
  783  if our heart is set on the sea, on the earth, or finally the sky?
  784  Assemblies of men, a procession, banquets, battles,
  785  does nature create them all at a word and make them ready?
  786  And this especially when, in the same region and place,
  787  the minds of others are thinking of things all far unlike.
  788  What further, when we see the images advancing in rhythm
  789  in our sleep, and moving their supple limbs,
  790  when nimbly they fling out their supple arms by turns
  791  and repeat the gesture to our eyes with a matching foot?
  792  No doubt the images are steeped in art and wander well taught,
  793  so that they can put on shows in the time of night.
  794  Or will this rather be the truth? Because in a single time,
  795  when we perceive, that is, when a single sound is uttered,
  796  many times lie hidden, which reasoning finds to exist,
  797  therefore it comes about that in any time whatever
  798  every image is at hand, ready in every place.
  799  So great is the mobility, and so great the abundance of things.
  800  Here, when the first perishes and a second is born after it
  801  in another posture, the earlier seems to have changed its gesture.
  802  And because they are thin, the mind cannot sharply see
  803  any but those it strains toward; and so all that are
  804  beyond these perish, except those it has made itself ready for.
  805  And it makes itself ready, and hopes it will come about
  806  that it sees what follows on each thing: and so it does.
  807  Do you not see that the eyes too, when things that are thin
  808  beyond these perish, except those it has made itself ready for, {{NOTE: 4.808 repeats 4.804 and breaks the sentence; most editors delete it}}
  809  they have begun to look at, strain and make themselves ready,
  810  and that without this we cannot see sharply?
  811  And yet even with things in plain view you can recognize
  812  that if you do not turn your mind to them, it is just as if
  813  they were all the time set apart and far removed.
  814  Why then is it a wonder if the mind loses all else
  815  except the things to which it is itself given over?
  816  Then, from small signs we form the greatest conjectures
  817  and lead ourselves into the snare of self-deception.
  818  It happens too at times that the image supplied
  819  is not of the same kind, but what was a woman before
  820  seems to be there in our hands, made into a man,
  821  or one face and one age follow after another.
  822  That we do not wonder at this, sleep and forgetfulness see to it.
  823  In these matters there is a fault I earnestly want you {{NOTE: textual crux; the transmitted "vehementer inesse" is corrupt, and the sense "I strongly wish you" follows the usual emendation}}
  824  to flee, an error to avoid with foresight:
  825  do not think that the bright lights of the eyes were created
  826  so that we might look before us, and that, so we could take
  827  long strides, the tapering lengths of calf and thigh,
  828  set firm upon the feet, are for that reason able to bend;
  829  then too, that the forearms were fitted to strong upper arms,
  830  and hands were given as servants on either side,
  831  so that we could do what would be of use for life.
  832  All other things of this kind which men expound, whatever they are,
  833  are all backward, by reasoning turned upside down,
  834  since nothing was born in the body so that we could use
  835  it, but what is born creates its use.
  836  Nor was there seeing before the lights of the eyes were born,
  837  nor pleading with words before the tongue was created;
  838  rather, the origin of the tongue came long before
  839  speech, and the ears were created long before
  840  sound was heard, and finally all the limbs
  841  existed, I think, before there was any use for them;
  842  they cannot, therefore, have grown for the sake of using.
  843  But, by contrast, to join hand to hand in the contests of battle,
  844  to mangle the frame and befoul the limbs with gore,
  845  came long before shining weapons flew,
  846  and nature compelled men to avoid a wound
  847  before the left arm, by art, held up the shield against it.
  848  And surely to give the tired body over to rest
  849  is far older than the soft coverings of a bed,
  850  and to quench thirst came before cups were born.
  851  These things, then, may be believed devised for the sake
  852  of using, since they were found out from use and life.
  853  But all those stand apart which, born first themselves,
  854  afterwards gave us the notion of their usefulness.
  855  Of this kind above all we see the senses and the limbs;
  856  therefore, again and again, you are far from able to believe
  857  that they could have been created for the service of usefulness.
  858  This too is no cause for wonder, that the nature
  859  of the body of each living creature seeks food.
  860  For indeed I have taught that many bodies flow and withdraw
  861  from things in many ways; but most must go
  862  from living creatures; for since they are worked by motion,
  863  many are breathed out through the mouth when, weary, they pant,
  864  and many are pressed from deep within and carried off in sweat.
  865  By these things, then, the body grows thin, and its whole
  866  nature is undermined, and pain follows on this.
  867  Food therefore is taken, to prop up the frame
  868  and, given within, to renew the strength, and to stop up
  869  the gaping desire for eating through limbs and veins.
  870  Moisture likewise goes off into all the places
  871  that call for moisture; and the many bodies of heat
  872  that have massed together and kindle fires in our stomach
  873  the liquid, coming, scatters and puts out like fire,
  874  so that the dry heat can burn the frame no longer.
  875  In this way, then, the panting thirst is washed out
  876  of our body, in this way the hungry desire is filled.
  877  Now how it comes about that we can take steps forward
  878  when we wish, and why it is given us to move our limbs,
  879  and what thing has grown used to pushing forward this great weight
  880  of our body, I shall tell: you, take in my words.
  881  I say that first images of walking come
  882  to our mind and strike the mind, as we said before.
  883  From there will arises; for no one begins to do
  884  anything before the mind has foreseen what it wills.
  885  Of what it foresees, an image of that thing exists.
  886  Therefore, when the mind so stirs itself that it wills to go
  887  and step forward, at once it strikes the force of spirit
  888  that is scattered through the whole body, through limbs and frame;
  889  and that is easy to do, since it is held joined with it.
  890  Then this in turn strikes the body, and so the whole
  891  mass is little by little pushed forward and moved.
  892  Further, the body then grows loose in texture too, and air,
  893  as surely it must, being always quick to move,
  894  comes through the opened passages and enters them in plenty,
  895  and so is dispersed into every smallest part

Notes (DRAFT)

[DRAFT] (4.1) Pieridum = the Muses, named for Pieria, their old haunt in Macedonia. In the Latin, 4.1-25 repeat 1.926-950 exactly, so by register policy (principle 3) the English here must be Book 1's committed lines word for word. The fresh draft is to be replaced with them before assembly.

[DRAFT] (4.7) religionum ... animum: religio is rendered plainly "religion", as at 1.63, so the "tight knots" are those of religion itself, not of mere superstition. animum is "mind"; the register's contrast between animus ("mind") and anima ("spirit") becomes explicit at 4.46 and 4.121.

[DRAFT] (4.31) simulacra: "images", the Epicurean eidola, thin films shed from the surface of bodies. The Latin also uses imago (4.38), effigiae (4.51) and figurae, which the translation keeps distinct as "image," "effigies," and "shapes".

[DRAFT] (4.32) The transition appears twice: 4.26-32 and 4.33-37 are two "since I have taught" clauses (atoms; the mind) followed by the same main clause, so 4.30-31 = 4.36-37. This is probably an unrevised double draft, which some editors resolve by deleting one set. The translation keeps both in Bailey's order and repeats the English word for word.

[DRAFT] (4.35) ordia prima is a shortened form of primordia, the same technical term. The translation's "first origins" departs from the register's "first-beginnings" (used for primordia at 4.50 and 4.111), and the translator may wish to harmonize it. Primordia is the word a modern reader will hear as "atoms".

[DRAFT] (4.46) Lines 45-53 are suspected of being misplaced or a remnant of the earlier draft. The sense of 4.42-50 is continuous, but 4.51-53 restate the thesis already announced at 4.30-31 and 4.36-37. The translation keeps every line in the source order and does not rearrange.

[DRAFT] (4.46-48) Acherunte: Acheron, a river of the underworld, here standing for the realm of the dead, as at 1.120. The point is Epicurean: dream-visions of the dead are only images, because at death the spirit (animas) and the mind (animi natura) are dissolved together (4.49-50), as argued in Book 3.

[DRAFT] (4.65-66) nam cur illa cadant ... hiscendist nulla potestas: this couplet recurs verbatim at 4.102-103, and the English repeats as well. hiscendist nulla potestas is an idiom for "there is no room to gape (to object)", meaning no one can open their mouth against the argument. The first appearance concerns surface films, the second mirror-images.

[DRAFT] (4.75-80) vela ... magnis intenta theatris: Roman theaters were shaded by coloured awnings stretched on masts (malos) and beams, so the light that passed through them tinted the crowd below. The audience (consessum, patrum matrumque) are the men and women in the seats, and the example shows colour being shed from the surface of a cloth.

[DRAFT] (4.102-103) Verbatim repeat of 4.65-66; see the note there. The English repeats the earlier lines word for word.

[DRAFT] (4.116) corum is the source's misprint for eorum ("of them"), meaning the tiny living creatures. The point is the Epicurean argument from minute animals: if a creature is so small that a third of it cannot be seen, its organs and the first-beginnings composing them must be smaller still.

[DRAFT] (4.125) panaces, habrotonum, centaurea: strong-smelling medicinal plants (all-heal, southernwood, centaury). "The panacea" in the English is the plant, not a general cure-all. absinthia taetra repeats the phrase from the honey-and-wormwood simile at 4.11.

[DRAFT] (4.126) The sentence breaks off at duobus and the source prints a lacuna before 4.127. The missing text presumably supplied the fingers rubbing the plant (perhaps digitis) and the point that its smell is carried by bodies too small to see. The translation prints the lacuna unnumbered and supplies nothing.

[DRAFT] (4.128) nulla vi cassaque sensu: the phrase can mean that the images are "without force" and also "bereft of sense", so that they are harmless and not themselves sentient. The translation takes cassaque sensu as "imperceptible to sense", and the translator may prefer to keep the wording closer to the Latin.

[DRAFT] (4.136) The source prints "aeraaëra", a doubling; the translation reads aëra (the Greek-form accusative of "air"). Gigantum refers to the Giants of myth, earth-born beings who fought the Olympian gods, whose faces the clouds seem to form.

[DRAFT] (4.144) The lacuna follows 144 and 145 resumes mid-argument: the lost lines must have introduced the surface-film account (the "summum quicquid", 145), and "semper enim" gives the reason for a claim now missing. The translation prints [lacuna] unnumbered and supplies nothing. Note also that 143-144 is a program ("Nunc ea… genantur") whose main verb is lost, so the English sentence is deliberately left hanging.

[DRAFT] (4.145) "Summum… de rebus" is the "outermost" film that things continually shed. This is the doctrine of the simulacra (here "images"), thin skins of atoms that keep the shape of their source. Epicurean doctrine holds that seeing occurs when these strike the eye.

[DRAFT] (4.150) The mirror argument explains reflection: the images cannot pass through a dense, smooth surface ("splendida… densaque… levor") and are not torn by it, so they rebound ("redundent", 154). Compare cloth (porous, passes them) and rock or wood (rough, shreds them).

[DRAFT] (4.163) "Ratione" (also 143, 191, 254) is rendered "principle" where it means the manner or mechanism of a process, in keeping with the register's "by what principle." It is not the doctrine "reasoning."

[DRAFT] (4.170) Acherunta ("Acheron"): the underworld river stands for the underworld itself, so the darkness of the storm is imagined as rising from Hades. Lucretius uses mythological imagery even as he denies its reality.

[DRAFT] (4.173) "Formidinis ora," the "faces of Terror," personifies fear as a figure with many faces, as the storm clouds seem to be. Compare the cloud-shapes of 4.136-140. The image echoes the "terror of the mind" that the proem says must be dispelled (1.146-148).

[DRAFT] (4.180) Lines 180-182 recur verbatim at 4.909-911, where they introduce the discussion of sleep. The English repeats them there word for word. The swan and the cranes belong to the Hellenistic tradition of the poet's modest "sweet" verse set against loud verbosity.

[DRAFT] (4.186) "E primis… minutis" renders "primis" as "first bodies" in keeping with the register's corpora prima, although the Latin has only the shorthand "primis." The "minute first bodies" are what make light and heat swift.

[DRAFT] (4.190) "Protelo": a technical term from ox-driving for a team yoked in tandem, each animal pulling the next in line; "stimulatur fulgere fulgur" ("flash goaded by flash") carries the image of one beam driving the next. The translation keeps the sense with "a team in tandem."

[DRAFT] (4.198) "Aeris intervallum": the manuscripts show "aeris," which the Perseus text normalizes as "aëris" in two places (187, 198). The translation reads the same word and gives "interval of air" both times, as a refrain.

[DRAFT] (4.206) "Quone… debere" is a rhetorical argument from the greater to the lesser: if the sun's light, which comes from deep inside, crosses the sky in a moment, images shed from the very surface must be faster. It argues from the faster to the slower.

[DRAFT] (4.211) The reflection of the stars in water is offered as proof ("specimen verum", 209) of the images' speed, since the stars appear in the water the instant it is set out. "Mundi" in 213 is rendered "of the world," so "stars of the world" means the stars of the whole cosmos.

[DRAFT] (4.220) "Exesor moerorum" ("eater of walls") describes the sea's spray as eroding walls along the shore, which shows that even such salty effluences are bodies leaving the sea. Lucretius uses it to argue that every kind of sensation depends on material streams.

[DRAFT] (4.224) "Absinthia" is wormwood, the bitter herb. Lucretius uses it again at 1.936-950, the famous honey-on-the-cup simile that returns at 4.11-25; the bitter taste here is a sensory example of effluence.

[DRAFT] (4.229) "Sentire sonare": "sense them sounding." Hearing, smell and sight are all treated as the reception of material streams, as the preceding examples have argued. The verse states compressed Epicurean doctrine that all perception is contact with bodies.

[DRAFT] (4.236) "Species" here is rendered "sight" in "fall upon our sight." The register gives "species" as "face, appearance, likeness"; the context of vision decides. The square shape felt in the dark is supposed to be known in the light only through its image.

[DRAFT] (4.246) The air between object and eye is pushed along ahead of the image and "sweeps the pupils," so that distance is judged by the quantity of air driven before it. This is the Epicurean account of depth perception.

[DRAFT] (4.269) The Latin has *imago* ("image") where the register fixes *simulacra* as "images" too; the translation lets both stand as "image(s)" here, so the reader should know that *imago* is the single reflected likeness (the mirror-picture) and *simulacra* the films that travel through air, which the passage treats as one stuff. Lucretius slides between them freely in this section (cf. 4.279 *imago*, 4.303 *simulacra*).

[DRAFT] (4.274) The "double, twin air" (*duplici geminoque… aere*) is the doctrine of the passage: seeing through an open door involves air on this side of the doorposts and air beyond, and the mirror image is likewise seen across two stretches of air, the one between us and the mirror and the one the image drives ahead of itself after reflection (cf. 4.291 *aeribus binis*). That is why the image appears set back behind the glass.

[DRAFT] (4.289) The source marks a lost line after 4.289; the sentence "it is right to wonder not at all" breaks off before its object, evidently "at the things seen in mirrors" or similar, which 4.290 resumes with *illis*. The translation prints the unnumbered [lacuna] and supplies nothing.

[DRAFT] (4.292) Here begins the explanation of lateral reversal: the image is not turned round intact but dashed back and pressed out in reverse, like a wet clay mask thrown against a beam (*cretea persona*, 4.297). The comparison is Lucretius's own and is physical rather than optical, since the image is a thin film that can be flattened and printed.

[DRAFT] (4.303) *Simulacra* is rendered "images" per the register (first fixed 1.123); these are the thin films of Epicurean theory, shed from the surfaces of things, not "idols" or mere pictures. The five or six images in a row (*quinque etiam aut sex*) are the reader's mirror-hall of repeated reflections.

[DRAFT] (4.322) *natura* is "nature" here, the agent that compels the rebound (*cogit natura referri*), so the angle of return is given as a law of nature's working. A modern reader may hear the law of reflection; that is the reader's association, not a claim of the poet's, who states only that images rebound at equal bends (*ad aequos… flexus*).

[DRAFT] (4.330) *semina ignis* ("seeds of fire") is the register's *semina rerum* applied to a particular stuff: bright light is made of fire-seeds which enter the eyes and cause pain. The passage treats colour and brightness as a matter of what actual bodies stream into the eye, a mechanism the verse states very briefly.

[DRAFT] (4.333) *arquati* are those with jaundice, called the "rainbow disease" (*arquatus morbus*, from *arcus*) for its colour. The Epicurean explanation is that seeds of the yellow-green sallowness (*luroris… semina*) stream from the sufferer's body and meet and tint the images coming to the eye, so that everything looks sallow. The translation renders the effect, "turn sallow", rather than the disease name.

[DRAFT] (4.337) Lucretius explains why we see into the light from darkness but not the reverse by the order in which two airs enter the pores of the eye: the thick, black air of gloom is driven out by the finer, swifter bright air that follows. The explanation depends on the doctrine of the passages (*vias*) in the eye, open or blocked by atoms of differing size and mobility.

[DRAFT] (4.361) *ad turnum* ("on a lathe") is a woodworker's image: the blunted angles of far towers look as if the stone structures had been turned round on a lathe. The verse is the poem's standard case of the senses reporting faithfully what reaches them (cf. 4.353–363), so the error is in the judgment, not the eye.

[DRAFT] (4.364) The shadow is explained as nothing but air deprived of light (*aer… lumine cassus*), and its apparent following of us as the ground being darkened and relit in succession. This is a case of what the Epicurean sense-doctrine takes as no illusion at all, but a real series of different patches of ground; the "shadow" does not follow us as one thing.

[DRAFT] (4.376) *quasi in ignem lana trahatur* ("as if wool were drawn into fire"): the simile of the instant destruction of light's first rays, which perish as wool is consumed on touching fire. The image supports the claim that new light constantly pours out and the old is annihilated, so the ground is stripped and refilled with ease.

[DRAFT] (4.379) The argument here is that the eyes report only light and shadow (lux atque umbra); judging whether a shadow is the same one moved, or a new one produced, belongs to the mind (animi ratio). This is the doctrine of the whole passage: error lies in the mind's added judgment, not in the sense. The refrain is Epicurean, "all sense-impressions are true."

[DRAFT] (4.384) "Reasoning of the mind" renders animi ratio, the mind's inferential faculty as opposed to the eyes. The register keeps "mind" for animus. A reader may know the term as "soul" or "rational soul," which the register forbids for anima and animus.

[DRAFT] (4.387) The ship illustration of relative motion is a commonplace of Greek sceptical and Epicurean argument; Lucretius uses it to show that the eyes accurately report the relative displacement and the mind misassigns which body moves. The stars (4.391) are the same case at large scale.

[DRAFT] (4.391) "Vaults of the ether" renders aetheriis cavernis, the sky imagined as a hollow dome to which the stars are fixed, which is the appearance being explained away here, not Lucretius's own cosmology. Epicurean doctrine left the nature of the heavenly bodies open to several explanations.

[DRAFT] (4.404) "Nature" (natura) is the subject of the dawn, which Lucretius personifies lightly; the English keeps the agent. The sun appears to touch the mountains, while the real distance includes seas and many lands (4.410-413), a stock of measurable absurdities used to show that apparent nearness is a failure of judgment, not of sight.

[DRAFT] (4.408) The distances are armaments metrics: "bowshots" (sagittae) and "casts of a javelin" (veruti), a rough Roman measure of range. The figures are deliberately absurdly small against the real distance.

[DRAFT] (4.414) The puddle between paving stones in a street (per strata viarum) is a Roman urban detail; the reflection offers a view of the sky "beneath the earth," a deliberate paradox of depth from a finger's depth of water.

[DRAFT] (4.426) The colonnade (porticus) whose parallel sides seem to converge into a cone is the classical argument from perspective, used in the Epicurean tradition. Lucretius adds the parallel flat-ceiling and floor detail (tecta solo iungens) to make the convergence vivid.

[DRAFT] (4.432) The sailors' sunset and the landsmen's broken oars (4.436) are paired: the first shows why the sailors' error is excusable, since they see only water and sky, and the sentence at 4.435 says that the senses are not therefore to be thought shaken in general (ne leviter credas labefactari undique sensus). The oar refraction at 4.438 is the standard case of an object appearing bent in water.

[DRAFT] (4.446) The stars seem to glide against moving clouds, but the clouds are what move; "true reasoning" renders vera ratione, again assigning the correction to the mind's reasoning, not to the eyes. The line lacks final punctuation in the Latin text followed.

[DRAFT] (4.447) The pressed eye gives double vision. Lucretius says it comes about "by a certain feeling" (quodam sensu), using the doubling as another case where the sense reports faithfully what the disturbed organ gives, and the error lies in the judgment.

[DRAFT] (4.453) The dream passage fits the doctrine of simulacra, the images that strike the mind in sleep, treated in full later in this book. Here it serves only as another case of apparent perception without a present object, and the mind adds belief (4.462-468).

[DRAFT] (4.469) The attack on the sceptic: one who says nothing can be known cannot know even that. The sceptical position is attributed to no named school, though a Roman reader would think of the Academy. "With his head where his footprints should be" (capite ipse suo in statuit vestigia) is a figure of absurd inversion, standing on one's head.

[DRAFT] (4.478) Lucretius gives the Epicurean account of the "criteria of truth": the notion of the true (notitiem veri) arises from the senses first, and reasoning (ratio) derives wholly from them, so if the senses were false all reasoning would be. This is the compressed core of the Epicurean canon.

[DRAFT] (4.491) Text uncertain: the transmitted videre ("to see") for the perception of soft, cold, and hot does not suit the argument, since those belong to touch and the sentence is about each sense having its own province. Editors emend or transpose; the translation follows the sense (perceived apart) without fixing a particular emendation.

[DRAFT] (4.499) The conclusion is a verse summary of the Epicurean position that every impression, at the time it occurs, is true (quod in quoquest his visum tempore, verumst), with each sense judged only within its own province and none able to correct another.

[DRAFT] (4.500-510) Lucretius answers the objection from the stick-in-water and tower illusions (Epicurean doctrine of 4.379ff.): if reasoning cannot explain why a square tower looks round at a distance, one must still not abandon the senses, since all inference rests on them (fidem primam, "first trust"). The "faulty causes" (mendose causas) are an acceptable fallback because they leave the evidence of sense intact.

[DRAFT] (4.513-521) The builder's rule, square and level (regula, norma, libella) is the analogy for the senses as the criterion of truth: a single false first measurement ruins the whole structure. Lucretius uses it again in the same spirit at 4.469-477; this is the Epicurean claim that if one sense is discredited, all reasoning built on it falls.

[DRAFT] (4.524-534) Voice is a body because it can strike the ear and scrape the throat; this is the Epicurean doctrine that anything able to act or be acted upon is corporeal (cf. 1.304ff., 1.443ff.). Here the "first-beginnings of voices" (primordia vocum, 4.531) are the same technical term as elsewhere. The placeholder at 534: Lucretius uses principia ("beginnings", 4.534, 4.543) and primordia (4.531, 4.544) interchangeably, and the English renders principia as "beginnings" to keep the Latin's two words distinct while not treating them as different things; this follows the register's rule that distinct Latin gets distinct English, with the merge acknowledged here.

[DRAFT] (4.545-546) The source prints barbita, "lyre", but a lyre (barbitos) does not "boom a hoarse drone" in the register Lucretius is contrasting with the trumpet; most editors emend to barbara, the "barbarous" (Phrygian, i.e. foreign) horn, and the translation follows that reading. The sense required is a contrast of low, rough sounds with the high, smooth song of the birds that follows.

[DRAFT] (4.547) "Daulian birds" (Dauliades... natae) are nightingales, after the myth in which Procne, daughter of the king of Daulis, was turned into a nightingale after avenging herself on Tereus; "gardens of Helicon" places them in the Muses' mountain. The line is textually disputed, and Bailey's reading has swans "from the torrents of Helicon"; the translation keeps the manuscript tradition's nightingales.

[DRAFT] (4.551) "Artificer" renders daedala (as at 1.7, daedala tellus), applied here to the tongue. The translator's note that most texts read verborum for nervorum cannot be confirmed from the printed Latin; the line as given has nervorum ("artificer with its sinews"), and the translation follows the source; the reading verborum would make it "artificer of words".

[DRAFT] (4.563-567) The crier (praeco) is the Roman public herald, who announced sales, proclamations and lost property to crowds. Lucretius's point is the divisibility of one voice among many ears without loss to each: each ear receives the whole word, because the voice "stamps" (obsignans) its form on each.

[DRAFT] (4.570-579) Echo is explained as voice thrown back from solid places (solidis... locis); the passage is a stock example of Epicurean explanation of marvels by mechanism. The claim that six or seven echoes were heard is Lucretius's own report (vidi, "I have seen").

[DRAFT] (4.580-594) Satyrs, Nymphs, Fauns and Pan are the rustic divinities and spirits of the Roman countryside, to whom the neighbors (finitimi) attribute echoes and night noises. Lucretius treats these as inventions, either to fill the lonely places with gods or from a love of telling and hearing tales (avidum auricularum); the Epicurean view is that the gods do not meddle in the world. Pan's reed pipe (fistula, calamos) is the syrinx of the myth.

[DRAFT] (4.599-602) Voices pass through winding openings (flexa foramina) while images (simulacra, the thin films of Epicurean vision, first introduced at 4.30ff.) must travel straight or are torn apart (perscinduntur). Glass (vitrei) is the example of a straight-pored body through which images pass, which is why we see through it.

[DRAFT] (4.605-606) The simile of the spark scattering into many fires (scintilla... in ignis) explains how one voice multiplies; the reader may hear a chain-reaction image, but that is an association and not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (4.615) Lucretius opens on taste: the mouth is treated as a place where "first-beginnings" of smooth or rough shape act on the sense, and the argument runs entirely on shape (levia/aspera). "Flavor" translates sucus, the juice pressed from food, not the sensation itself.

[DRAFT] (4.618) The sponge image (ceu plenam spongiam) shows how chewing wrings flavor out of food, as water is wrung from a sponge. It is the poet's only picture of the mechanism, and it carries the whole account of how taste reaches the palate.

[DRAFT] (4.638) The snake that dies of human spittle (serpens, hominis quae tacta salivis) is a piece of ancient natural lore, also found in Pliny. Lucretius uses it as the extreme case of one substance being food to one creature and poison to another.

[DRAFT] (4.640) Hellebore (veratrum) was a drug and poison for humans, yet goats and quails were said to eat it and fatten. Like the snake, it is an ancient commonplace used to prove that a substance's effect depends on the eater's make-up.

[DRAFT] (4.644) "Seeds" renders semina, the register's fixed rendering of Lucretius's word for the first bodies as they exist mixed in compound things. "That we said before" refers to the doctrine of mixed seeds in Book 2, where the same point is made for the variety of things.

[DRAFT] (4.650) "Pores" renders foramina, with the gloss quae perhibemus ("which we call"). This marks it as a term Lucretius is adopting for the passages in the body that match the shapes of the seeds, and the argument that follows depends on that matching.

[DRAFT] (4.653) The shapes listed here (triquetra, quadrata, rutunda, multangula) give the pores their geometry. They are Lucretius's own shorthand for the variety of atomic shapes argued in Book 2, applied now to the passages in living bodies.

[DRAFT] (4.662) "Rough and hooked bodies" (aspera … hamataque) are the shapes Lucretius assigns to what tastes bitter. The same smooth and hooked contrast carries his account of sweet and bitter in Book 2, so the reader should take it as a standing doctrine, not a new one.

[DRAFT] (4.667) "First-beginnings" renders principiorum, in the plural and without prima or rerum. It is the register's primordia cluster, here used in the sense of the atoms whose arrangement (positurae) changes in fever, so that the same honey tastes bitter to the sick.

[DRAFT] (4.671) "Honey" is Lucretius's standard example of a thing that is a mixture of smooth and rough bodies. The phrase "a thing we have often shown you above already" points back to the earlier passages on mixed seeds, including the honey-and-bitter-wormwood image in Book 1.

[DRAFT] (4.683) The sacred geese of Juno on the Capitol woke the defenders when the Gauls attempted a night attack on the citadel (traditionally 390 BC; Livy 5.47). Lucretius calls the goose the "savior of the citadel" (arcis servator), which gives the notice of a human smell an unexpected patriotic dignity.

[DRAFT] (4.693) Odor is said to be "torn apart" (distractus) into the air because its first-beginnings are larger and more loosely held than those of voice or light. That is why it travels slowly and weakly; the later argument about walls of stone (4.699) rests on the same size difference.

[DRAFT] (4.713) The belief that lions fear the cock is widespread in ancient writers (compare Pliny, Natural History 8.52 and 10.47). Lucretius takes it as a fact needing explanation, and his answer is that seeds from the cock's body strike the lion's eyes painfully, while in human eyes they either fail to enter or pass straight through.

[DRAFT] (4.724) "Images" renders simulacra, the register's fixed term for the thin films that stream off things. The reader may know them as "idols" or "eidola"; the poem uses the same word for what strikes the eye and, here, for the finer ones that act on the mind.

[DRAFT] (4.727) The spider's web and gold leaf (aranea bratteaque auri) are the comparisons for how thin the images are. They join in the air easily, which the later Centaur argument needs.

[DRAFT] (4.731) "Mind" renders animi naturam, and the passage turns on the claim that the mind's substance is so fine that images too thin for the eye still move it. The reader who knows Book 3 will recall that animus is made of the smallest and roundest atoms.

[DRAFT] (4.732) Centaurs, Scyllas and Cerberus are the stock monsters of myth. Lucretius uses them to show that dream and imagination can present things that never existed, and his explanation is that images of real things combine in the air.

[DRAFT] (4.734) This line repeats 1.135 verbatim in the Latin, where it names the images of the dead that haunt Ennius's account. The English repeats its own line from there, word for word; the first occurrence also introduces Ennius's vision of Homer, which the reader may wish to recall.

[DRAFT] (4.739) The Centaur has no living original (nulla fuit … natura animata), so its image cannot have come off a real body. It arises when the images of horse and man meet by chance and cling together, "as we said before," so the monster is a composite assembled in the air and not an emanation from any one thing.

[DRAFT] (4.742) "As we said before" (quod diximus ante) refers back to the account of how images join in the air in Book 4's earlier part. The same appeal to prior argument recurs at 4.746 (ut prius ostendi).

[DRAFT] (4.758) "Mind" appears here as mens animi, a compound in which Lucretius joins the two words. The register keeps mens and animus apart, but here mens animi is rendered "the thought of the mind," following the policy that mens is non-technical outside the Book 3 contrast.

[DRAFT] (4.761) The sleeper "sees" a dead person because images of him still circulate, and the senses are inactive to refute the vision (4.762-767). The idea that the dead appear in dreams is an old one, and Lucretius uses it to give a natural cause for the belief in ghosts.

[DRAFT] (4.768) "For the rest" renders quod super est, the register's transitional formula, and here it opens the explanation of why dream-images seem to move and dance. The explanation is that a rapid succession of slightly different images produces the appearance of movement.

[DRAFT] (4.776) The explanation of movement by rapid replacement of images (tanta est mobilitas) may remind a modern reader of a film's succession of still frames. That is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (4.777-778) Lucretius opens a new run of problems about vision and thought with a stock formula of promise ("multa... quaeruntur"). The questions that follow are Epicurean: how the mind thinks of exactly what it wants, how dream-images can dance in order, and why the mind sees only what it attends to. All are answered from the doctrine of simulacra, called "images" here as elsewhere in the register.

[DRAFT] (4.781) "Simulacra" is rendered "images", the film-like effluences from objects that Book 4 makes the cause of all sight and of thought. The question asks whether the images stand ready, as guards ("tuentur"), to meet each wish. The poem soon answers that thought is an act of attention, not of command.

[DRAFT] (4.785) "Omnia sub verbone creat natura": "verbone" is the transmitted form, an old by-form of "verbo", and the translation reads it as "at a word". The phrase pictures nature as an obedient servant, which Lucretius means to be absurd.

[DRAFT] (4.788-793) Dreams of dancing figures ("in numerum procedere", "ludos") are taken from the passage Lucretius gave in 4.768-776. The "docta" (taught) images are mocked as having been trained like dancers, and the passage then gives the real answer, which is the speed of the images.

[DRAFT] (4.794-799) The doctrine compressed here is that perceived time is made of many unperceived moments ("tempora multa latent, ratio quae comperit esse"). In each of these a fresh image can arrive, so the mind seems to be served at once. "Ratio" here is the reasoning that finds what the senses cannot, as in the register.

[DRAFT] (4.799) Repeats 4.774 verbatim: "tanta est mobilitas et rerum copia tanta". Here "rerum" means the images (the "store of things"), not things in general.

[DRAFT] (4.800-801) These lines repeat 4.771-772 nearly word for word. Their sense fits the dream-dance, where a succession of slightly different images looks like one figure changing its posture ("gestum mutasse"), and the translation matches the earlier lines. The repetition is Lucretius's own and is probably an intrusion from 4.771-772 here.

[DRAFT] (4.802-806) The mind (animus) sees only what it has prepared itself for ("quae ex sese ipse paravit"). The images are too fine to be seen unless the mind strains toward them ("contendit"). This attention is Lucretius's account of why thought follows desire without any image being summoned.

[DRAFT] (4.808) This line repeats 4.804 ("praeterea pereunt, nisi quae ex se ipse paravit") and interrupts the sentence about the eyes, which breaks off with no main verb. Editors generally treat it as an intrusion from 4.804, or at least as damaged, and the translation keeps the line as transmitted so that the break remains visible.

[DRAFT] (4.823) The text is corrupt in "vehementer ainesse inesse" (the transmitted "inesse" does not parse as it stands). The usual emendation, from "ainesse" read as "ainesseäinesse", gives "I strongly want you to flee this fault", which the translation follows.

[DRAFT] (4.823-842) This is the famous argument against teleology: eyes were not made for seeing nor legs for walking, since each organ existed before its use and use arose from it ("quod natumst id procreat usum", 4.835). A modern reader may associate it with later anti-purposive accounts of living things, but that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (4.832-833) "Perversa praepostera... ratione" is a tight phrase: the reasoning is "backward" (literally "put last what is first") and "turned upside down". The translation gives both words their force and keeps the Latin's rhetorical blame.

[DRAFT] (4.843-850) The examples (fighting and wounds, rest and beds, thirst and cups) are meant to show that the need and the behavior precede the invention. "Nature compelled" ("natura coegit") marks the instinct, as against art ("per artem") in the shield.

[DRAFT] (4.853-857) Lucretius divides things into those devised from use ("ex usu... reperta") and those that gave us the notion of use after they had been born. The senses and limbs are of the second kind, so he rejects the explanation "utilitatis ob officium". The same argument closes with a refrain-like "etiam atque etiam", which marks his insistence.

[DRAFT] (4.858-876) Hunger and thirst are explained by the doctrine of effluences: bodies ("corpora") stream from creatures, and food replaces them. Lucretius refers back to this ("docui", 4.860) without a book citation; it is the doctrine that things constantly flow off and are replaced, taught in the earlier books.

[DRAFT] (4.877-880) This announces the account of voluntary movement, which Lucretius has promised ("dicam"). The address "tu percipe dicta" turns again to Memmius, who is addressed as "you" throughout.

[DRAFT] (4.881-891) The chain is: an image of walking strikes the mind (animus), the mind strikes the spirit (anima) spread through the body, and the spirit moves the body. The register keeps "mind" for animus and "spirit" for anima, and the distinction matters here, since the two are joined ("coniuncta") in a single causal chain.

[DRAFT] (4.892-897) Air entering the loosened body is the second cause, so the body moves under two forces, like a ship driven by sails and wind ("velis ventoque"). The simile is Lucretius's own, with the two forces matching sail and wind.

[DRAFT] (4.898-906) The final comparisons answer the worry that tiny bodies (corpuscula) cannot move a great one. Wind, a single rudder, and a machine with pulleys and treadwheels ("trocleas et tympana") all show a small force moving a large weight. "Tympana" are treadwheels or drums in the Roman lifting machine, a crane worked by men walking inside a wheel.

[DRAFT] (4.907) Opening of the sleep section. The swan and crane comparison (parvus ut est cycni melior canor, ille gruum quam clamor) is a recycled proem-style apology for brevity: Lucretius promises few sweet verses rather than many. The same swan/crane contrast appears at 3.6-7 (hirundo and cycni, equine and kids), and the poet's self-presentation as a swan recurs there.

[DRAFT] (4.912) The address to Memmius resumes (tu mihi da tenuis auris animumque sagacem). "Fine ears" is the register's rendering of tenuis auris, meaning delicate, discriminating hearing. The plea anticipates that the reader will reject the account as impossible (ne fieri negites), a worry Lucretius voices elsewhere when a doctrine seems incredible.

[DRAFT] (4.916) "Spirit" (vis animae) is the register's fixed rendering of anima, the vital principle spread through the body, in contrast to animus, the mind, seated in the breast. On the Epicurean account sleep is a partial displacement of the anima: some is expelled and some is pushed deep inside, while enough stays to keep the body alive (4.920-928, with the buried-fire image, cinere obrutus ignis).

[DRAFT] (4.943) "First-beginnings" (principiorum) is the register's term for the atoms, here called by the alternative name that the register fixes as primordia, and this is the first time the segment uses it. It is paired with the "first parts and first elements" at 4.941 (primas partis elementaque prima), so the blows of the air are said to reach down to the atomic level of both body and mind.

[DRAFT] (4.970) Patriis chartis means "the pages of our native tongue," i.e. Latin, as against the Greek in which Epicurus wrote. The line is a rare moment in which Lucretius shows himself in his own dream-life, and it matches his complaint about the poverty of Latin (patrii sermonis egestas, 1.832, 3.260).

[DRAFT] (4.999) The text is disordered here. In the printed order, the puppies at 998-999 are followed by a repetition of 992-995 at 1000-1003, and 1004 ends with the dogs looking at "unfamiliar faces." Editors take 1000-1003 as a displaced duplicate of 992-995, and 999 as the broken end of a lost line about the puppies; the translation follows the printed order and does not repair it.

[DRAFT] (4.1029) Babylonica refers to costly embroidered Babylonian coverlets, a stock luxury item in Roman writing. The detail makes the dreamer's bed-wetting incongruous: he dreams of relieving himself at a pool or beside jars (dolia curta), and drenches expensive bedding.

[DRAFT] (4.1030) Here the argument turns from general dreams to adolescent wet dreams, which Lucretius explains by the doctrine of simulacra, rendered in the register as "images" (simulacra, 4.1032). The images of a beautiful face and coloring reach the seed-swollen parts of a youth at puberty, and in sleep act as if the act itself had happened (transactis omnibus rebus). This passage opens the longer treatment of love and desire that follows in the rest of Book 4.

[DRAFT] (4.1037) The "seed of which we spoke before" refers to the account of the generation of seed in the body at the opening of this section (the transition from the boy's first stirrings, 4.1030ff.). Here Lucretius begins the physiology of desire proper: seed ripens with age, is stirred by the sight of the beloved's simulacra, and is drawn toward its source of excitation.

[DRAFT] (4.1047) The line closely echoes 4.1034 (incitat inritans loca turgida semine multo); here it follows 4.1045-1046 awkwardly, repeating "swollen with seed" and introducing a second verb of driving on where the sentence has already reached its goal. Some editors bracket or transpose it; the translation keeps it in the source order and simply renders it, so the repetition shows.

[DRAFT] (4.1049) The comparison with a wound (vulnus) develops the image of 4.1048 (saucia amore): as blood spurts toward the injury and toward the striker, so the seed rushes toward the person who struck with the "weapons of Venus" (Veneris telis). The ictus language belongs to the Roman poetic tradition of love as a wound or dart, which Lucretius takes in a literal physiological sense.

[DRAFT] (4.1058) Venus here is the sexual drive itself, and "Love" (Amor) is its name, derived (hinc) from the physiology just described: in the Epicurean reading the gods Venus and Cupid are not persons but names for a bodily process. The drop (gutta) of Venus' sweetness is the first stirring, and "chill care" (frigida cura) follows, the sorrow the poet goes on to describe.

[DRAFT] (4.1061) Simulacra (images) are the Epicurean films of atoms shed from the surface of things and carried through the air to the eyes and mind. The beloved's image is thus present to the lover even when the beloved is absent, and the same name-word that keeps hovering about the ears (nomen dulce obversatur) keeps desire alive. This explains why Lucretius advises fleeing them (fugitare decet simulacra).

[DRAFT] (4.1071) The "wandering Venus" (volgivaga Venus) is the Venus of the street, casual or bought love; the doubled "wandering" (volgivaga ... vagus) is deliberate, and the translation keeps it. The advice is therapeutic rather than moralizing: discharge the seed into any body, so that it does not fix on one (4.1065-1066).

[DRAFT] (4.1073) Lucretius's argument that the man who avoids love loses nothing of Venus' fruit rests on Epicurus's distinction between pleasure and the pain that often accompanies it; the "pure" pleasure (purast ... voluptas) is that of the sane man, who takes the advantages without the penalty (sine poena). This is Epicurean hedonism applied to sex, not an ascetic rejection of it.

[DRAFT] (4.1088) The sense is that nature "fights back" (repugnat): love alone intensifies with satisfaction, unlike hunger and thirst, which are filled because food and drink enter the body and occupy fixed parts (certas ... partis, 4.1092). The doctrine behind it is that the image-films of the beloved never become the stuff of the body, so they can feed no real need and only inflame it.

[DRAFT] (4.1097) The simile of the thirsty dreamer who drinks in his sleep and still thirsts (bibere in somnis sitiens) is a standard figure for frustrated desire, and here it makes the point that in love one chases only simulacra (laticum simulacra). The translation renders the paradox of thirsting in the middle of a river (in medioque ... torrenti flumine) literally.

[DRAFT] (4.1107) The "woman's fields" (muliebria ... arva) belong to the old agricultural metaphor of marriage and procreation as sowing and ploughing, found throughout Latin and Greek poetry; conserat is the verb of sowing. The translation keeps the image and does not explain it away.

[DRAFT] (4.1124) Babylonian perfumes (Babylonia ... unguenta), Sicyonian shoes (Sicyonia), emeralds, Alindan and Cean fabrics: the list catalogues the luxury goods of the Roman demimonde, and each geographical adjective stands for a specific fashionable import. The shoes "laugh" (rident) in the sense of gleam, and are on her feet, not his, so the spender is bankrupting himself on a mistress's finery.

[DRAFT] (4.1129) The fathers' "well-gotten wealth" (bene parta patrum) is the family patrimony squandered; the anademata (Greek headbands) and mitrae (turbans) are named in Greek or Greek-derived words because they are the foreign luxuries of a courtesan, whose finery Lucretius mocks. The translation gives them in English.

[DRAFT] (4.1141) "Love that is true and at its most prosperous" (in amore ... proprio summeque secundo) renders the Latin literally: even in happy love the bitterness rises from the "fountain of delights" (4.1133), and unhappy love (in adverso atque inopi) is worse. The point is that the evils are inseparable from the passion, not accidents of failure.

[DRAFT] (4.1145) "By the principle I have taught" (qua docui ratione) points back to the earlier advice to fly the images and turn the mind elsewhere (4.1063-1072) and, more broadly, to the whole reasoning of the book. This is the register's "ratio" used instrumentally.

[DRAFT] (4.1148) The "nets" and "knots" (retibus ... nodos) belong to the hunting and fishing imagery of love as a snare, which runs from Greek epigram through Roman elegy. The translation keeps the physical image and does not tone it down.

[DRAFT] (4.1160) The catalogue of pet names that follows (4.1160-1169) is a famous passage, imitated by Ovid (Ars Amatoria 2.657-662) and Molière. In the Latin, many of the euphemisms are Greek words transliterated (melichrus, acosmos, Palladium, dorcas, chariton mia, cataplexis, traulizi, ischnon eromenion, rhadine, philema), a mark of the lovers' affectation; the translation puts them into English and in quotation marks. The text here is also disputed in places, and some lines show corruption.

[DRAFT] (4.1168) "Ceres herself, nursing Iacchus" (Ceres ... ab Iaccho): Iacchus is the child-god of the Eleusinian mysteries, associated with Demeter (Ceres) and Dionysus; here a big-breasted woman is called Ceres for her fullness. The reading ab Iaccho is uncertain, and the translation follows the source text as given; the line is a comic compression of the epithet.

[DRAFT] (4.1169) The "she-Silenus" and "satyress" (Silena ac Satura) are female versions of Silenus and satyrs, the snub-nosed, thick-lipped followers of Dionysus, so the flattering names for a snub-nosed woman are a joke at her expense. "Kiss" (philema) is Greek for a kiss.

[DRAFT] (4.1186) The "backstage parts" (postscaenia) are the area behind the stage of a Roman theater, here a figure for the unglamorous routines (perfume, hygiene, etc.) that women hide from the men they want to keep. The translation renders the theatrical word literally.

[DRAFT] (4.1191) The closing "make allowance for human things" (humanis concedere rebus) is a mild and tolerant note after the satire: the poet recommends that a woman of good heart be forgiven her imperfections, in return, so that love becomes a livable arrangement rather than a madness. This line ends the section on love and passes to the end of Book 4.

[DRAFT] (4.1192) Lucretius is arguing against the idea that female pleasure is always feigned. The woman who "seeks joys in common" (communia quaerens gaudia) anticipates the point he states outright at 1208, that pleasure in love is shared. The passage returns to the question of mutual pleasure that began at 4.1192.

[DRAFT] (4.1199) The doctrine is that animals mount and submit only because each feels desire of its own (ipsa... ardet abundans natura), so mutual pleasure is the cause of mating. "Venus" here stands for the act of mating itself, not the goddess, and the translation keeps the name as Lucretius uses it.

[DRAFT] (4.1203) The dogs caught at the crossroads (in triviis) are an observed, rather crude, proof of mutual pleasure. Lucretius reads their refusal to pull free as evidence that they "know" shared joys (mutua gaudia nossent). The crossroads are where dogs gather, and Trivia (Diana, goddess of crossroads) is named elsewhere in the poem (1.84).

[DRAFT] (4.1209) Here begins Lucretius's account of heredity. In his view both parents contribute seed (semen), and the child resembles whichever seed prevails, or both when neither does. The seed of the mother is a real contributor to the child, which was a contested position, since Aristotle gave the female only matter, not seed.

[DRAFT] (4.1220) Resemblance to grandparents and great-grandparents is explained by first-beginnings (primordia) hidden in the parents' bodies and handed down along the line (a stirpe). This is the first occurrence in this segment of the register term primordia, "first-beginnings", the Epicurean atoms. A modern reader may hear an anticipation of recessive inheritance. That is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (4.1233) Lucretius turns on the belief that the gods can withhold fertility, and he denies it. The sacrifices and prayers at altars (aras, altaria) and the consultation of oracles (sortis) are useless, since the causes of barrenness are physical. This is the argument of the proem again: religio, here the practice of appeasing gods, is shown to be pointless.

[DRAFT] (4.1240) Barrenness is explained by the consistency of the seed, too thick (crasso) or too thin (liquido). The thin seed cannot adhere and is expelled in miscarriage (abortu), and the thick seed cannot travel far or mix with the woman's seed. Each is a mechanical failure of the seed's "stroke" and fit, with no divine agency involved.

[DRAFT] (4.1248) Harmoniae is Greek (ἁρμονίαι), the fittings or attunements of two bodies. Here it names the compatibility of the pair, whose seeds either fit or do not, and the translator's cross-reference to 3.100-135 concerns Lucretius's rejection of the "harmony" theory of the soul. The word is used there in a different sense, so the cross-reference is better checked than assumed. The translation could keep "harmonies" and note that the Latin uses the Greek word.

[DRAFT] (4.1268) The advice not to move wantonly (molles motus) is aimed at wives, and the harlots (scorta) at 1274 are the contrast, who move to avoid conception. The furrow and ploughshare image (sulcum... vomeris) is a standing figure for intercourse and sowing in Latin, and in the poem the sowing metaphor is used also at 4.1233, satum.

[DRAFT] (4.1278) Lucretius denies that love for a plain woman is caused by divine intervention (divinitus), including the "arrows of Venus", that is, the darts a god or Cupid would shoot. He gives instead her good manners and care of herself, and then habit (consuetudo). The translation renders muliercula as "a little woman" to keep the diminutive, which is mildly dismissive.

[DRAFT] (4.1286) The dripping water that wears through stone (guttas... pertundere saxa) is a favorite image of Lucretius and recurs at 1.313, where it is applied to the wearing away of a stone by water. The comparison here is of habit slowly overcoming resistance in love.
