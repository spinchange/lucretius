    2  were given out long ago by Athens of illustrious name;
    3  she it was who renewed life and enacted laws,
    4  and first gave sweet consolations for life
    5  when she bore a man found to have such a heart,
    6  who once poured out all things from his truth-telling lips;
    7  whose glory, though he is extinguished, for divine discoveries
    8  is spread abroad and, old already, carried up to heaven.
    9  For when he saw that nearly all that need demands for living
   10  was by now made ready for mortals,
   11  and that life, as far as could be, was set on a safe footing,
   12  that men, powerful, abounded in riches and honor and praise,
   13  and stood out in the good name of their children,
   14  and yet at home the heart in each was no less anxious,
   15  and harried life, against the mind's own will, without any
   16  pause, and were compelled to rage in hostile complaints,
   17  he understood that it was the vessel itself that made the fault,
   18  and that by its fault everything was corrupted within,
   19  whatever was gathered from outside and came in, however good;
   20  partly because he saw that it was leaking and full of holes,
   21  so that by no means could it ever be filled;
   22  partly because he saw it fouled, as it were, with a loathsome taste
   23  everything it had taken in, whatever it was, within.
   24  Therefore with truth-telling words he purged men's breasts,
   25  and set a limit to desire and to fear,
   26  and set forth the highest good, toward which we all strain,
   27  what it was, and pointed out the road by which, on a narrow track,
   28  we could press on toward it on a straight course,
   29  and what evil there was everywhere in mortal affairs,
   30  coming about and flying abroad in various forms, whether
   31  by natural chance or force, since nature had so arranged it,
   32  and from what gates it was fitting to go out to meet each;
   33  and he proved that the human race, for the most part in vain,
   34  rolls the gloomy waves of cares within its breast.
   35  For just as children tremble and fear everything
   36  in blind darkness, so we in the light are afraid

   54  The other things that on earth and in heaven are seen to happen
   55  by mortals, when often they hang with fearful minds,
   56  make their minds lowly with dread of the gods,
   57  and press them down, flattened to the earth, because
   58  ignorance of causes compels them to assign things
   59  to the command of the gods and to yield them the kingdom.
   60  The causes of whose workings they can in no way see,
   61  and they suppose them to be done by divine power.
   62  For those who have learned well that the gods lead a life without care,
   63  if yet meanwhile they wonder by what principle
   64  each thing can be carried on, above all in those things
   65  that are seen overhead in the shores of the ether,
   66  are carried back again into the old religions
   67  and take on harsh masters, whom the wretches believe
   68  can do all things, ignorant of what can come to be
   69  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded,
   70  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep;
   71  and so all the more they wander, carried along by blind reasoning.
   72  Unless you spit these things out of your mind and cast far from you
   73  thinking what is unworthy of the gods and alien to their peace,
   74  the holy powers of the gods, diminished by you, will often
   75  harm you; not that the gods' highest power can be violated
   76  so as to thirst in anger to exact harsh penalties,
   77  but because you yourself will picture those who rest in tranquil peace
   78  as rolling great waves of anger,
   79  nor will you approach the shrines of the gods with a calm breast,
   80  nor the images that are carried from their holy body
   81  into the minds of men, heralds of the divine form,
   82  will you have strength to take in with tranquil peace of mind.
   83  From this you can see what kind of life must follow.
   84  And that truest reasoning may drive this far from us,
   85  though much has already gone forth from me,
   86  yet much remains, and must be adorned with polished
   87  verses; the order of heaven and of fire must be grasped,
   88  storms and the bright thunderbolts must be sung,
   89  what they do and from what cause they are carried, whatever it be,
   90  lest you tremble, out of your wits, when heaven's parts are divided,
   91  from what quarter the flying fire has come, or into which half
   92  it has turned from here, in what way through walled-in places
   93  it has slipped in, and how, having held sway, it has carried itself out.
   94  The causes of whose workings they can in no way see,
   95  and they suppose them to be done by divine power.
   96  First, the blue expanses of heaven are shaken by thunder
   97  because, flying on high, there clash together
   98  the clouds of the ether, when the winds fight from opposite sides.
   99  For no sound comes from a clear part of the sky,
  100  but wherever the clouds are massed in a denser column,
  101  from there, so much the more, a roar often comes with great rumbling.
  102  Further, clouds cannot be of so dense a body
  103  as stones and timbers are, nor again
  104  so thin as are the mists and the flying smoke;
  105  for either they would have to fall, pressed down by brute weight
  106  like stones, or, like smoke, they could not hold together
  107  or keep in the icy snows and the showers of hail.
  108  They give out a sound, too, above the levels of the wide world,
  109  as at times the canvas stretched over great theaters
  110  gives a crackle, tossed about among the poles and beams,
  111  and sometimes, torn through, runs wild in the boisterous breezes
  112  and imitates the brittle sound of papers;
  113  for this kind too you may recognize in thunder,
  114  or when hanging clothes and flying papers
  115  are twisted and beaten through the air by the lashes of the wind.
  116  For it happens too at times that clouds cannot so much collide
  117  head on as pass along each other's flank
  118  with contrary motion, grazing their bodies slowly,
  119  whence that dry sound brushes the ears and is long
  120  drawn out, until they have come out of the narrow regions.

  121  In this way too all things, shaken by heavy thunder, are often
  122  seen to tremble, and, suddenly torn apart,
  123  the vast walls of the capacious world seem to have leapt asunder,
  124  when all at once a gathered storm of strong wind
  125  has twisted itself into the clouds and, shut in there,
  126  with its whirling eddy more and more on every side
  127  compels the cloud to grow hollow, its body thickened all around;
  128  afterwards, when the wind's force and keen onset have broken it,
  129  then, split, it gives a crash with a terrifying crackling sound.
  130  Nor is this a wonder, when a little bladder full of air
  131  often gives no little sound when it is suddenly burst.
  132  There is also a way, when winds blow through the clouds,
  133  for them to make sounds; for indeed we see clouds, branching
  134  and rough, often borne along in many ways;
  135  just as, when the blasts of the northwest wind blow through a dense wood,
  136  the leaves give a sound and the branches a crash.
  137  It happens too at times that the roused force of a strong wind
  138  tears through the cloud, breaking it with a straight onset;
  139  for what a blast can do there the plain fact shows us
  140  here, where it is gentler, when even so on earth it overturns
  141  tall trees, wrenching them out from their deepest roots.
  142  There are waves too through the clouds, which give a kind of murmur
  143  heavily, as they break; as happens likewise in deep
  144  rivers and on the great sea, when the surf is broken.
  145  It happens too when from cloud into cloud the burning force
  146  of the thunderbolt falls; if this cloud by chance receives the fire
  147  in much moisture, it slaughters it at once with a great outcry;
  148  as iron glowing white from the hot furnaces at times
  149  hisses, when we have plunged it into icy water close by.
  150  Again, if a drier cloud receives the fire,
  151  it is kindled all at once and burns with a huge sound,
  152  as if flame were to roam through mountains leafy with laurel,
  153  burning them up with great onset in a whirlwind of winds;
  154  nor does anything, more than the Delphic laurel of Phoebus,
  155  burn with terrible sound in the crackling flame.
  156  Finally, often a great crashing of ice and a downfall
  157  of hail gives a sound high up in great clouds;
  158  for when the wind packs them, there are broken, crammed into narrow space,
  159  the frozen mountains of storm-cloud, mixed with hail.
  160  It lightens likewise when the clouds by collision have struck out
  161  many seeds of fire, as if stone should strike
  162  on stone, or on iron; for then too a light
  163  leaps out and the fire scatters its bright sparks.
  164  But it happens that we receive the thunder with our ears after
  165  the eyes see the lightning, because always to the ears
  166  things arrive more slowly than the things that move the sight.
  167  You may learn this from this too: if you see someone
  168  far off cutting down the growth of a tree with a double-edged axe,
  169  it happens that you see the stroke before the blow through the ears
  170  gives its sound; so too we see the lightning before
  171  we receive the thunder, which is sent out together with the fire
  172  from a like cause, born of the same collision.
  173  In this way too clouds dye places with swift light
  174  and the storm lightens with a trembling onset.
  175  When wind has invaded a cloud and, turning about there,
  176  has made the cloud, as I showed before, grow hollow and thick,
  177  it grows hot by its own mobility; as you see all things,
  178  heated through by motion, take fire; indeed a leaden
  179  sling-bullet, whirled along a long course, even melts.
  180  Therefore when this wind, hot, has cut through the black cloud,
  181  it scatters the seeds of heat, squeezed out as if by force all at once,
  182  which make the flickering lightnings of flame;
  183  then the sound follows, which reaches our ears more slowly
  184  than the things that arrive at the lights of our eyes.
  185  This happens, of course, when clouds are dense and at the same time
  186  piled up high, one upon another, with wondrous onset.
  187  Do not let it deceive you that we, below, see
  188  how broad they are rather than how high they stand piled up.
  189  For observe, when clouds that are made like mountains
  190  are carried crosswise through the air by the winds,
  191  or when you see them heaped along great mountains,
  192  one above another, and the higher pressing down,
  193  set at rest in their station, the winds buried on every side;
  194  then you will be able to recognize their great masses
  195  and to discern caverns built as if of hanging rocks,
  196  which, when a storm has risen, the winds
  197  have filled, and, shut in, they chafe with a great murmur
  198  in the clouds, and threaten like wild beasts in cages;
  199  now from here, now from there, they send roars through the clouds,
  200  and, seeking a way out, they turn about and roll together
  201  seeds of fire out of the clouds, and so drive together
  202  many, and whirl the flame within the hollow furnaces,
  203  until the cloud is torn apart and they have flashed out, gleaming.
  204  For this cause too it happens that the mobile
  205  golden color of liquid fire flies down to earth,
  206  because the clouds themselves must have very many seeds
  207  of fire; for indeed when they are without any moisture,
  208  their color is mostly flaming and bright.
  209  For indeed from the light of the sun they must
  210  take in much, so that with reason they glow red and pour out fires.
  211  When therefore the wind, driving these, has thrust them into one
  212  place and compressed them, crowding them, they pour out, squeezed,
  213  the seeds that make the colors of flame flash out.
  214  It lightens also when the clouds of heaven grow thin;
  215  for when the wind lightly draws them apart as they go
  216  and dissolves them, there must fall, against their will,
  217  the seeds that make the flash. Then without foul
  218  terror and sounds it lightens, and with no uproar.
  219  For the rest, with what nature thunderbolts are endowed
  220  is declared by their strokes, and by the burnt-in signs of heat
  221  and the marks breathing out heavy airs of sulphur;
  222  for these are signs of fire, not of wind or of rain.
  223  Further, they often set fire too to the roofs of houses
  224  and with swift flame are masters in the very dwellings.
  225  This fire, subtle above all other fires,
  226  nature has composed for you of tiny and mobile
  227  bodies, which nothing at all can withstand.
  228  For the strong thunderbolt passes through the walls of houses
  229  as shouts and voices do, passes through stones, through bronze,
  230  and in an instant of time makes bronze and gold liquid.
  231  Likewise it makes wine, though the jars are whole, suddenly
  232  vanish away, because, no doubt, easily all around
  233  it loosens and makes porous the sides of the jar,
  234  its heat as it comes, and, winding into the wine itself,
  235  dissolving nimbly, scatters the first-beginnings of the wine.
  236  And this the heat of the sun seems unable, in a lifetime,
  237  to accomplish, strong as it is with its flashing glow.
  238  So much more mobile and more masterful is this force.
  239  Now, by what means these are begotten, and are made with so great
  240  an onset that by their stroke they can split towers apart,
  241  overthrow houses, tear away beams and rafters,
  242  and heave up and shift the monuments of men,
  243  take the life from men, lay low the herds everywhere,
  244  by what force they can do all other things of this kind,
  245  I shall unfold, and not delay you longer with promises.

  246  Thunderbolts, we must suppose, are born from thick clouds
  247  heaped up on high; for from a clear sky none,
  248  nor from clouds of slight density, are ever sent.
  249  For beyond doubt the plain fact shows that this is so:
  250  that at such times the clouds grow dense through the whole air,
  251  so that we would think all the darkness on every side had left
  252  Acheron and filled the great caverns of the sky;
  253  so far, when the foul night of storm-clouds has risen,
  254  do faces of black terror hang over us from above,
  255  when the tempest begins to work up its thunderbolts.
  256  Further, very often a black storm-cloud out over the sea,
  257  like a river of pitch let down from the sky, falls
  258  upon the waves, crammed with darkness, far off, and drags
  259  a black tempest heavy with thunderbolts and gales,
  260  itself filled full, above all, with fires and winds,
  261  so that on land too men shudder and seek shelter.
  262  So, then, we must suppose that above our heads
  263  the tempest towers high; for they would not bury the lands
  264  in so great a gloom unless many clouds were built up
  265  on many others above, and the sun taken away;
  266  nor could they overwhelm the earth, coming, with so great a rain
  267  as to make the rivers overflow and the fields swim,
  268  if the upper air were not built high with heaped-up clouds.
  269  Here, then, all things are full of winds and fires;
  270  that is why on every side come roarings and flashes.
  271  For indeed I have shown above that the hollow clouds hold
  272  very many seeds of heat, and must take in many more
  273  from the rays of the sun and from their burning.
  274  When the same wind that drives them together into one
  275  place, whatever it may be, has pressed out many seeds
  276  of heat, and at once has mingled itself with that fire,
  277  a whirlwind, worked in there, spins in the narrow space
  278  and sharpens the thunderbolt in hot furnaces within;
  279  for it is kindled in two ways: it grows hot by its own
  280  mobility, and from its contact with the fire.
  281  Then, when the force of the wind has grown hot through and the heavy
  282  onset of fire has come in, the thunderbolt, ripe as it were,
  283  suddenly tears through the cloud, and the roused blaze is carried
  284  on, lighting up all places with its flashing lights.
  285  A heavy sound follows it, so that the precincts of heaven
  286  seem to burst apart all at once and crush us from above.
  287  Then a trembling heavily assails the lands, and rumblings
  288  run across the high sky; for at that time nearly the whole
  289  tempest is shaken and trembles, and roarings are set moving.
  290  From this shaking follows a heavy and abundant rain,
  291  so that the whole upper air seems to be turned to rain
  292  and so, rushing headlong, to bring back the flood;
  293  so great a downpour is sent by the rending of the cloud
  294  and the storm of the wind, when the sound flies out with the burning stroke.
  295  There are times, too, when the force of wind, roused from outside,
  296  falls upon a strong cloud that is ripe at its crest;
  297  and when it has torn through it, at once there falls that fiery
  298  whirl which by our native name we call the thunderbolt.
  299  The same happens in other parts, wherever the force has borne it.
  300  It happens too that at times the force of wind, sent without fire,
  301  nonetheless catches fire in the space of its long passage,
  302  while, as it comes, it sheds along its course certain bodies,
  303  large ones, which cannot pass through the air as readily,
  304  and, scraping up others from the air itself, carries
  305  tiny ones, which, mixed together, make fire as they fly;
  306  in a way not far different from how a lead
  307  bullet often grows hot in its course, when, shedding many
  308  bodies of cold, it has taken fire in the air.
  309  It happens too that the force of the blow itself rouses fire,
  310  when the force of wind, sent cold and without fire, has struck,
  311  doubtless because, when it has struck with a violent stroke,
  312  elements of heat can flow together out of the wind itself
  313  and at the same time out of the thing that then receives the stroke;
  314  as, when we strike a stone with iron, fire flies out,
  315  nor, because the force of iron is cold, do the seeds
  316  of hot brightness rush together any the less at the stroke.
  317  So, then, a thing must also be kindled by the thunderbolt
  318  if it happens to be suited and fit for flames.
  319  Nor can the force of the wind be wholly and utterly cold,
  320  that force which is sent from on high with so much power,
  321  but that, if it is not kindled with fire before, along its course,
  322  it still arrives warmed and mingled with heat.
  323  But the thunderbolt's mobility and heavy stroke come about,
  324  and thunderbolts commonly run their course with swift gliding,
  325  because the force itself, first roused, gathers itself wholly
  326  within the clouds and takes on a great effort for going,
  327  then, when the cloud can no longer hold the growing onset,
  328  the force is squeezed out and so flies with wondrous onset,
  329  like the missiles that fly, sent from mighty engines of war.
  330  Add that it is made of small and smooth elements,
  331  nor is it easy for anything to withstand such a nature;
  332  for it slips between and pierces through the loose-knit passages,
  333  and so is not held back by many collisions
  334  and delayed; for this reason it flies, gliding with swift onset.
  335  Then too, since by nature all weights strive
  336  downward, when a blow is added to this as well,
  337  the mobility is doubled and that onset grows heavier,
  338  so that more violently and more swiftly it shatters with blows
  339  whatever stands in its way and delays it, and keeps its path.
  340  Finally, since it comes with a long onset, it must take on
  341  mobility again and again, which grows as it goes
  342  and increases its strong powers and gives strength to its stroke;
  343  for it makes whatever seeds of it there are
  344  be carried all straight on, as it were into one place,
  345  throwing them all, as they roll, into that one course.
  346  Perhaps too, as it comes, it draws from the air itself certain
  347  bodies, which by their blows kindle its mobility.
  348  And it comes unharmed through things, and passes through many
  349  leaving them whole, since the liquid fire passes through their pores.
  350  And many it shatters, when the thunderbolt's own bodies
  351  have fallen on the bodies of things where their weave is held.
  352  Further, it easily dissolves bronze and at once makes gold
  353  boil, because its force is made finely of small
  354  bodies and out of smooth elements,
  355  which easily work their way in, and, once worked in, suddenly
  356  dissolve all knots and loosen the bonds.

  357  And in autumn above all the high house of heaven, bright with stars,
  358  is shaken on every side, and the whole earth with it,
  359  and when the flowering seasons of spring unfold themselves.
  360  For in the cold fires are lacking, and in the heat the winds
  361  fail, nor are the clouds then of so dense a body.
  362  When, therefore, the seasons of heaven stand between the two,
  363  then all the various causes of the thunderbolt run together.
  364  For the very strait of the year mixes cold with heat,
  365  and a cloud needs both of these for the forging of thunderbolts,
  366  so that there is discord among things, and with a great tumult
  367  the air, raging with fires and winds, heaves like a sea.
  368  For the first part of heat is the last part of cold;
  369  that is the season of spring; therefore unlike things
  370  must fight with one another and, once mixed, throw all into turmoil.
  371  And when the last heat, mixed with the first cold,
  372  rolls round, the season that goes by the name of autumn,
  373  here too the bitter winters clash with the summers.
  374  Therefore these must be named the straits of the year,
  375  nor is it a wonder if at that time the most thunderbolts
  376  come about, and a turbulent storm is stirred up in the sky,
  377  since it is troubled on both sides by a two-fronted war,
  378  on this side by flames, on that by winds and moisture mixed.
  379  This is to see through the very nature of the fire-bearing
  380  thunderbolt, and to see by what force it does each thing,
  381  not to unroll the Tyrrhenian chants backward, to no purpose,
  382  searching out the signs of the hidden mind of the gods:
  383  from what quarter the flying fire has come, or into which half
  384  it has turned from here, in what way through walled-in places
  385  it has slipped in, and how, having held sway, it has carried itself out,
  386  or what harm the stroke of the thunderbolt from heaven can do.
  387  But if Jupiter and the other gods shake the shining
  388  precincts of heaven with terrifying sound
  389  and hurl fire wherever each one has the will,
  390  why do they not make those who have some heedless, loathsome crime
  391  be struck, and breathe out the flames of lightning
  392  from a breast run through, a sharp lesson to mortals,
  393  and why instead does one conscious of nothing shameful
  394  roll in the flames, innocent, and find himself entangled,
  395  seized suddenly by the whirlwind and fire of heaven?
  396  Why, too, do they aim at empty places and labor in vain?
  397  Or are they then training their arms and hardening their muscles?
  398  And why do they let their father's weapon be blunted
  399  on the earth? Why does he himself allow it, not spare it for foes?
  400  Finally, why does Jupiter never, with the sky clear all round,
  401  hurl a thunderbolt on the earth and pour out its sounds?
  402  Or as soon as the clouds have come up, does he himself then
  403  go down into them, to aim his weapon's stroke from close at hand?
  404  By what principle, again, does he send it to sea? What charge
  405  has he against the waves, the liquid mass, the floating plains?
  406  Further, if he wants us to beware the thunderbolt's stroke,
  407  why does he hesitate to let us see it when it is sent?
  408  But if he wants to crush us with fire when we do not expect it,
  409  why does he thunder from that quarter, so that we can avoid it,
  410  why does he first stir up darkness and roarings and rumblings?
  411  And how could you believe that he sends it into many places
  412  at once? Or would you dare to argue that it has never happened
  413  that several strokes were made at one and the same time?
  414  But it has often happened, and must happen,
  415  that just as it rains and showers fall in many regions,
  416  so many thunderbolts come about at one time.
  417  Finally, why does he shatter the holy shrines of the gods
  418  and his own illustrious seats with hostile thunderbolt,
  419  and break the well-made images of the gods, and strip
  420  his own likenesses of their honor with a violent wound?
  421  And why does he mostly aim at high places, and why do we see
  422  the most traces of his fire on the tops of mountains?
  423  For the rest, it is easy to learn from these things
  424  how those which the Greeks, from what they are, named presters
  425  come down from above into the sea, by what principle.
  426  For it happens at times that a kind of column, let down
  427  from heaven, descends into the sea, and round it the waters
  428  boil, stirred up by heavily breathing blasts,
  429  and whatever ships are then caught in that tumult
  430  are harried and come into the utmost danger.
  431  This happens when at times the stirred-up force of the wind
  432  cannot burst the cloud it has begun on, but presses it down,
  433  so it is like a column let down from heaven into the sea,
  434  little by little, as if something were pushed by a fist
  435  and an arm's thrust from above, and stretched out into the waves;
  436  and when the wind has split it, from there its force bursts out
  437  into the sea and works a wondrous boiling in the waves;
  438  for a whirling eddy comes down and draws that cloud
  439  down together with it, a cloud of pliant body;
  440  and as soon as it has thrust it, heavy, down to the sea's levels,
  441  the eddy at once plunges whole into the water and stirs
  442  the whole sea with enormous sound, forcing it to boil.
  443  It happens too that the eddy of wind itself wraps itself
  444  in clouds, scraping together seeds of cloud from the air,
  445  and imitates a prester let down from heaven;
  446  when this has let itself down to the earth and broken up,
  447  it vomits out a monstrous force of whirlwind and storm.
  448  But because this happens only rarely, and the mountains must
  449  block it on land, the same thing is seen more often
  450  on the wide view of the sea and under the open sky.
  451  Clouds grow together when many bodies, flying
  452  up here in the space of heaven, have suddenly come together,
  453  rougher bodies, such as, though entangled in slight ways,
  454  can still be held together, pressed one upon another.
  455  These first make the small clouds take their shape;
  456  then these take hold of one another and flock together
  457  and by joining grow, and are carried by the winds,
  458  until at last a savage storm has risen.
  459  It happens too that mountain peaks, the nearer they are to heaven,
  460  the more they smoke, the higher they stand,
  461  continually, with the thick murk of tawny cloud,
  462  because, when the clouds first come to take shape,
  463  before the eyes can see them, thin as they are, the winds
  464  carry them and drive them together to the mountain's topmost peaks;
  465  only here, when a greater throng has gathered,
  466  and condensed, can they appear, and at the same time
  467  they seem to rise from the mountain's very top into the clear sky.
  468  For that places high up lie open to the winds
  469  the fact itself shows, and our senses, when we climb high mountains.

  470  Further, that from the whole sea too nature lifts very many
  471  bodies, clothes declare, when they are hung up on the shore
  472  and take into themselves a clinging of moisture.
  473  So it seems all the more that many bodies, to swell the clouds,
  474  can also rise up together from the salt surge of the ocean;
  475  for the principle of all moistures is of one blood.
  476  Further, from all the rivers, and at the same time from the earth
  477  itself, we see mists and steaming heat rise up,
  478  which are pressed out from there like a breath and so carried upward,
  479  and they suffuse the sky with their murk, and high
  480  clouds they furnish, little by little, by coming together;
  481  for the sign-bearing ether's heat presses on them from above too,
  482  and, as if by packing them close, weaves rain-cloud beneath the blue.
  483  It happens too that into this sky there come from outside those
  484  bodies that make clouds and flying storm-clouds;
  485  for I have taught that their number is past counting, and the sum
  486  of the deep infinite, and have shown with what mobility
  487  the bodies fly, and how in a moment
  488  they are used to passing across a space beyond telling.
  489  It is no wonder, therefore, if often in a short time
  490  a storm and darkness, with such mighty winds,
  491  cover seas and lands, looming over them from above,
  492  since on every side, through all the pores of the ether,
  493  and, as it were, through the great world's breathing-holes all around,
  494  a way out and a way in stands granted to the elements.
  495  Come now, how rainy moisture grows together in the high
  496  clouds, and how, sent down to the earth as a shower,
  497  it falls, I will unfold. First, I will prove that seeds of water
  498  rise up, many at once, together with the clouds themselves
  499  from all things, and that both grow alike in this way,
  500  both the clouds and the water, whatever there is in the clouds,
  501  just as in us the body grows alike together with the blood,
  502  and sweat too, and whatever moisture, finally, is in the limbs.
  503  Often, too, the clouds take in a great deal of the sea's
  504  moisture, just like hanging fleeces of wool,
  505  when the winds carry the clouds above the great sea.
  506  On a like principle from all the rivers moisture
  507  is lifted into the clouds. And when many seeds of water
  508  have come well together there in many ways, swelled from all sides,
  509  the crowded clouds strive to send out their moisture
  510  in two ways; for the wind's force thrusts them together, and the very
  511  abundance of storm-clouds, driven together in a greater throng,
  512  urges and presses from above and makes the showers flow out.
  513  Further, when the clouds too are thinned out by winds
  514  or dissolved, struck from above by the heat of the sun,
  515  they send down rainy moisture and drip, as wax set over
  516  a hot fire wastes away and melts in plenty.
  517  But a violent shower comes when violently, in both ways,
  518  the clouds are pressed, by their heaped force and by the onset of the wind.
  519  And rains are used to holding on a long time and lingering long
  520  when many seeds of water are set in motion,
  521  and clouds on clouds and drenching storm-clouds
  522  are borne on over them from every quarter everywhere,
  523  and when the whole earth, steaming, breathes its moisture back.
  524  Here, when the sun with its rays amid the dark storm
  525  has shone facing it, against the spray of the rain-clouds,
  526  then the color of the bow appears in the black clouds.
  527  The other things that grow on high and are created on high,
  528  and that grow together in the clouds, all of them, utterly
  529  all, snow, winds, hail, and the chill hoarfrosts,
  530  and the great force of ice, the great hardening of waters,
  531  and the check that everywhere reins in the eager rivers,
  532  it is still quite easy to discover and see in the mind,
  533  how all of them come about and why they are created,
  534  once you have learned well what is granted to the elements.
  535  Come now, by what principle the motions of the earth come about,
  536  learn. And first of all, be sure you think the earth to be,
  537  below as well as above, full on every side of windy
  538  caverns, and to carry many lakes and many pools
  539  in its lap, and cliffs and sheer-broken rocks;
  540  and many rivers hidden beneath the back of the earth
  541  you must suppose roll on with force their sunken waves, unseen;
  542  for the thing itself demands that it be like itself throughout.
  543  With these things, therefore, joined on and set beneath it,
  544  the earth trembles above, shaken by great collapses,
  545  when beneath it age has undermined huge caverns;
  546  indeed whole mountains fall, and from the great and sudden
  547  shock the tremors creep out widely from there.
  548  And with reason, since shaken by wagons there tremble
  549  whole houses beside the road, under no great weight,
  550  and they leap no less if anything in the road
  551  jolts up the iron-bound rims of the wheels on either side.
  552  It happens too, when into great and vast pools of water
  553  a huge clod rolls down from the earth through age,
  554  that the earth too is tossed by the wave of the water and totters;
  555  as a vessel cannot stand firm in water unless the liquid
  556  within has ceased to be tossed about in an unsteady wave.
  557  Further, when wind, gathered through the hollow places of the earth,
  558  leans forward from one quarter and presses,
  559  straining with great strength upon the deep caverns,
  560  the earth leans over to where the headlong force of the wind presses.
  561  Then the buildings that are built up above the earth,
  562  and the more so the higher each rises toward heaven,
  563  lean and threaten, pitched forward toward the same quarter,
  564  and the beams, thrust out, overhang, ready to go.
  565  And yet men fear to believe that for the nature of the great world
  566  some fatal time and overthrow is waiting,
  567  even when they see so vast a mass of lands leaning over!
  568  But unless the winds drew breath, no force could rein things in
  569  or hold them back from destruction as they go;
  570  as it is, because they draw breath by turns and grow heavy,
  571  and, as it were, gathered, come back again, and give way, driven back,
  572  for this reason the earth more often threatens collapse
  573  than causes it; for it leans over and springs back,
  574  and, having slipped forward, recovers its seat in balance.
  575  By this principle, therefore, all buildings totter,
  576  tops more than middles, middles than bottoms, bottoms hardly at all.
  577  This too is a cause of that same great trembling:
  578  when wind and some very great force of air, suddenly
  579  arising either from outside or from the earth itself,
  580  has hurled itself into the hollow places of the earth, and there
  581  among the great caverns first roars with tumult
  582  and is carried whirling round, and then, when its force, driven on
  583  and harried, bursts out of doors, and at the same time, splitting the deep
  584  earth, it makes a great gaping chasm.
  585  This happened at Sidon in Syria, and was so at Aegium
  586  in the Peloponnese, cities which this outbreak of air
  587  and the earthquake that rose up threw into ruin.
  588  And many walls besides have fallen through great
  589  upheavals on land, and many cities down through the sea
  590  have settled to the bottom, together with their citizens.
  591  But if it does not break forth, still the very onset of the air
  592  and the fierce force of the wind is spread through the earth's many holes
  593  like a shudder, and from there strikes in a trembling;
  594  as when cold comes deep into our limbs
  595  it shakes them against their will, forcing them to tremble and move.
  596  So through the cities people tremble with a twofold terror:
  597  they fear the roofs above, and they dread below lest nature
  598  should suddenly dissolve the caverns of the earth,
  599  or lest, torn apart, it spread its gaping chasm wide
  600  and, thrown into confusion, seek to fill it with its own ruins.
  601  Therefore, however much they may think that heaven and earth
  602  will be uncorrupted, entrusted to eternal safety,
  603  yet now and then the very present force of danger
  604  sets beneath them this goad of fear as well, from some quarter,
  605  lest the earth, snatched from under their feet, be swept away
  606  into the abyss, and the sum of things, betrayed, follow
  607  utterly, and a confused ruin of the world come to be.

  608  First, men wonder that nature does not make the sea larger,
  609  when so great is the downrush of waters into it,
  610  when all the rivers come to it from every side.
  611  Add the wandering rains and the flying storms
  612  which sprinkle and drench all seas and lands;
  613  add its own springs: yet all these, against the sum of the sea,
  614  will be scarcely the increase of a single drop;
  615  so it is less a wonder that the great sea does not grow.
  616  Further, the sun draws off a great part with its heat.
  617  For indeed we see that clothes dripping with moisture
  618  are dried out by the sun with its burning rays;
  619  and we see the seas are many and spread wide beneath it.
  620  Therefore, however small a portion of moisture the sun
  621  may sip from the level sea at any one place,
  622  yet over so great a space it will take much from the waves.
  623  Then again the winds too can lift a great part
  624  of moisture as they sweep the levels, since very often
  625  we see the roads dried by the winds in a single night
  626  and soft mud hardening into crusts.
  627  Further, I have shown that the clouds too lift much
  628  moisture, taken up from the great level of the sea,
  629  and scatter it everywhere over the whole round of lands,
  630  when it rains on the earth and the winds carry the clouds.
  631  Finally, since the earth is of a porous body
  632  and is joined to the sea, girding its shores on every side,
  633  it must be that, as water's moisture comes from land into sea,
  634  so it seeps likewise into the lands from the salt level;
  635  for the brine is strained out, and the matter of moisture
  636  oozes back, and all of it gathers at the heads of the rivers,
  637  and from there returns over the lands, a column of sweet water,
  638  where the way, once cut, has carried the waves down with liquid foot.
  639  Now what the principle is by which, through the jaws of Mount Etna,
  640  fires at times breathe out in so great a whirlwind
  641  I shall unfold; for with no common disaster did the storm
  642  of flame arise and, holding sway over the Sicilian fields,
  643  turned the faces of the neighboring peoples toward itself,
  644  when, seeing all the precincts of heaven smoking and sparkling,
  645  they filled their hearts with fearful anxiety
  646  at what new things nature might be working.
  647  In these matters you must look widely and deeply
  648  and see far out into every quarter,
  649  that you may remember the sum of things is unfathomable,
  650  and to see that one heaven, of the whole sum,
  651  is how small a part, how minute a fraction it makes,
  652  not even so great a part as one man is of the whole earth.
  653  If you set this well before you and look at it squarely,
  654  and see it squarely, you would cease to wonder at many things.
  655  For does any of us wonder if someone has taken
  656  into his limbs a fever arising with burning heat,
  657  or any other pain of disease through the body?
  658  For suddenly the foot swells; often a sharp pain
  659  seizes the teeth, invades the very eyes;
  660  the sacred fire breaks out and, creeping in the body, burns
  661  whatever part it has seized, and crawls through the limbs,
  662  no doubt because there are seeds of many things,
  663  and this earth and heaven bear enough of disease and harm
  664  for the force of measureless disease to grow up from them.
  665  So, then, we must think that to the whole of heaven and earth
  666  all things are supplied from the infinite in plenty,
  667  so that on a sudden the earth can be shaken and moved,
  668  and a rapid whirlwind run across sea and lands,
  669  Etna's fire overflow, and the heaven burst into flame;
  670  for this too happens, and the precincts of heaven burn,
  671  and storms of rain come with a heavier gathering
  672  when by chance the seeds of waters have so assembled.
  673  "But the stormy blaze of that fire is too enormous."
  674  Of course; and a river seems greatest to the man
  675  who has never before seen a greater, and enormous
  676  a tree or a man seems, and all things of every kind
  677  the greatest each has seen, he imagines are enormous,
  678  though all things, with heaven and earth and sea,
  679  are nothing to the entire sum of the whole sum.
  680  Now, however, in what ways that flame, suddenly roused,
  681  blows out from the vast furnaces of Etna
  682  I shall unfold. First, the nature of the whole mountain
  683  is hollow beneath, held up for the most part on caverns of flint.
  684  Further, in all the caves there is wind and air.
  685  For wind arises when air is stirred and set in motion.
  686  When this has grown hot through and through and, raging, has heated
  687  all the rocks around, where it touches, and the earth, and from them
  688  has struck out fire, hot with swift flames,
  689  it rises and so flings itself out on high through the straight jaws.
  690  And so it carries its heat far, and far it scatters
  691  the ash, and rolls smoke in thick darkness,
  692  and thrusts out at the same time rocks of wondrous weight;
  693  so you need not doubt that this is the stormy force of air.
  694  Further, along a great part of its length the sea breaks its waves
  695  at the roots of that mountain and draws back its surge.
  696  From this sea, caves reach beneath to the high jaws
  697  of the mountain. By this way, we must admit, there passes

  698  and the fact compels, that it pierces deep from the open sea
  699  and breathes out, and therefore lifts the flame
  700  and throws up rocks and raises clouds of sand.
  701  For on the topmost summit there are craters, as they themselves
  702  give them the name, what we call jaws and mouths.
  703  There are some things, too, for which to state one cause
  704  is not enough, but several, though one of them is the true one;
  705  just as, if you yourself should see the lifeless body
  706  of a man lying far off, it is fitting to name all
  707  the causes of death, so that the one cause of his death is named;
  708  for you could not prove that he died by steel
  709  or by cold, or by disease, or perhaps by poison,
  710  but we know that what befell him is something
  711  of this kind. So we can say the same in many matters.
  712  The Nile rises toward summer and overflows the plains,
  713  unique on earth, the river of all Egypt.
  714  It waters Egypt often in the middle of the heat,
  715  either because in summer the north winds blow against its mouths,
  716  at that time of year when they are said to be the Etesians,
  717  and blowing against the stream hold it back, and driving
  718  the waters upward fill it and force it to stay.
  719  For beyond doubt these blasts are carried against
  720  the stream, blasts driven from the chill stars of the pole;
  721  that river comes from the heat-bearing quarter, from the south,
  722  among the black generations of men, their color baked through,
  723  rising far within, from the region of midday.
  724  It may be, too, that a great heap of sand
  725  blocks up the mouths against the opposing waves,
  726  when the sea, stirred up by winds, sweeps sand inward;
  727  by this means it happens that the river's outlet is less free
  728  and the waters likewise have less downhill onrush.
  729  It happens too, perhaps, that more rains fall at its head
  730  at that time when the Etesian blasts of the north winds
  731  drive all the clouds into those regions.
  732  Of course, when they have been driven toward the region of midday
  733  and have come together there, at last, against the high mountains,
  734  the clouds are crowded together and pressed by force.
  735  Perhaps it grows from deep in the high mountains of the Ethiopians,
  736  where the sun, surveying all things, forces the white snows
  737  to come down into the plains with its melting rays.

  738  Come now: whatever the Avernian places and lakes may be,
  739  I will explain to you with what nature they are endowed.
  740  First, the name by which they are called, Avernian, was given
  741  from the fact itself, because they are hostile to all birds:
  742  when birds in their flight have come over those places,
  743  they forget the oarage of their wings, they slacken their sails,
  744  and fall headlong, their soft necks drooping, stretched out
  745  on the ground, if the nature of the place so happens to have it,
  746  or in the water, if a lake of Avernus lies beneath.
  747  Such a place is near Cumae, where the mountains, filled
  748  with pungent sulphur, smoke, enriched with hot springs.
  749  There is one too within the walls of Athens, on the very
  750  citadel's top, by the temple of kindly Pallas Tritonis,
  751  where the hoarse ones never bring their bodies on the wing,
  752  the crows, not even when the altars smoke with offerings;
  753  so constantly do they shun it, not from Pallas's fierce anger
  754  at their watchfulness, as the poets of the Greeks have sung,
  755  but the nature of the place does the work by its own power.
  756  In Syria too, it is said, a place is likewise to be seen
  757  where, as soon as even four-footed beasts have first set
  758  their footsteps in it, its very force makes them drop heavily,
  759  as if they had suddenly been slaughtered to the divine shades.
  760  All these things are brought about by a natural principle,
  761  and the causes they come from, their origin, are plain to see,
  762  lest a gate of Orcus be believed to lie in these regions,
  763  and we then think spirits are led from here to Acheron's shores,
  764  led down, it may be, by the gods of the shades below,
  765  as wing-footed stags are often thought, with their nostrils,
  766  to draw from their lairs the crawling generations of wild things.
  767  How far this is driven from true reasoning,
  768  take note; for now I attempt to speak of the thing itself.
  769  First, I say this, as I have often said before as well:
  770  that in the earth there are shapes of things of every kind;
  771  many that are food, life-giving, and many that diseases
  772  can drive in upon us and that can hasten death.
  773  And that different things suit different creatures better
  774  for the ordering of life we have shown before,
  775  because of their unlike nature and their unlike
  776  textures, one from another, and their first shapes.
  777  Many things pass hostile through the ears, many through the very
  778  nostrils make their way in, harmful and rough in their touch,
  779  nor are there few that must be shunned by touch, nor again few
  780  to be fled from by sight, or that are bitter in taste.
  781  Next, you can see how many things there are for a man
  782  bitterly hostile to sense, foul and oppressive;
  783  first, to certain trees an oppressive shade is given,
  784  so much so that they often cause pains of the head
  785  if someone has lain stretched out beneath them on the grass.
  786  There is also, on the great mountains of Helicon, a tree
  787  that is used to killing a man with the foul smell of its flower.
  788  Plainly all these rise up out of the earth for this reason,
  789  that many seeds of many things, in many ways,
  790  the earth holds mixed together, and gives them forth apart.
  791  And a night-lamp, newly put out, when with its sharp
  792  reek it strikes the nostrils, puts to sleep there and then
  793  the one whose sickness makes him fall and foam at the mouth.
  794  And a woman, put to sleep by heavy castoreum, sinks back,
  795  and the bright work slips from her tender hands,
  796  if she has smelled it at the time of her monthly flow.
  797  Many things besides make the limbs droop, and through the joints
  798  loosen them, and shake the spirit in its seats within.
  799  Again, if you also linger in hot baths
  800  too full, and bathe, how easily, in the tub of boiling water,
  801  it comes about that you often collapse in the middle!
  802  And the heavy force and smell of charcoal makes its way
  803  how easily into the brain, unless we first take water!
  804  But when a burning fever, mastering the limbs, has taken hold,
  805  then the smell of wine is like a killing blow.
  806  Do you not see, too, that in the earth itself sulphur
  807  is produced, and bitumen thickens with a foul smell,
  808  and again, where men follow the veins of silver and of gold,
  809  searching with iron the hidden places deep in the earth,
  810  what smells Scaptensula breathes out from below?
  811  Or what evil the gold mines breathe out!
  812  What faces, what colors they give to men!
  813  Do you not see, or hear, how in a short time
  814  they are apt to die, and how the store of life fails them,
  815  those whom the great force of necessity holds to such work?
  816  All these exhalations, therefore, the earth seethes out
  817  and breathes forth into the open and the sky's free spaces.
  818  So also the Avernian places must send up against birds
  819  a deadly force, which rises from the earth into the breezes
  820  so that it poisons the space of sky over some part;
  821  and as soon as a bird has been carried on its wings into it,
  822  there it is hampered, seized by unseen poison,
  823  so that it falls straight down where the exhalation is directed.
  824  And when it has collapsed there, the same force of that exhalation
  825  takes the remnants of life from all its limbs.
  826  For indeed at first it stirs in it a kind of turmoil;
  827  afterwards it comes about, when they have fallen at last
  828  into the poison's very springs, life too must there be vomited,
  829  because there is a great abundance of evil all around.
  830  It happens too that this force and exhalation of Avernus
  831  drives apart the air, whatever lies between birds and earth,
  832  so that at times the place there is left almost empty.
  833  And when, flying, they have come directly over this place,
  834  at once the effort of their wings limps, empty,
  835  and all the striving of the wings on either side is betrayed.
  836  Here, when they cannot lean their weight and rest upon their wings,
  837  plainly, it forces them by their weight to slip down to the earth,
  838  nature does, and lying now through the almost empty void
  839  they scatter their spirits through all the passages of the body.

  840  Further, the water in wells grows colder in summer,
  841  because the earth dries out with the heat, and whatever seeds
  842  of heat it may have of its own, it lets go into the breezes.
  843  The more, therefore, the earth is exhausted by heat,
  844  the colder too becomes the water hidden in the earth.
  845  Again, when all the earth is pressed by cold and draws together
  846  and as it were congeals, plainly in drawing together
  847  it squeezes out into the wells whatever heat it holds.
  848  There is, by the shrine of Hammon, a spring in the light of day
  849  cold, and hot in the time of night, so the story goes.
  850  At this spring men marvel too much, and suppose that by the fierce
  851  sun beneath the earth it partly grows hot
  852  when night has covered the lands in fearful darkness.
  853  And this is removed far, too far, from true reasoning.
  854  For when the sun, handling the naked body of the water,
  855  could not make it warm from its upper side,
  856  though its light above enjoys such burning heat,
  857  how could it here, beneath earth so dense in body,
  858  cook the water through and nurse it with warm heat?
  859  Especially since it can scarcely, through the walls of houses,
  860  make its heat work its way in with its blazing rays.
  861  What, then, is the principle? Doubtless that the earth is more
  862  porous around the spring than the rest of the earth,
  863  and there are many seeds of fire near the body of the water.
  864  So, when night has buried the earth in dew-bearing waves,
  865  at once the earth grows cold deep down and draws together.
  866  By this principle it happens that, as if pressed by a hand,
  867  it squeezes out into the spring whatever seeds of fire it holds,
  868  which make the water hot to the touch, and its steam hot.
  869  Then, when the sun has risen and parted the earth with its rays
  870  and made it porous, its warm heat mixing in,
  871  back again to their old seats return the first-beginnings
  872  of fire, and all the water's heat withdraws into the earth.
  873  For this reason the spring grows cold in the light of day.
  874  Further, by the rays of the sun the water's
  875  moisture is tossed, and in the light thins out with the trembling heat;
  876  therefore it happens that whatever seeds of fire it has
  877  it lets go, just as often the frost it holds within itself
  878  it gives up, and dissolves its ice and loosens its knots.

  879  Cold too is the spring above which tow, when set there, often
  880  throws out a flame, catching fire at once,
  881  and a pine torch, lit on a like principle, shines on the waves
  882  wherever, floating, it is driven along by the breezes.
  883  No wonder: because in the water there are very many seeds
  884  of heat, and from the earth itself, from deep below, it must be
  885  that bodies of fire rise up through the whole spring
  886  and at the same time breathe out and go forth into the breezes,
  887  yet not so many that the spring could become hot;
  888  further, a force compels them, scattered, to break out
  889  suddenly through the water and to gather together above.
  890  Of this kind in the sea at Aradus is the spring of fresh
  891  water, that gushes and pushes away the salt waves round it;
  892  and in many other regions too the level sea offers
  893  a timely service to thirsty sailors,
  894  in that it spews up fresh water among the salt waves.
  895  So, then, through that spring those seeds can break out
  896  and bubble forth; and when they come together in the tow
  897  or cling to the body of the torch,
  898  they easily blaze up at once, because the tow and torches
  899  also hold in themselves many seeds of fire.
  900  Do you not see, too, that when you bring a wick, just now
  901  put out, close to the lights at night, it catches fire before
  902  it has touched the flame, and a torch on a like principle?
  903  And many other things, further, touched first by the heat itself,
  904  blaze up at a distance before fire soaks them from close by.
  905  This, then, we must think also happens in that spring.
  906  For the rest, I shall begin to set out by what covenant
  907  of nature it comes about that this stone can draw iron,
  908  which the Greeks call the Magnes, from its native name,
  909  because it arose within the native bounds of the Magnetes.
  910  Men marvel at this stone; for it often makes
  911  a chain out of rings hanging down from itself.
  912  For indeed you may see at times five or more
  913  in a hanging line, swung about by the light breezes,
  914  where one hangs down from another, clinging underneath,
  915  and each from the next comes to know the stone's force and bonds;
  916  so pervasively does its force prevail throughout.
  917  In matters of this kind many things must be established before
  918  you can give a reasoning of the thing itself,
  919  and it must be approached by very long windings;
  920  so all the more I ask of you attentive ears and mind.
  921  First, from all the things we see, whatever they are,
  922  there must ceaselessly flow, be sent out and scattered
  923  bodies that strike the eyes and provoke the sight.
  924  And from certain things odors flow perpetually;
  925  as cold from rivers, heat from the sun, surge from the waves
  926  of the sea, eater of walls along the shores,
  927  nor do various sounds cease to stream through the air;
  928  finally, a moisture of salty flavor often comes into the mouth
  929  when we walk beside the sea, and when we watch
  930  wormwood being diluted and mixed, its bitterness touches us.
  931  So true is it that from all things each thing is carried off
  932  in a stream, and sent away in all directions on every side,
  933  and no delay, no rest is granted in the flowing,
  934  since we perpetually feel, and may always
  935  see all things, smell them, and sense them sounding.
  936  Now I shall go back to recall how porous of body
  937  all things are; which is clear also in my first song.
  938  For indeed, although to know this bears on many matters,
  939  among the first, for this very matter now at hand,
  940  which I set about discussing, it must be established
  941  that nothing is in view but body mixed with void.
  942  First, it happens that in caves the rocks above
  943  sweat with moisture and drip with trickling drops.
  944  Likewise sweat streams out from all our body,
  945  the beard grows, and hairs through all our members, through our limbs.
  946  All food is spread into the veins, and increases and feeds
  947  even the furthest parts of the body and the little nails.
  948  Likewise we feel cold and warm heat pass through bronze,
  949  we feel them likewise pass through gold
  950  and through silver, when we hold full cups.
  951  Finally, through the stone partitions of houses voices
  952  fly through, smell streams through, and cold, and the heat
  953  of fire, which is used to piercing even the strength of iron,
  954  finally where the breastplate of the sky encloses all around,
  955  and the force of disease as well, when it creeps in from outside;
  956  and storms that have arisen in earth and sky
  957  withdraw, far off, into sky and earth, as is right;
  958  since nothing is put together except with porous body.
  959  Add to this that not all the bodies, whatever they are, thrown off
  960  from things are endowed with the same effect on sense
  961  and fitted in the same way to all things.
  962  First, the sun bakes the earth and makes it parched,
  963  but it melts the ice, and on the high mountains forces the high
  964  piled-up snows to waste away under its rays;
  965  finally, wax set in its heat becomes liquid.
  966  Fire likewise makes bronze liquid and dissolves gold,
  967  but hides and flesh it draws and contracts into one.
  968  The moisture of water, again, hardens iron from the fire,
  969  but softens hides and flesh hardened by heat.
  970  The wild olive so delights the bearded she-goats,
  971  as if it truly flowed with ambrosia and were steeped in nectar,
  972  though for man nothing grows more bitter than its sour leaf.
  973  Finally, the pig flees marjoram oil and fears every
  974  ointment; for to bristly pigs it is a sharp poison,
  975  though to us it sometimes seems, as it were, to give new life.
  976  But on the other hand, though mud to us is the foulest
  977  filth, to pigs this same thing seems delightful,
  978  so that they roll in it, whole, insatiably.
  979  This also remains, before I set about to speak
  980  of the thing itself, a point that seems to need saying first.
  981  Since many pores have been given to various things,
  982  they must be endowed with natures unlike one another
  983  and each must have its own nature and its own paths.
  984  For indeed living creatures have various senses in them,
  985  each of which takes in its own proper object;
  986  for we see sounds penetrate by one way, by another flavor
  987  from juices, by another the scents of reek.
  988  Clearly it is the nature of the paths that compels this,
  989  varying in many ways, as we showed a little before.
  990  Further, one thing seems to stream through rocks,
  991  another through wood, another to pass through gold,
  992  and another to make its way out through silver and glass;
  993  for here appearances are seen to flow, there heat to go,
  994  and the same things to let one thing through more quickly than others.
  995  Clearly it is the nature of the paths that compels this,
  996  varying in many ways, as we showed a little before,
  997  because of the unlike nature and texture of things.
  998  Therefore, when these things have been well confirmed and laid down,
  999  all standing ready for us, set out beforehand,
 1000  for the rest, from here the reasoning comes easily, and the whole
 1001  cause laid open, that lures the force of iron.

 1002  First, from this stone there must flow very many
 1003  seeds, or a current, which with its blows drives apart all the air
 1004  that is lodged anywhere between the stone and the iron.
 1005  When this space is emptied, and a great deal of place
 1006  in the middle is emptied, at once the iron's first-beginnings
 1007  slip forward into the void and fall, joined, and so it happens
 1008  that the ring itself follows, and so goes with its whole body.
 1009  Nor is there anything that, from its own first elements,
 1010  entangled and tightly linked, holds together more closely
 1011  than the nature of strong iron, that cold, rough thing.
 1012  So it is less a wonder, the thing that is called strange,
 1013  if bodies, rising in numbers from the iron,
 1014  cannot go into the void without the ring itself following;
 1015  and this it does, and follows, until it has reached
 1016  the stone itself and clung to it by hidden fastenings.
 1017  The same happens in every direction: wherever a place
 1018  becomes empty, whether from the side or from above,
 1019  the neighboring bodies are carried at once into the void;
 1020  for indeed they are driven by blows from elsewhere, nor can they
 1021  rise upward of their own accord into the air.
 1022  To this is added further, so that it can happen the more,
 1023  that this thing is helped too by an aid and a motion:
 1024  for as soon as the air in front of the ring is made thinner,
 1025  and the place made emptier and more voided,
 1026  it comes about at once that the air lodged behind it
 1027  as it were carries it forward from the rear and pushes it on.
 1028  For the air set all around things always beats on them;
 1029  but at such a time it happens that it pushes the iron on,
 1030  because on one side space stands empty and takes it in.
 1031  This air I tell you of, through the iron's many pores
 1032  working its way finely into its small parts,
 1033  thrusts and drives it, as the wind drives a ship and its sails.
 1034  Finally, all things must have air in their body,
 1035  since they are of rare body, and air
 1036  is set around all things and laid close against them.
 1037  This air, therefore, that lies hidden deep within the iron,
 1038  is always tossed in restless motion, and so
 1039  beats on the ring beyond doubt and stirs it from within;
 1040  that is, the ring is carried the same way it has already
 1041  plunged once, and has made its effort toward the empty part.
 1042  It happens too that the nature of iron draws back from this stone
 1043  at times, used as it is to flee and follow by turns.
 1044  I have even seen Samothracian iron rings leap,
 1045  and iron filings rage at the same time inside bronze
 1046  bowls, when this Magnesian stone was set beneath;
 1047  so eagerly does the iron seem to long to flee the stone.
 1048  With bronze set between, so great a discord is created
 1049  because, no doubt, when the current of the bronze has first
 1050  seized and taken possession of the open paths of the iron,
 1051  the stone's current comes after, and finds everything full
 1052  in the iron, and has no way to swim through as before;
 1053  it is forced, therefore, to strike and beat with its flow
 1054  the iron's texture; and in this way it spits from itself
 1055  and drives through the bronze what, without it, it often draws in.
 1056  In these matters cease to wonder at this: that the current
 1057  from this stone cannot drive other things in the same way.
 1058  For some stand firm, relying on their weight, such as gold;
 1059  others, because they are of so rare a body that the current
 1060  flies through untouched, cannot be driven anywhere;
 1061  of which kind the matter of wood is seen to be.
 1062  The nature of iron, therefore, placed between the two,
 1063  when it has taken in some small bodies of bronze, then it happens
 1064  that the Magnesian stones drive it with their stream.
 1065  And yet these things are not so foreign to other things
 1066  that I have only a scant supply of examples of this kind
 1067  that I could recount, each fitted uniquely to the other.
 1068  You see, first, that stones are bonded by lime alone.
 1069  Timber is joined into one by the glue of bulls,
 1070  so that the veins of the boards more often gape with a flaw
 1071  than the bull-glue's bonds can loosen their hold.
 1072  The vine-born liquors dare to mingle with the springs
 1073  of water, though heavy pitch cannot, nor light olive oil.
 1074  And the purple color of the shellfish is joined in one
 1075  body with the wool, so that it can never be parted,
 1076  not if you took pains to renew it with Neptune's flood,
 1077  not if the whole sea wished to wash it out with all its waves.
 1078  Again, is there not one thing alone that couples gold to gold,
 1079  and does it not come about that bronze is joined to bronze by white lead?
 1080  How many others one could find! What then?
 1081  You have no need anywhere of such long windings,
 1082  nor is it fitting for me to spend so much labor here;
 1083  but it is better to grasp many things briefly, in few words.
 1084  Things whose textures have so fallen out, matching one another,
 1085  that hollows meet solids, the hollows of this the solids of that,
 1086  and of that with this, these have the best joining among themselves.
 1087  There is also the case where, as if folded with rings and hooks,
 1088  certain things can be held coupled to one another;
 1089  which seems to happen rather with this stone and iron.
 1090  Now what the principle of disease is, and from where, suddenly,
 1091  a force of sickness, risen, can blow up a deadly disaster
 1092  for the race of men and the herds of beasts,
 1093  I shall unfold. First, that there are seeds of many things
 1094  that are vital to us, I have taught above,
 1095  and that, on the other hand, many that make for disease and death
 1096  must fly about; when these by chance have happened to rise
 1097  and have disturbed the sky, the air becomes diseased.
 1098  And all that force of diseases, and pestilence,
 1099  either comes from outside, as clouds and mists from above
 1100  come through the sky, or else often, rising of itself,
 1101  surges up from the earth, when, being damp, it has gathered rot,
 1102  struck by unseasonable rains and suns.
 1103  Do you not see, too, that by the strangeness of sky and waters
 1104  those are attacked who come from far away from country and home,
 1105  because things differ so widely?
 1106  For how much do we think the sky of the Britons differs
 1107  from that in Egypt, where the axis of the world limps,
 1108  or how much what is in Pontus differs from Gades, and from
 1109  to the black generations of men, their color baked through?
 1110  As we see these four regions differ among themselves,
 1111  by the four winds and quarters of the sky,
 1112  so the color and face of men are seen to differ
 1113  widely, and diseases to hold the races kind by kind.
 1114  There is the elephant disease, which by the streams of the Nile
 1115  is born in the middle of Egypt, and nowhere else.
 1116  In Attica the feet are attacked, and the eyes in the Achaean
 1117  lands. So one place is hostile to one part
 1118  and limb, another to another; the varying air does this.
 1119  Therefore, when a sky that happens to be alien to us
 1120  stirs itself, and a hostile air has begun to creep,
 1121  like mist and cloud it creeps little by little, and all
 1122  that it passes through it confounds, and forces to change,
 1123  it happens too that, when at last it comes into our sky,
 1124  it corrupts it, and makes it like itself, and alien.
 1125  This new disaster and pestilence, therefore, suddenly
 1126  either falls on the waters, or settles on the crops themselves,
 1127  or on other food of men and fodder of beasts,
 1128  or else its force stays hanging in the air itself,
 1129  and when, breathing, we draw in the mingled air from there,
 1130  we must suck that too into our body along with it.
 1131  On a like principle, pestilence often comes on oxen too,
 1132  and sickness upon the sluggish bleating flocks.
 1133  Nor does it matter whether we travel into places
 1134  hostile to us and change the cloak of sky,
 1135  or nature of its own accord brings a corrupted sky to us,
 1136  or something we are not used to using,
 1137  which can attack us by its fresh arrival.

 1138  A pattern of disease like this, and a death-bearing heat, once
 1139  made the fields deadly within the borders of Cecrops
 1140  and laid waste the roads and drained the city of its citizens.
 1141  For coming from deep within, risen in the borders of Egypt,
 1142  having measured out much air and the swimming plains of the sea,
 1143  it fell at last upon the whole people of Pandion.
 1144  From then on, in throngs, they were handed over to disease and death.
 1145  At first they carried their heads on fire with fever
 1146  and both their eyes red with a light suffused through them.
 1147  Further, the throat, black within, sweated
 1148  with blood, and the path of the voice, hedged with ulcers, closed,
 1149  and the tongue, the mind's interpreter, oozed gore,
 1150  weakened by its ills, heavy to move, rough to the touch.
 1151  Then, when through the throat it had filled the chest, and the very
 1152  force of the disease had flowed into the sick men's grieving heart,
 1153  then indeed all the barriers of life began to totter.
 1154  The breath rolled out from the mouth a foul stench,
 1155  the way rotten corpses stink when they are flung out.
 1156  And then the strength of the whole mind, and the whole
 1157  body, grew faint, now on the very threshold of death.
 1158  And with these intolerable ills an anxious anguish
 1159  was a constant companion, and complaint mixed with groaning,
 1160  and frequent retching, often through night and day,
 1161  forcing the sinews and limbs constantly to cramp,
 1162  broke them down, wearing them out, worn out already.
 1163  Nor could you see in anyone, with excessive heat,
 1164  the outermost part on the surface of the body burning,
 1165  but rather it offered a lukewarm touch to the hands,
 1166  and all the body at once reddened with ulcers as if burned in,
 1167  as it does when the sacred fire spreads through the limbs.
 1168  But the inmost part of these men blazed to the bones,
 1169  a flame blazed in the stomach as in furnaces within.
 1170  Nothing, then, however light and thin, could you turn
 1171  to use for anyone's limbs, but always wind and cold.
 1172  Some gave their limbs, burning with disease, to the icy
 1173  rivers, throwing their naked bodies into the waves.
 1174  Many plunged headlong, from high, into the nymphs of the wells,
 1175  coming at them with their mouths themselves gaping open:
 1176  thirst, not to be appeased, plunging their parched bodies in,
 1177  made a great flood of water no more than a few drops.
 1178  Nor was there any rest from the ill: their bodies lay
 1179  worn out. Medicine muttered in silent fear,
 1180  since so often, wide open and burning with disease,
 1181  they would roll the lights of their eyes, deprived of sleep.
 1182  And many signs of death besides were given then:
 1183  the mind's thought disordered in grief and fear,
 1184  the brow gloomy, the face frenzied and fierce,
 1185  the ears, further, troubled and full of sounds,
 1186  the breath quick, or else huge and drawn at long intervals,

 1188  spittle thin and in small flecks, tinged with the color of saffron
 1189  and salt, brought up through the throat with difficulty by a hoarse cough.

 1191  and from the feet the cold would climb little by little,
 1192  without fail. So too, finally, at the very last hour,
 1193  the nostrils pinched, the tip of the nose
 1194  sharp and thin, the eyes hollowed, the temples hollow, the skin cold
 1195  and hard, the mouth slack in a gaping grin, the brow stayed taut.
 1196  And not long after, their limbs lay stiff in death.
 1197  And about the eighth bright light of the sun,
 1198  or even at the ninth lamp of day, they gave back their life.

 1201  wasting and death awaited him later on,

 1203  corrupted blood flowed out from the brimming nostrils.
