    1  Out of such great darkness you who first were able to raise
    2  so clear a light, shining it on the good things of life,
    3  you I follow, glory of the Greek race, and now in your
    4  deep-pressed tracks I set down the planted prints of my feet,
    5  not so much from desire to compete as out of love,
    6  because I long to imitate you; for how should a swallow contend
    7  with swans, or what could kids on trembling legs
    8  achieve in running to match the strong horse's force?
    9  You, father, are the discoverer of things; you supply us
   10  with a father's precepts, and from your pages, famous one,
   11  as bees in flowering glades sip at everything,
   12  so we likewise feed on all your golden sayings,
   13  golden, and always most worthy of perpetual life.
   14  For as soon as your reasoning, sprung from a divine mind,
   15  begins to proclaim aloud the nature of things,
   16  the terrors of the mind scatter and flee, the walls of the world
   17  draw apart; I see things carried on through the whole void.
   18  The power of the gods appears, and their quiet seats,
   19  which neither winds shake nor clouds with rainstorms
   20  sprinkle, nor does snow, congealed by bitter frost,
   21  falling white, do them violence; an ever-cloudless upper air
   22  covers them and laughs with light poured wide:
   23  nature, moreover, supplies them all things, and nothing
   24  at any time diminishes their peace of mind.
   25  But on the other hand nowhere do the precincts of Acheron appear,
   26  nor does the earth prevent all things from being seen
   27  that are carried on beneath our feet, below, through the void.
   28  At these things a certain divine pleasure
   29  and a shudder seize me there, that by your power nature
   30  lies so plainly open, laid bare on every side.
   31  And since I have taught what the origins of all things
   32  are like, and how, differing in their various shapes,
   33  they fly of their own accord, driven by eternal motion,
   34  and in what way each thing can be created from them,
   35  next after these things, it seems, the nature of the mind
   36  and of the spirit must now be made clear in my verses,
   37  and that fear of Acheron driven headlong out of doors
   38  which troubles human life from its very depths,
   39  suffusing all things with the blackness of death, and leaves
   40  no pleasure clear and pure.
   41  For though men often say that diseases are more to be feared,
   42  and a life of disgrace, than the Tartarus of death,
   43  and that they know the nature of the mind is of blood,
   44  or else of wind, if so their fancy happens to run,
   45  and that they have no need whatever of our reasoning,
   46  from this you may observe that all these things are boasted for the sake
   47  of praise rather than because the thing itself is approved.
   48  These same men, exiled from their country and banished far
   49  from the sight of men, befouled with some ugly charge,
   50  afflicted, in short, with every hardship, still live on,
   51  and yet wherever they come in their misery they make offerings to the dead,
   52  and slaughter black cattle, and to the divine shades
   53  send down funeral gifts, and in bitter circumstances
   54  turn their minds far more keenly to religion.
   55  So it is the more fitting to watch a man in doubtful dangers
   56  and to learn in adversity what he is;
   57  for then at last true words are drawn up from the depth
   58  of the heart, and the mask is torn away; what is real remains.
   59  Again, avarice and the blind craving for honors,
   60  which force wretched men to overstep the bounds
   61  of right, and sometimes, as partners and agents of crime,
   62  to strive night and day with surpassing labor
   63  to rise up to the height of wealth: these wounds of life
   64  are fed in no small part by the fear of death.
   65  For foul contempt and bitter want, as a rule,
   66  seem far removed from a sweet and stable life,
   67  and to linger, as it were, already before the gates of death;
   68  and while men, driven by a false terror, wish
   69  to have fled far from these and removed themselves far, far away,
   70  they amass a fortune from the blood of citizens, and greedily
   71  double their riches, piling slaughter on slaughter;
   72  cruelly they rejoice at a brother's sad funeral,
   73  and hate and fear the tables of their kin.
   74  On a like principle, and from this same fear, they are often
   75  wasted by envy: that before their eyes that man is powerful,
   76  that man is gazed at, who walks clothed in bright honor,
   77  while they themselves, they complain, roll in darkness and mire.
   78  Some perish for the sake of statues and a name.
   79  And often, through fear of death, so great a hatred
   80  of life and of seeing the light seizes human beings
   81  that with grieving hearts they decree their own death,
   82  forgetting that this fear is the fountain of their cares:
   83  that this harasses shame, this bursts the bonds of friendship
   84  and, in sum, urges them to overturn piety;
   85  for often before now men have betrayed country and dear parents,
   86  seeking to shun the precincts of Acheron.
   87  For just as children tremble and fear everything
   88  in blind darkness, so we in the light are afraid
   89  at times of things that are no more to be feared than
   90  what children in the dark shudder at and imagine will come.
   91  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness
   92  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
   93  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
   94  First, I say that the mind, which we often call thought,
   95  in which the counsel and governance of life are placed,
   96  is a part of a human being no less than hand and foot
   97  and eyes stand forth as parts of the whole living creature.

   98  that the sense of the mind is not placed in a fixed part,
   99  but is a certain vital condition of the body,
  100  which the Greeks call harmony, because it makes us
  101  live with sense, though the mind is in no part;
  102  as when good health is often said to belong
  103  to the body, and yet it is not any part of the healthy man,
  104  so they do not place the sense of the mind in a fixed part;
  105  and in this they seem to me to wander far astray.
  106  And so the body, which is seen in plain view, is often sick,
  107  when yet in another, hidden part we are glad;
  108  and in turn the reverse often happens, the other way about,
  109  when one wretched in mind is glad in the whole body;
  110  in no other way than if, when a sick man's foot is aching,
  111  his head meanwhile should happen to be in no pain.
  112  Further, when the limbs are given up to soft sleep
  113  and the body, a heavy weight, lies sprawled without sense,
  114  there is yet something else in us which at that time
  115  is stirred in many ways and receives into itself
  116  all the motions of joy and the empty cares of the heart.
  117  Now, so that you may know the spirit too is in the limbs,
  118  and that the body does not feel by any harmony,
  119  first of all it happens that when much of the body is taken away
  120  life still often lingers in our limbs.
  121  And again this same life, when a few bodies of heat
  122  have scattered abroad and air has been driven out through the mouth,
  123  at once deserts the veins and leaves the bones;
  124  so that you may know from this that not all bodies
  125  have an equal share, nor equally prop up our safety,
  126  but rather that these, which are seeds of wind and of warm
  127  heat, see to it that life lingers in the limbs.
  128  There is therefore heat and a vital wind in the body
  129  itself, which deserts our limbs as we die.
  130  Therefore, since the nature of the mind has been found,
  131  and of the spirit, to be a kind of part of man, give back the name
  132  of harmony, brought down to the musicians from lofty Helicon,
  133  or else they themselves drew it from elsewhere and carried it
  134  over to that thing which then had need of a name of its own.
  135  Whatever it is, let them keep it; you, take in the rest of my words.

  136  Now I say that mind and spirit are held joined together
  137  with one another and make up from themselves a single nature,
  138  but that the head, so to speak, the lord over the whole body,
  139  is the counsel which we call the mind and thought.
  140  And this is set and fixed in the middle region of the breast.
  141  For here dread and fear leap up, around these places
  142  joys caress us: here, therefore, are thought and mind.
  143  The rest of the spirit, scattered through the whole body,
  144  obeys, and moves at the nod and impulse of the mind.
  145  This alone, of itself, knows for itself and is glad for itself,
  146  when nothing moves either the spirit or the body along with it.
  147  And just as, when the head or the eye is hurt in us
  148  by an attack of pain, we are not tormented in our whole
  149  body, so the mind is sometimes hurt itself
  150  or thrives with joy, when the rest of the spirit
  151  through the limbs and frame is stirred by nothing new;
  152  but when the mind is moved by a more violent fear,
  153  we see the whole spirit through the limbs feel with it,
  154  and so sweat and pallor break out over the whole
  155  body, and the tongue falters and the voice dies away,
  156  the eyes grow dark, the ears ring, the limbs give way,
  157  and finally, from the mind's terror, we see
  158  men often collapse; so that anyone may easily learn from this
  159  that the spirit is joined with the mind, and that, struck by the mind's
  160  force, it at once drives the body forward and strikes it.
  161  This same reasoning teaches that the nature of mind and spirit
  162  is bodily; for when it is seen to drive the limbs forward,
  163  to snatch the body from sleep and change the face,
  164  and to steer and turn the whole man,
  165  none of which we see can happen without touch,
  166  nor touch, in turn, without body, must we not admit
  167  that mind and spirit consist of a bodily nature?
  168  Further, you see the mind is acted on together with the body
  169  and feels along with it, in our body.
  170  If the grim force of a weapon, though it does not strike out life,
  171  is driven in, laying open bones and sinews within,
  172  yet there follows a faintness, and a pleasant sinking
  173  to the earth, and, on the earth, the turmoil born in the mind,
  174  and now and then a wavering will, as it were, to rise.
  175  Therefore the nature of the mind must be bodily,
  176  since it suffers from bodily weapons and their blow.
  177  Now of what sort of body this mind of yours is, and from what
  178  it is composed, I shall go on to give an account in words.
  179  To begin with, I say that it is exceedingly fine, and is made
  180  of exceedingly tiny bodies. That this is so
  181  you may turn your mind to here, so as to know it thoroughly.
  182  Nothing is seen to come about in so swift a way
  183  as what the mind proposes to happen and itself begins;
  184  therefore the mind stirs itself more quickly than any of the things
  185  whose nature is seen plain before our eyes.
  186  But what is so very mobile must consist of exceedingly round
  187  and exceedingly tiny seeds,
  188  so that, struck by a small impulse, they can be moved.
  189  For water moves and flows at the slightest impulse,
  190  being made, of course, of small shapes that roll.
  191  But the nature of honey, by contrast, is more steady,
  192  its liquid more sluggish and its motion more delayed;
  193  for the whole store of its matter clings more together,
  194  surely because it is not made of bodies so smooth
  195  nor so fine and round.
  196  For a hovering, light breath of air can make
  197  a high heap of poppy seed flow away for you from the top,
  198  but against a pile of stones or of ears of grain
  199  it has no power. Therefore, as bodies are the smallest
  200  and smoothest, so they enjoy mobility;
  201  but whatever are found to be of greater weight
  202  and rougher, the more firmly they are fixed.
  203  Now therefore, since the nature of the mind has been found
  204  exceptionally mobile, it must consist of exceedingly
  205  small and smooth and round bodies.
  206  This fact, once known to you, my good friend, will be found useful
  207  in many matters and will be called timely.
  208  This too reveals its nature,
  209  how thin the texture it is made of, and in how small
  210  a place it would be held, if it could be gathered into a ball:
  211  that as soon as the untroubled quiet of death has taken hold
  212  of a man, and the nature of mind and spirit has withdrawn,
  213  you could see nothing taken away there from the whole body
  214  in appearance, nothing in weight: death leaves all intact
  215  except the vital sense and the warm heat.
  216  Therefore the whole spirit must be of exceedingly small
  217  seeds, linked through veins, flesh, sinews,
  218  inasmuch as, when all of it has gone from the whole body,
  219  the outer contour of the limbs still keeps itself
  220  unharmed, and not one jot of weight is lacking.
  221  It is just as when the flower of Bacchus has died away, or when
  222  the sweet breath of a perfume has scattered into the air,
  223  or when savor has departed from some body;
  224  still the thing itself seems to the eyes no smaller
  225  for that, nor is anything taken from its weight,
  226  surely because many tiny seeds make up
  227  the savors and the scent in the whole body of these things.
  228  Therefore, again and again, the nature of mind and spirit
  229  you may know is made of exceedingly minute
  230  seeds, since when it flees it takes away no weight.
  231  Yet we must not think this nature simple.
  232  For a certain thin breath leaves the dying,
  233  mixed with heat, and heat, in turn, draws air with it;
  234  nor is there any heat that has no air mixed in it;
  235  for since its nature is of loose texture, it must be
  236  that many first-beginnings of air move within it.
  237  So now the nature of the mind is found to be threefold;
  238  and yet these are not enough, all together, to create sensation,
  239  since the mind does not accept that any of these can create
  240  the sense-bearing motions, or what it then turns over in the mind.
  241  Therefore a certain fourth nature must also be
  242  added to these; it is altogether without a name;
  243  than which nothing exists more mobile or more thin,
  244  nor made of smaller and smoother elements;
  245  it is first to distribute the sense-bearing motions through the limbs.
  246  For it is first stirred, made as it is of small shapes;
  247  then heat takes up the motions, and the unseen power of wind,
  248  then air; then all things are set in motion:
  249  the blood is shaken, then the flesh is thrilled through,
  250  all of it; last it is passed to the bones and the marrow,
  251  whether it is pleasure or the opposite burning.
  252  Nor can pain lightly penetrate this far, nor sharp
  253  harm seep through, without all things being thrown into confusion
  254  so far that life lacks room and the spirit's
  255  parts scatter through all the passages of the body.
  256  But mostly an end is made, as it were, at the surface of the body
  257  to these motions: and for this reason we are able to hold on to life.
  258  Now in what way these are mingled with one another, and in what
  259  modes arranged they thrive, though I am eager to give an account,
  260  the poverty of our native speech drags me back against my will;
  261  but still, as far as I can touch on it in sum, I shall touch it.

  262  For the first-beginnings of the beginnings run among one another
  263  in their motions, so that no single one can be set apart
  264  nor its power be made separate by space,
  265  but they stand out, as it were, the many forces of one body.
  266  Just as in the flesh of any living thing, commonly,
  267  there is smell and a certain color and savor, yet from all these
  268  there is made up one complete growth of body,
  269  so heat and air and the unseen power of wind,
  270  mingled, create one nature, together with that mobile
  271  force which hands out from itself to them the beginning of motion,
  272  from which the sense-bearing motion first arises through the flesh.
  273  For this nature lies hidden deep down and underneath,
  274  and there is nothing further below it in our body,
  275  and it is in its turn the very spirit of the whole spirit.
  276  Just as in our limbs and through the whole body
  277  the force of mind and the power of spirit lie mingled and hidden,
  278  because it is created of bodies small and few,
  279  so, you see, this force that has no name, made of minute
  280  bodies, lies hidden, and is, as it were, the very spirit
  281  in its turn of the whole spirit, and rules through the whole body.
  282  On a like principle, wind and air and heat
  283  must be active, mingled with one another through the limbs,
  284  and one lie more beneath or stand out more than another,
  285  so that one thing may be seen to be made from them all,
  286  lest heat and wind apart, and apart the power
  287  of air, should destroy sense and, drawn asunder, dissolve it.
  288  The mind has also that heat which it takes on in anger,
  289  when it boils up and fire flashes more fiercely from the eyes;
  290  there is also much cold breath, the companion of fear,
  291  which stirs shuddering in the limbs and rouses the frame;
  292  there is also that state of air made calm,
  293  which comes about in a tranquil breast and a serene face.
  294  But there is more of the hot in those whose hearts are fierce
  295  and whose angry mind easily boils over into anger,
  296  of which kind above all is the violent force of lions,
  297  who often, groaning, burst their breasts with roaring
  298  and cannot contain the billows of their anger in their breast.
  299  But the cold mind of deer has more of wind
  300  and more quickly rouses icy breaths through the flesh,
  301  which make a trembling motion rise up in the limbs.
  302  But the nature of oxen lives more by calm air,
  303  nor ever does anger's smoky torch, set beneath, rouse it too far,
  304  spreading over it the shadow of blind darkness,
  305  nor is it numbed, pierced through by the icy shafts of fear;
  306  it is set between the two, the deer and the savage lions.
  307  So it is with the race of men: although training may make
  308  some equally polished, still it leaves behind
  309  those first traces of the nature of each one's mind.
  310  Nor must we think that faults can be torn out by the roots,
  311  so that one man does not run more readily to fierce anger,
  312  another not be assailed a little sooner by fear, a third
  313  not take certain things more mildly than is right.
  314  And in many other things the varied natures of men
  315  must differ, and the characters that follow on them;
  316  whose hidden causes I cannot now set out,
  317  nor find as many names as there are shapes
  318  of the beginnings, from which this variety of things arises.
  319  This I see that I can affirm in these matters:
  320  that so slight are the traces of our natures left behind,
  321  which reasoning cannot drive away from us,
  322  that nothing hinders us from living a life worthy of gods.
  323  This nature, then, is held in by the whole body
  324  and is itself the body's guardian and the cause of its safety;
  325  for they cling to one another by common roots
  326  and it seems they cannot be torn apart without destruction.
  327  Just as to tear the smell from lumps of frankincense
  328  is not easy, without its nature too perishing,
  329  so to draw out the nature of mind and spirit
  330  from the whole body is not easy, without all being dissolved.
  331  So, with their beginnings interwoven from their first origin,
  332  they come to be endowed with a life held in partnership,
  333  nor, it seems, can either, without the force of the other,
  334  the power of body or of mind, feel apart, by itself,
  335  but by common motions between them on both sides
  336  sense is fanned and kindled for us through the flesh.
  337  Further, the body by itself is never born,
  338  nor grows, nor is seen to last after death.
  339  For not as the liquid of water often gives off the heat
  340  that was given it, and is not for that reason torn apart itself,
  341  but stays unharmed, not so, I say, can the limbs, left behind,
  342  endure the separation of the spirit,
  343  but they perish utterly, torn apart, and rot away.
  344  So from the beginning of life the mutual contacts
  345  of body and spirit learn the motions of life,
  346  even while laid away in the mother's limbs and womb,
  347  so that separation cannot happen without ruin and harm;
  348  so that you may see, since the cause of their safety is joined,
  349  that their nature too must stand joined together.
  350  For the rest, if anyone denies that the body feels
  351  and believes that the spirit, mingled through the whole body,
  352  takes on this motion which we name sense,
  353  he fights against even plain and true facts.
  354  For who will ever tell us what it is for the body to feel,
  355  if not what the thing itself has openly shown and taught us?
  356  "But when the spirit is let go, the body lacks sense in every part."
  357  Yes, for it loses what was not its own in life,
  358  and loses much besides when it is driven out of life.
  359  Further, to say that the eyes can see nothing,
  360  but that the mind looks out through them as through open doors,
  361  is hard, since the sense of the eyes leads us the other way;
  362  for sense drags us and thrusts us to the pupils themselves,
  363  especially since often we cannot see bright things,
  364  because the light of our eyes is hampered by light.
  365  This does not happen with doors; for the doorways through which
  366  we ourselves look, when thrown open, take on no labor.
  367  Further, if our eyes serve in place of doors,
  368  then, it seems, the mind ought to see things better
  369  with the eyes taken out, the very doorposts removed.
  370  In these matters you could by no means accept
  371  what the holy judgment of the man Democritus lays down,
  372  that the first-beginnings of body and of mind, one by one,
  373  set side by side alternately, diversify and knit the limbs.
  374  For not only are the elements of the spirit much smaller
  375  than those of which our body and flesh are made,
  376  but also yield to them in number, and are sparsely scattered
  377  through the limbs, so that you may promise this much at least:
  378  as small as are the first bodies which, falling on us,
  379  can stir sense-bearing motions in the body, so wide
  380  are the intervals the first origins of the spirit keep.

  381  For we do not feel at times the clinging of dust
  382  upon the body, nor chalk dashed on our limbs and settling there,
  383  nor do we feel mist at night, nor the spider's slender threads
  384  meeting us, when we are netted in them as we go,
  385  nor the same spider's shriveled garment fallen on our head,
  386  nor the feathers of birds, nor the flying down of plants,
  387  which, from their great lightness, mostly fall only reluctantly,
  388  nor do we feel the passage of every creeping creature,
  389  nor each single footprint, one by one,
  390  that gnats and the rest set down upon our body.
  391  So far must many things be stirred in us first
  392  before the first-beginnings of the spirit, shaken, can feel,
  393  the seeds mixed with our bodies through the limbs,
  394  and before, beating across these gaps, they can
  395  run together, unite, and leap apart in turn.
  396  And the mind holds the barriers of life more firmly
  397  and is more master over life than the force of spirit.
  398  For without thought and mind no part of the spirit
  399  can remain in the limbs for the smallest part of time,
  400  but follows readily as companion and departs into the air
  401  and leaves the limbs gone cold in the chill of death.
  402  But he stays in life in whom thought and mind remain,
  403  however torn his trunk, with the limbs all round cut off;
  404  the trunk, with spirit taken from around it and limbs removed,
  405  lives and draws in the life-giving airs of heaven;
  406  deprived, if not entirely, yet of a great part of its spirit,
  407  it still lingers and holds fast in life;
  408  as, when the eye is torn all round, if the pupil remains
  409  unharmed, the living power of seeing stands,
  410  provided only you do not ruin the whole orb of the eye
  411  and cut around the pupil and leave it all alone;
  412  for that too will not happen without the destruction of both.
  413  But if that tiny part in the middle of the eye is eaten away,
  414  at once the light sets and darkness follows,
  415  though the bright orb be otherwise unharmed.
  416  By this covenant spirit and mind are always bound.
  417  Come now, so you may know that in living creatures the minds
  418  and the light spirits are things born and mortal,
  419  verses long sought out and found with sweet labor
  420  I shall go on to set out, worthy of your life.
  421  You, see that you yoke both of them under one name,
  422  and when, for example, I go on to speak of spirit,
  423  teaching that it is mortal, believe that I speak of mind as well,
  424  inasmuch as it is one thing, and a thing joined together.
  425  To begin with, since I have shown that it is fine, and made
  426  of minute bodies, and formed of beginnings much smaller
  427  than the flowing moisture of water
  428  or mist or smoke; for in mobility it far
  429  excels, and moves when struck by a slighter cause;
  430  indeed it is moved even by images of smoke and mist,
  431  as when, lulled in sleep, we see altars
  432  breathe their steam on high and send up smoke;
  433  for beyond doubt it is images that are borne to us here.
  434  Now therefore, since, when vessels are shattered on every side,
  435  you see the moisture flow out and the liquid run away,
  436  and since mist and smoke depart into the air,
  437  believe that the spirit too is scattered, and perishes far
  438  more swiftly, and is sooner dissolved into its first bodies,
  439  once it has been taken from a man's limbs and has withdrawn;
  440  for indeed, since the body, which is as it were its vessel,
  441  cannot contain it when shaken apart by some cause
  442  and made rare by the drawing of blood from the veins,
  443  how could you believe it could be contained by any air,
  444  which is rarer than our body and less able to contain?
  445  Further, we feel that the mind is born together with the body,
  446  and grows along with it, and along with it grows old.
  447  For just as children totter with a body weak
  448  and tender, so a slender judgment of the mind goes with it.
  449  Then when their age has grown up into sturdy strength,
  450  their counsel too is greater and the mind's force more increased.
  451  Later, when the body is shaken by the strong forces
  452  of age, and the limbs have sunk with their strength blunted,
  453  the wit limps, the tongue raves, the mind totters,
  454  all things fail and are lacking at one time.
  455  It follows, therefore, that the whole nature of the spirit
  456  is dissolved as well, like smoke, into the high breezes of the air;
  457  since we see it born alongside, and alongside
  458  grow, and, as I have shown, worn out by age, give way together.
  459  Add to this that we see, just as the body itself
  460  takes on monstrous diseases and hard pain,
  461  so the mind takes on sharp cares and grief and fear;
  462  therefore it follows that it shares in death as well.
  463  What is more, in diseases of the body there often strays
  464  the mind; for it loses its wits and speaks crazed words,
  465  and at times by a heavy lethargy is carried down into deep
  466  and endless sleep, the eyes and the nodding head drooping,
  467  so that it neither hears voices nor has the power to know
  468  the faces of those who, calling it back to life,
  469  stand around, wet with tears on face and cheek.
  470  Therefore you must admit the mind too is dissolved,
  471  since the contagions of disease make their way into it;
  472  for pain and disease are each of them craftsmen of death,
  473  as we have been thoroughly taught before by the deaths of many.
  474  And since the mind, like a sick body, is healed
  475  and equally the mind is healed, the body with an empty ...
  476  Finally, why, when the force of wine has pierced a man,
  477  fierce, and its heat has withdrawn, spread out, into the veins,
  478  a heaviness of the limbs follows, his legs are hobbled
  479  as he staggers, his tongue grows slow, his mind is sodden,
  480  his eyes swim, shouting, sobbing, quarrels swell,
  481  and then whatever else of this kind follows:
  482  why are these, unless because the vehement violence of wine
  483  is wont to confound the spirit within the body itself?
  484  But whatever things can be confused and hindered
  485  show that, if a somewhat harsher cause worked its way in,
  486  they would perish, deprived of future life.
  487  What is more, often, suddenly compelled by the force of disease,
  488  someone before our eyes, as if by a stroke of lightning,
  489  falls down and foams at the mouth, groans and trembles in his limbs,
  490  loses his wits, stiffens his sinews, writhes, pants
  491  irregularly, and wears out his limbs with tossing,
  492  no doubt because the force of the disease, dispersed through the limbs,
  493  foaming, drives and troubles the spirit, as on the salt sea
  494  the waves seethe under the strong force of the winds.
  495  Next, a groan is forced out, since the limbs are afflicted
  496  with pain, and in general because the seeds of voice
  497  are driven out and carried in a mass outside through the mouth
  498  where they are used, so to speak, to go, and where a road is built.
  499  Loss of wits comes about because the force of mind and spirit
  500  is thrown into confusion and, as I have shown, split apart,
  501  is scattered, torn asunder by that same poison.
  502  Then when the cause of the disease has turned back, and the sharp
  503  humor of the corrupted body has gone back into its hiding-places,
  504  then first, as if tottering, he gets up, and to all
  505  his senses gradually returns, and takes back his spirit.
  506  Since these, then, even within the body itself, are tossed
  507  by such great diseases, and suffer, torn apart in wretched ways,
  508  why do you believe that the same, without a body, in the open air,
  509  can pass their lifetime among the strong winds?
  510  And since the mind, like a sick body, is healed,
  511  as we discern, and we see that it can be turned by medicine,
  512  this too foretells that the mind lives a mortal life.
  513  For he must add parts, or shift them from their order,
  514  or at any rate take away some jot from the sum,
  515  for whoever sets about and begins to change the mind
  516  or seeks to bend any other nature whatever.
  517  But neither will it let parts be shifted in it, nor added,
  518  that which is immortal, nor will it let one jot flow away;
  519  for whatever is changed and passes out of its own bounds,
  520  this at once is the death of what it was before.
  521  Therefore the mind, whether it sickens, gives out
  522  mortal signs, as I have shown, or whether medicine turns it.
  523  So surely does the true thing seem to meet false reasoning
  524  and bar its escape as it goes
  525  and convict the false with a two-edged refutation.
  526  Finally, we often see a man go little by little
  527  and lose the vital sense limb by limb:
  528  first the toes on the feet and the nails turn livid,
  529  then the feet and legs die, then after that through the other
  530  limbs the tracks of chill death go creeping.
  531  Since this nature of the spirit is split and does not
  532  come forth whole at one time, it must be held mortal.
  533  But if perhaps you think it can draw itself inward
  534  through the limbs and gather its parts into one,
  535  and so withdraw sense from all the limbs,
  536  yet that place where so great a store of spirit
  537  is gathered ought to be seen to have greater sense;
  538  and since it is nowhere, no doubt, as we said before,
  539  the spirit, torn apart, is scattered abroad and so perishes.
  540  What is more, even if we chose to grant what is false
  541  and allow that the spirit can be massed in the body of those
  542  who leave the light, dying bit by bit,
  543  still you must admit the spirit is mortal,
  544  nor does it matter whether it perishes scattered through the air
  545  or, drawn in out of its parts, grows dull and brutish,
  546  since more and more, on every side, sense fails
  547  the whole man, and less and less of life remains on every side.

  548  And since the mind is a single part of a man and stays
  549  fixed in a certain place, just as the ears and eyes are,
  550  and the other senses that steer our life,
  551  and since, just as hand or eye or nostrils, set apart
  552  and severed from us, cannot feel or even be,
  553  but yet, in such a state, are abandoned in a short time,
  554  so the mind cannot by itself be without the body and the man
  555  himself, which seems to be a kind of vessel for it,
  556  or whatever else you would rather picture as more closely
  557  joined to it, since the body clings to it with a bond.
  558  Again, the living powers of body and mind
  559  have strength and enjoy life only joined to each other;
  560  for neither without the body can it put forth the motions of life,
  561  the nature of the mind, alone by itself, nor again
  562  can a body deprived of spirit endure and use its senses.
  563  Clearly, just as the eye itself, torn out by the roots,
  564  cannot make out any thing apart from the whole body,
  565  so the spirit and the mind by themselves seem to have no power.
  566  No wonder, since, mingled through veins and flesh,
  567  through sinews and bones, they are held in by the whole body,
  568  and their first-beginnings cannot leap apart freely
  569  across great intervals; therefore, shut in, they move
  570  with the sense-bearing motions which, outside the body,
  571  cast out into the breezes of air after death, they cannot move,
  572  because they are not held in by a like principle;
  573  for air would be a body, a living thing, if the spirit
  574  could hold itself together and shut itself within those motions
  575  which before it carried on in the sinews and in the body itself.
  576  Therefore again and again, when the whole covering of the body
  577  is undone and the breaths of life are cast out,
  578  you must admit that the senses of the mind are dissolved,
  579  and the spirit too, since the cause of the two is joined.
  580  Again, since the body cannot endure the parting
  581  from the spirit without rotting away in a foul stench,
  582  why do you doubt that, risen from deep within, from the depths,
  583  the force of the spirit has flowed out, diffused like smoke,
  584  and that therefore the body, changed and rotten, has collapsed
  585  in such great ruin, because the spirit's foundations have been moved
  586  deep from their place, and flow outward through the limbs
  587  and through all the windings of the passages there are in the body
  588  and its openings? So in many ways you can learn
  589  that the nature of the spirit went out through the limbs, divided,
  590  and was torn apart in itself within the body itself
  591  before it slipped out and swam forth into the breezes of air.
  592  What is more, while it still turns within the bounds of life,
  593  often, from some cause, the spirit seems nonetheless shaken
  594  and about to go, and to be loosed whole from the whole body,
  595  and the face seems to grow faint as at the final hour
  596  and all the limbs to drop, slack, from the bloodless body.
  597  Such is the case when it is said that it has gone ill with the mind,
  598  or that the spirit has failed; when now there is alarm and all
  599  long to catch hold again of the last bond of life;
  600  for then the mind and all the power of the spirit
  601  are shaken. And these totter together with the body itself,
  602  so that a cause a little heavier could dissolve them.
  603  Why do you doubt, then, that once driven out of the body,
  604  feeble, outside, in the open, its covering removed,
  605  not only could it not endure through all time
  606  but could not hold together for the least space of time?
  607  For no one dying seems to feel
  608  the spirit going out unharmed from the whole body,
  609  nor rising first to the throat and the gullet above,
  610  but failing, placed as it is in a fixed region,
  611  just as he knows the other senses dissolve, each in its own
  612  part. But if our mind were immortal,
  613  it would not, dying, so lament that it was being dissolved,
  614  but rather that it went out and left its garment, like a snake.
  615  Again, why is the thought and judgment of the mind never
  616  born in the head or the feet or hands, but in all men clings
  617  to one seat and to fixed regions,
  618  if fixed places are not assigned to each thing for its birth,
  619  and where each thing, once created, can endure,
  620  and so exist with its many-fashioned members so divided
  621  that the order of the limbs never appears reversed?
  622  So strictly does thing follow thing, and flame is not wont
  623  to be created in rivers, nor cold to be begotten in fire.
  624  Further, if the nature of the spirit is immortal
  625  and can feel when separated from our body,
  626  we must, I think, suppose it furnished with five senses.
  627  Nor by any other principle can we picture to ourselves
  628  the spirits below wandering in Acheron.
  629  And so the painters and the earlier generations of writers
  630  have brought spirits on the scene furnished thus with senses.
  631  But neither eyes nor nostrils nor the hand itself can exist
  632  apart for the spirit, nor apart the tongue, nor ears;
  633  therefore they cannot by themselves feel, or even be.
  634  And since we feel that vital sense is present in the whole
  635  body, and see that the whole is a living thing,
  636  if suddenly, with a swift blow, it is cut through the middle
  637  by some force, so as to sever the two halves apart,
  638  no doubt the force of the spirit too, divided
  639  and cleft, will be torn asunder together with the body.
  640  But what is cut and parts into any pieces at all
  641  plainly denies that its own nature is eternal.
  642  They tell how scythe-bearing chariots lop off limbs,
  643  often so suddenly, hot with the mingled slaughter,
  644  that there seems to quiver on the ground the part which from the limbs
  645  has fallen, cut away, while yet the mind and the man's force,
  646  through the mobility of the harm, cannot feel the pain;
  647  and at once, since the mind is given over to the zeal of battle,
  648  with what is left of the body it makes for battle and slaughter,
  649  and often does not grasp that its left arm is lost, shield and all,
  650  that wheels and ravening scythes have dragged off among the horses;
  651  another, that his right has fallen, as he climbs and presses on.
  652  Then another tries to rise when his leg is taken away,
  653  while nearby on the ground the dying foot works its toes.
  654  And the head cut off from the warm and living trunk
  655  keeps on the ground a living look and open eyes
  656  until it has given up all the remnants of the spirit.
  657  What is more, if, its tongue flickering, its tail threatening,
  658  a serpent's, with its long body, both of them
  659  it pleased you to cut into many pieces with the steel,
  660  you will see all the pieces, severed, each apart, with the wound
  661  still fresh, writhe and sprinkle the earth with gore,
  662  and the fore part turn back to seek itself with its mouth,
  663  to grip with its bite, struck by the burning pain of the wound.
  664  Shall we say, then, that there are whole spirits in all those
  665  small pieces? But on that principle it will follow
  666  that one living creature had many spirits in its body.
  667  Therefore that which was one has been divided along with
  668  the body; and so both must be thought mortal,
  669  since alike they are cut apart into many pieces.

  670  Further, if the nature of the spirit is immortal
  671  and works its way into the body at our birth,
  672  why can we not remember, besides, the age lived before,
  673  nor keep any traces of the things we have done?
  674  For if the power of the mind is changed so greatly
  675  that all holding of past things has fallen away from it,
  676  that, I think, wanders now not far from death;
  677  and so you must confess that what was before
  678  has perished, and that what is now has now been created.
  679  Further, if, when our body is already complete,
  680  the living power of the mind is wont to be brought in
  681  at the moment we are born and cross the threshold of life,
  682  it would not then be fitting that, with the body and together
  683  with the limbs, it should be seen to grow within the very blood,
  684  but fitting rather that it live alone, for itself, as in a cage,
  685  and yet in such a way that the whole body flows with sense.
  686  Therefore, again and again, we must not think that spirits
  687  are without a beginning, nor released from the law of death;
  688  for we must not think they could have been bound so closely
  689  to our bodies, having worked their way in from outside,
  690  a thing the plain fact shows to be wholly the reverse;
  691  for the spirit is so knit through veins, flesh, sinews,
  692  and bones, that even the teeth are given a share in sense,
  693  as toothache shows, and the twinge of icy water,
  694  and a rough stone bitten on, met suddenly in the bread;
  695  nor, since they are so closely woven in, do they seem able
  696  to go out unharmed and unloose themselves safe
  697  from all the sinews and the bones and joints.
  698  But if perhaps you think the spirit, working in from outside,
  699  is wont to make its way, seeping, through our limbs,
  700  so much the more surely will it perish, being fused with the body;
  701  for what seeps through is dissolved, and therefore perishes;
  702  for it is parted out through all the passages of the body.
  703  As food, when it is dispersed into all the limbs and frame,
  704  perishes and supplies another nature out of itself,
  705  so spirit and mind, however whole they go fresh
  706  into the body, are yet dissolved in seeping through,
  707  while, as through passages, there are dispersed into all the frame
  708  the tiny parts of which this nature of the mind is created,
  709  the nature which now has mastery in our body, born
  710  from that which perished then, parted through the frame.
  711  Therefore it seems the nature of the spirit is neither
  712  deprived of a birthday nor exempt from a funeral.
  713  Again, are seeds of the spirit left behind, or not,
  714  in the lifeless body? If they are left and are in it,
  715  there will be no way it can rightly be held immortal,
  716  since it has gone off diminished, with parts lost.
  717  But if it has fled away, taken off with its parts so intact
  718  that it has left no parts of itself in the body,
  719  from where do corpses, their flesh now going rancid,
  720  breathe out worms, and from where so great a host of living things,
  721  boneless and bloodless, surge through the swollen frame?
  722  But if perhaps you believe that spirits work their way in from outside
  723  into worms, and can come one by one into bodies,
  724  and do not reckon why many thousands of spirits
  725  should gather where one has withdrawn, still there is this
  726  that it seems must be asked and brought to decision:
  727  whether, in the end, the spirits hunt down each of the seeds
  728  of little worms and build themselves a place to be,
  729  or work their way, as it were, into bodies already complete.
  730  But why they should do it themselves, or why they should labor,
  731  there is no saying. For indeed, when they are without a body,
  732  they do not flit about troubled by diseases and cold and hunger;
  733  for it is the body, more akin to these ills, that suffers them,
  734  and the mind endures many evils by contact with it.
  735  But grant it as useful as you like for them to make a body
  736  when they come in under; yet there seems no way they could.
  737  Therefore spirits do not make bodies and frames for themselves.
  738  Nor yet is there a way they could work their way into bodies
  739  already complete; for neither could they be finely
  740  knit in, nor will contact be made through shared feeling.
  741  Again, why does fierce violence go with the grim breed
  742  of lions, cunning with foxes, and flight with deer,
  743  handed down from their sires, their sires' fear spurring their frame,
  744  and why indeed do all other things of this kind
  745  come to be in limbs and temper from the beginning of life,
  746  if not because, fixed by its own seed and breed,
  747  the force of the mind grows equally with every body?
  748  But if it were immortal and used to change
  749  bodies, living things would have mixed characters:
  750  the dog of Hyrcanian seed would often flee
  751  the charge of the horned stag, and the hawk would tremble
  752  through the breezes of the air, fleeing at the coming of the dove,
  753  men would lack sense, and the wild generations of wild beasts be wise.
  754  For that rests on false reasoning, when they say
  755  an immortal spirit is altered when its body is changed;
  756  for what is changed is dissolved, and therefore perishes;
  757  for its parts are transposed and shift from their order;
  758  therefore they must be able to be dissolved through the frame too,
  759  so that in the end they all perish together with the body.
  760  But if they say that the spirits of men always pass
  761  into human bodies, still I shall ask why from wise
  762  a spirit could turn foolish, and why no boy is prudent,
  763  if not because, fixed by its own seed and breed,
  764  nor the mare's foal as well trained as the strong horse's force.
  765  No doubt they will take refuge in this: that in a tender body
  766  the mind grows tender. But if this happens, you must confess
  767  that the spirit is mortal, since, changed throughout the frame,
  768  it loses so greatly its former life and sense.
  769  Or how will it be able, in step with every body,
  770  to grow strong and touch the longed-for flower of age,
  771  this force of the mind, unless it is a partner at the first origin?
  772  Or why does it wish to go out of limbs grown old?
  773  Does it fear to stay shut up in a rotting body,
  774  fear that its house, worn out by the long length of years,
  775  may fall in on it? But there are no dangers for the immortal.
  776  Again, that spirits stand ready at the couplings of Venus
  777  and the births of beasts seems a thing to laugh at,
  778  immortals waiting for mortal limbs
  779  in number beyond number, and contending headlong
  780  among themselves which first and before all shall work its way in;
  781  unless perhaps the covenants of spirits are so agreed
  782  that whichever comes flying first works its way in
  783  first, and they do not strive with their strength one whit.
  784  Again, there can be no tree in the upper air, no clouds
  785  in the deep sea, nor can fish live in the fields,
  786  nor blood be in wood, nor sap in stones.
  787  It is fixed and ordered where each thing grows and is.
  788  So the nature of the mind cannot arise without the body,
  789  alone, nor exist far off from sinews and blood.
  790  For if it could, the force of the mind itself, much sooner,
  791  could be in the head or shoulders or the lowest heels,
  792  and would be wont to be born in any part you please,
  793  and still remain in the same man and the same vessel.
  794  And since even in our body it is evidently fixed
  795  and ordered, it seems, where each can be and grow,
  796  spirit and mind, each apart, all the more must we deny
  797  that it can, entire, endure outside the body, or be born.
  798  Therefore, when the body has perished, you must confess
  799  that the spirit has perished, torn apart through the whole body.
  800  For indeed, to join the mortal to the eternal, and to think
  801  that they can feel together and act each upon each,
  802  is folly; for what must be thought more at odds
  803  or more disjoined from each other and more discordant
  804  than that what is mortal, joined to the immortal
  805  and everlasting in union, should bear savage storms?
  806  Further, whatever things endure eternal must, of necessity,
  807  either, because they are of solid body, repel blows
  808  and suffer nothing to enter them that could tear apart
  809  their close-knit parts within, as the bodies of matter
  810  are, whose nature we have shown before;
  811  or be able to last through all time for this reason,
  812  that they are exempt from blows, as the void is,
  813  which stays untouched and is not affected one whit by a stroke;
  814  or else because there is no supply of place around them
  815  into which things could, as it were, part and be dissolved,
  816  as the sum of sums is eternal, nor is there outside it
  817  any place into which they might scatter, nor are there bodies that
  818  could fall upon them and dissolve them with a strong blow.

  819  But if perhaps it should rather be held immortal for this reason,
  820  that it is kept fortified by the things that give it life,
  821  either because things foreign to its safety do not come at all,
  822  or because those that do come are by some means driven back
  823  and retreat before we can feel what harm they do,

  824  for besides that it sickens along with the body's diseases,
  825  there comes that which often torments it about things to come,
  826  keeps it wretched in fear, and wears it out with cares,
  827  and for misdeeds in the past its sins gnaw it with remorse.
  828  Add the madness proper to the mind, and its forgetfulness of things;
  829  add that it is plunged into the black waves of lethargy.
  830  Death, therefore, is nothing to us, and concerns us not a whit,
  831  since the nature of the mind is held to be mortal.
  832  And just as in time gone by we felt no distress
  833  when the Carthaginians were coming from every side to the clash,
  834  when all things, shaken by the trembling tumult of war,
  835  shuddered and quaked beneath the high breezes of the upper air,
  836  and it stood in doubt under which side's rule all men
  837  must fall, all that is human, by land and by sea,
  838  so, when we shall not be, when the parting of body and spirit
  839  has come about, from which we are joined and fitted into one,
  840  surely nothing at all, to us who then shall not be,
  841  will be able to happen or to move our sense,
  842  not if earth should be mingled with sea, and sea with sky.
  843  And even if it does feel, after it is torn from our body,
  844  the nature of the mind along with the power of the spirit,
  845  still it is nothing to us, who by the joining and wedlock
  846  of body and spirit exist, joined and fitted into one.
  847  Nor, if time should gather up our matter
  848  after death and bring it back again as it is now placed,
  849  and the lights of life should once more be given us,
  850  would even that event concern us in any way at all,
  851  once the recollection of ourselves has been broken off.
  852  And as it is, nothing touches us of ourselves, we who before
  853  existed, nor does anguish over those selves affect us now.
  854  For when you look back over the whole of measureless time,
  855  its past expanse, and then how manifold
  856  the motions of matter are, you could easily believe this,
  857  that seeds have often been set in the same order as now,
  858  these very seeds of which we now consist, in times before.
  859  And yet we cannot recapture this with remembering mind;
  860  for a pause of life has been thrown between, and all the motions
  861  have wandered off at random, astray, far from the senses.
  862  For if there is to be misery and distress, perhaps,
  863  he too must himself exist at that time, to whom ill can
  864  befall. Since death takes this away, and prevents the existence
  865  of him to whom these troubles could be brought together,
  866  we may know that there is nothing to be feared in death,
  867  that he who is not cannot be wretched, and that not a whit
  868  it differs whether he had never been born at any time,
  869  when immortal death has taken away his mortal life.
  870  Therefore, when you see a man indignant on his own behalf
  871  that after death he will either rot, his body laid away,
  872  or perish in flames or in the jaws of wild beasts,
  873  you may know that he does not ring true, and that there lurks
  874  some blind goad in his heart, however much he himself denies
  875  that he believes any sense will remain to him in death.
  876  For he does not, I think, grant what he professes, nor its ground,
  877  nor tear himself up by the roots from life and cast himself out,
  878  but, unknowing, makes something of himself survive.
  879  For when each man, alive, pictures to himself the future,
  880  that birds and beasts will tear his body in death,
  881  he pities himself; for he does not separate himself from it,
  882  nor withdraw enough from the body flung away, and that body
  883  he fancies is himself, and, standing by, taints with his own sense.
  884  Hence he resents that he was created mortal,
  885  and does not see that in real death there will be no other self
  886  who could, alive, mourn for himself destroyed,
  887  and, standing, grieve that he lies there, torn or burned.
  888  For if it is an evil in death to be mauled by the jaws and bite
  889  of wild beasts, I do not find how it is not bitter
  890  to be laid on the fire and scorched in hot flames,
  891  or to be placed in honey and stifled, and to stiffen
  892  with cold, lying on the top surface of an icy slab,
  893  or to be pressed from above, buried under the weight of earth.
  894  'Now, now no glad home will receive you, nor your excellent
  895  wife, nor will your sweet children run to snatch
  896  your kisses first and touch your heart with silent sweetness.
  897  You will not be able to guard your flourishing affairs
  898  and your own. Wretched man,' they say, 'wretchedly, all of them,
  899  one hostile day has taken from you, so many prizes of life.'
  900  This they do not add in these matters: 'nor does any longing
  901  for those things any longer remain with you as well.'
  902  If they saw this rightly in mind and followed it in their words,
  903  they would free themselves from great anguish and fear of mind.
  904  'You indeed, as you are now, laid asleep in death, so will you be
  905  for all the time that remains, freed from every grievous pain;
  906  but we, beside the horrifying pyre, you turned to ash,
  907  have wept insatiably, and for all eternity
  908  no day will take the sorrow from our hearts.'
  909  This, then, must be asked of him: what is so very bitter
  910  in it, if it all comes down to sleep and rest,
  911  that anyone should waste away in eternal grief.
  912  This too men often do, when they recline at table and hold
  913  their cups, and shade their faces with garlands:
  914  they say from the heart: 'Brief is this fruit for poor little men;
  915  soon it will have been, and never after can it be called back.'
  916  As if in death this were chief among their evils,
  917  that thirst should burn the wretches and parch them dry,
  918  or that longing for some other thing should sit upon them.
  919  For no one misses himself and his life then,
  920  when mind and body alike lie quiet in sleep;
  921  for as far as we care, that sleep could be everlasting,
  922  and no longing for ourselves touches us;
  923  and yet by no means then throughout our limbs do those
  924  first-beginnings wander far from the sense-bearing motions,
  925  when a man, startled from sleep, gathers himself together.
  926  Much less, then, must we think that death is to us,
  927  if there can be less than what we see to be nothing;
  928  for a greater scattering of the throng of matter
  929  follows on death, and no one wakes and stands up again
  930  whom once the cold pause of life has followed.

  931  Finally, if the nature of things should suddenly find a voice
  932  and should herself rebuke one of us in this way:
  933  'What ails you so, mortal, that you give yourself up too far
  934  to sick grieving? Why do you groan at death and weep?
  935  For if the life you have lived till now was welcome to you,
  936  and all its good things have not, as into a vessel full of holes,
  937  run through and perished unenjoyed,
  938  why not withdraw like a guest who has had his fill of life,
  939  and with an even mind, you fool, take your untroubled rest?
  940  But if whatever you enjoyed has been poured out and lost,
  941  and life offends you, why do you seek to add more,
  942  only to be lost badly again and perish all unenjoyed,
  943  and not rather make an end of life and of labor?
  944  For there is nothing more that I can devise and find for you
  945  to please you: all things are always the same.
  946  If your body is not yet withering with years, and your limbs
  947  are not worn out and slack, still all things remain the same,
  948  if you should go on living to outlast all generations,
  949  and still more if you were never going to die.'
  950  What do we answer, except that nature presses a just suit
  951  and sets out a true case in her words?
  952  But if now someone older, well on in years, should complain
  953  and lament his death, poor wretch, more than is right,
  954  would she not rightly cry out louder and rebuke in a sharp voice:
  955  'Away with your tears, you bottomless pit, and check your complaints.
  956  You have had all the prizes of life, and now you wither;
  957  but since you always crave what is absent and scorn what is here,
  958  your life has slipped away from you unfinished and unenjoyed,
  959  and death, unlooked for, has taken its stand at your head
  960  before you can depart full and sated with things.
  961  As it is, let go of all things that do not suit your age,
  962  and with an even mind, come, yield to greater ones: you must.'
  963  Rightly, I think, she would plead, rightly rebuke and reproach;
  964  for the old, thrust out by the newness of things, gives way
  965  always, and one thing must be restored out of others.
  966  No one is handed down into the Pit or to black Tartarus;
  967  matter is needed, so that later generations may grow;
  968  and yet they all, their life lived through, will follow you;
  969  and so, no less than you, these have fallen before and will fall.
  970  So one thing will never cease to rise from another,
  971  and life is given to none to own, to all to use.
  972  Look back, again, how the age gone by of eternal time
  973  before we are born was nothing to us.
  974  This, then, is the mirror nature holds up to us of the time
  975  to come, after our death at last.
  976  Does anything horrible appear there, does anything seem
  977  grim? Is it not more untroubled than any sleep?
  978  And indeed whatever things are told to exist
  979  in deep Acheron, they are all here, in our life.
  980  No wretched Tantalus fears the great rock hanging in the air,
  981  as the story goes, numb with empty dread;
  982  rather, in life an empty fear of the gods oppresses
  983  mortals, and they fear the fall that chance may bring to each.
  984  Nor do birds enter Tityos as he lies in Acheron,
  985  nor can they truly find anything to search for
  986  beneath his great breast through all eternity.
  987  However vast the sprawl of his body as it lies there,
  988  though with outspread limbs it should cover not just nine acres
  989  but the whole circle of the earth,
  990  still he could not bear eternal pain
  991  nor furnish food from his own body forever.
  992  But Tityos is here among us: the one lying in love,
  993  whom birds tear, whom anxious anguish eats away,
  994  or cares rend through with some other craving.
  995  Sisyphus too is here in life before our eyes,
  996  the man who seeks from the people the rods and the savage axes,
  997  thirsting for them, and always withdraws beaten and grim.
  998  For to seek power, which is empty and is never given,
  999  and always to endure hard labor for it,
 1000  this is to push uphill, straining against the mountain,
 1001  a rock which yet from the very summit rolls back again
 1002  and rushes headlong for the levels of the open plain.
 1003  Then, always to feed the ungrateful nature of the mind,
 1004  to fill it with good things and never satisfy it,
 1005  as the seasons of the year do for us when they come
 1006  round again and bring their offspring and their varied charms,
 1007  and yet we are never filled with the fruits of life,
 1008  this, I think, is what they mean by the girls in the flower of age
 1009  who, they tell, pour water into a vessel full of holes,
 1010  which yet can by no means be filled.
 1011  Cerberus, too, and the Furies, and the lack of light,
 1012  Tartarus belching horrible blasts of heat from its jaws!
 1013  These are nowhere, and indeed cannot be;
 1014  but in life there is fear of punishment for evil deeds,
 1015  notable as the deeds are notable, and atonement for crime:
 1016  prison, and the horrible hurling down from the rock,
 1017  scourges, executioners, the dungeon, pitch, hot plates, torches;
 1018  and even if these are absent, the mind, conscious of its deeds,
 1019  fearing beforehand, applies goads and scorches itself with whips,
 1020  and does not see meanwhile what limit to its evils there
 1021  can be, nor what end at last to its punishments,
 1022  and fears that these same things may grow heavier in death.
 1023  Here, in the end, the life of fools becomes an Acheron.
 1024  This too you might at times say to yourself:
 1025  'Even good Ancus has left the light with his eyes,
 1026  who was better than you, you shameless one, in many things.
 1027  Since then many other kings and lords of things
 1028  have perished, who ruled over great nations.
 1029  He too, the very one who once laid a road across the great sea
 1030  and gave his legions a way to go over the deep
 1031  and taught them to cross the salt lagoons on foot
 1032  and, trampling with his horses, scorned the roar of the sea,
 1033  robbed of the light, poured out his spirit from a dying body.
 1034  The son of the Scipios, thunderbolt of war, terror of Carthage,
 1035  gave his bones to the earth as if he were the lowest servant.
 1036  Add the discoverers of learning and of graces,
 1037  add the companions of the Heliconian Muses; among them Homer,
 1038  who alone held the scepter, sleeps in the same rest as the others.
 1039  Finally Democritus, after ripe old age
 1040  warned him that the remembering motions of his mind were slackening,
 1041  went of his own will to meet death and offered up his head.
 1042  Epicurus himself died, the light of his life run through,
 1043  he who surpassed the human race in genius and put out
 1044  all the stars, as the sun in the ether does, once risen.
 1045  And will you hesitate, and resent it, to die?
 1046  You, whose life is all but dead while you still live and see,
 1047  who waste the greater part of your life in sleep,
 1048  and snore while awake, and never cease to see dreams,
 1049  and carry a mind troubled with empty dread,
 1050  and often cannot find what is wrong with you, when,
 1051  drunk, poor wretch, you are pressed by many cares on every side
 1052  and drift wandering in the wavering error of your mind.'

 1053  If men could, just as they plainly feel
 1054  a weight lies in the mind, wearing them out with its heaviness,
 1055  also come to know from what causes it arises, and from where
 1056  so great a mass, as it were, of evil stands in the breast,
 1057  they would not live their lives as now we mostly see them,
 1058  each not knowing what he wants for himself, and always seeking
 1059  to change his place, as if he could set his burden down.
 1060  Often he goes out of doors from his great house,
 1061  who is weary to death of home, and suddenly comes back,
 1062  since indeed he feels himself no better off outside.
 1063  He races to his villa, driving his ponies headlong,
 1064  as if pressing on to bring help to a house on fire;
 1065  he yawns at once, the moment he has touched the villa's threshold,
 1066  or goes off heavily into sleep and looks for forgetfulness,
 1067  or even hurries back to make for the city and see it again.
 1068  So each man flees himself, yet that self, as happens, of course,
 1069  he cannot escape; he clings to it against his will, and hates it,
 1070  because, sick as he is, he does not grasp the cause of his disease;
 1071  if he saw it clearly, each man would leave his affairs behind
 1072  and study first of all to know the nature of things,
 1073  since it is the state of eternal time, not of a single hour,
 1074  that is in question, the state in which mortals must spend all
 1075  the age that remains to be waited out after death.
 1076  Finally, to tremble so much amid doubtful dangers,
 1077  what great and evil craving for life compels us?
 1078  Surely an end of life stands waiting for mortals,
 1079  and death cannot be avoided: we must go to meet it.
 1080  Further, we turn about in the same place and stay there always,
 1081  and no new pleasure is hammered out by living;
 1082  but while what we long for is absent, it seems to surpass
 1083  the rest; then, when that has come, we long for something else,
 1084  and an equal thirst for life holds us, always gaping.
 1085  And it is in doubt what fortune the coming age will carry,
 1086  or what chance will bring us, or what end is at hand.
 1087  Nor by prolonging life do we take away one jot
 1088  from the time of death, nor can we subtract anything from it
 1089  so that, once destroyed, we might perhaps be dead for less long.
 1090  Therefore you may live out as many generations as you like;
 1091  that eternal death will be waiting for you none the less,
 1092  and he will be no more for no less long who, with today's
 1093  light, made an end of life, than he
 1094  who perished many months and years before.
