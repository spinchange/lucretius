    1  I range through the pathless places of the Pierides, trodden before
    2  by no one's foot. It is a joy to come to untouched springs
    3  and drink from them, a joy to pluck new flowers
    4  and to seek for my head a crown of distinction from there
    5  where the Muses have veiled no one's temples before;
    6  first because I teach of great things, and go on
    7  to free the mind from the tight-tied knots of religion,
    8  then because on a dark matter I compose such lucid
    9  verses, touching all of it with the Muses' charm.
   10  For this too seems not to be without good reason;
   11  for just as physicians, with foul wormwood for children,
   12  when they try to give it, first touch the rims round the cups
   13  with the sweet and golden liquid of honey,
   14  so that the unforeseeing age of children may be fooled
   15  as far as the lips, and meanwhile drink down the bitter
   16  juice of wormwood, and, deceived, may not be undone,
   17  but rather, restored by such means, may grow strong,
   18  so I now, since this reasoning often seems
   19  rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd
   20  shrinks back from it, have wished, in sweet-speaking
   21  Pierian song, to set out for you our reasoning
   22  and, as it were, to touch it with the Muses' sweet honey,
   23  if by such means I might perhaps hold your mind
   24  on our verses, while you perceive the whole
   25  nature of things and feel its usefulness through and through.
   26  But since I have taught what the origins of all things
   27  are like, and how, differing in their various shapes,
   28  they fly of their own accord, driven by eternal motion,
   29  and in what way each thing can be created from them,
   30  I shall now begin to treat for you what bears closely on these things:
   31  that there exist what we call the images of things,
   32  which are to be named, as it were, films or bark;
   33  and since I have taught what the nature of the mind is,
   34  and from what things, joined with the body, it is made and thrives,
   35  and in what way, torn apart, it returns to its first-beginnings,
   36  I shall now begin to treat for you what bears closely on these things:
   37  that there exist what we call the images of things,
   38  because the image carries a likeness and form like that thing's
   39  from whose body, whatever it is, it is said to be shed and wander;
   40  which, like films torn from the outermost body of things,
   41  fly this way and that through the air,
   42  and these same, meeting us when we are awake, terrify
   43  our minds, and in sleep too, when often we behold shapes
   44  strange to see, and the images of those who lack the light,
   45  which have often roused us in horror as we lay languid
   46  in sleep: lest we should think by chance that spirits escape
   47  from Acheron, or that shades fly among the living,
   48  or that something of us can be left behind after death,
   49  when the body and the nature of the mind, destroyed together,
   50  have gone their separate ways, each into its own first-beginnings.
   51  I say, therefore, that effigies of things and thin shapes
   52  are sent off from things, from their outermost bark;
   53  which one may come to know from this, however dull one's heart.
   54  To begin with, since among things open to view many things
   55  send off bodies, some diffused loosely abroad,
   56  as logs send off smoke and fires heat,
   57  and some more closely woven and dense, as when at times
   58  cicadas put off their smooth coats in summer,
   59  and when calves at birth send off films
   60  from the outermost body, and again when the slippery serpent
   61  strips off its garment among the thorns; for we often see
   62  the brambles decked with their fluttering spoils.
   63  And since these things happen, a thin image too must
   64  be sent off from things, from the outermost body of things.
   65  For why those should fall and withdraw from things more
   66  than those that are thin, there is no power to open one's mouth;
   67  especially since on the surface of things there are bodies
   68  many and minute, which could be thrown off in the same order
   69  they had before and keep the outline of the form,
   70  and much more swiftly, inasmuch as they can be less hindered,
   71  being few and placed in the very front.
   72  For certainly we see many things thrown off and lavished,
   73  not only from the depth and from within, as we said before,
   74  but from the surface, often, the very color too.
   75  And this is commonly done by awnings, yellow and red
   76  and dark blue, when stretched over great theaters
   77  they flap and quiver, spread out on masts and beams;
   78  for there the assembly seated in the tiers beneath, and all
   79  the face of the stage, the fathers and the mothers down below,
   80  they stain, and make them ripple with their color.
   81  And the more closely the walls of the theater are shut in
   82  all around, the more everything inside, steeped in charm,
   83  laughs, with the light of day drawn in.
   84  Therefore, since linens send off a dye from their surface,
   85  each thing too must send off thin effigies,
   86  since in both cases they are thrown from the surface.
   87  There are therefore by now sure traces of forms,
   88  which commonly fly about, endowed with a fine thread,
   89  and cannot be seen one by one, apart.
   90  Further, every smell, smoke, heat, and other things
   91  of the kind stream out from things diffused for this reason:
   92  because while they come out from the depths, risen to the outside,
   93  they are torn apart on their winding path, and there are no straight
   94  mouths to the roads by which, once risen, they can strive to get out.
   95  But on the other hand, when a thin film of surface color
   96  is thrown off, there is nothing that can tear it apart,
   97  since it is ready at hand, placed in the very front.
   98  Finally, whatever images appear to us in mirrors,
   99  in water, and in every shining surface, must,
  100  since they are endowed with a likeness like that of things,
  101  consist of images sent off from those things.
  102  For why those should fall and withdraw from things more
  103  than those that are thin, there is no power to open one's mouth.
  104  There are therefore thin effigies of forms, and like them,
  105  which, though no one can make them out
  106  one by one, yet thrown back by constant and repeated rebounding
  107  return a sight from the level surface of mirrors;
  108  and in no other way, it seems, could they be preserved
  109  so that shapes so like each thing are returned.
  110  Come now, how thin a nature the image is made of,
  111  learn. And in the first place, since the first-beginnings are so far
  112  beneath our senses, and so much smaller
  113  than those things which the eyes first begin to be unable to see,
  114  yet now, so that I may confirm this too, how fine the origins
  115  of all things are, learn in a few words.
  116  First, there are living creatures already so tiny that a third part
  117  of them could by no means be seen.
  118  What must one think any one of their entrails is like!
  119  What of the heart's ball, or the eye's? What of the limbs? The joints?
  120  How tiny they are! And what, further, of each first-beginning
  121  of which the spirit and the nature of the mind must consist?
  122  Do you not see how fine they are, and how minute?

  127  Why not rather recognize that images of things wander about,
  128  many, in many ways, with no force, and void of sense?
  129  But lest you perhaps think that only those wander about,
  130  whatever images of things withdraw from things,
  131  there are also those that are born of their own accord and themselves
  132  take shape in this sky, which is called air,
  133  which, formed in many ways, are borne on high,
  134  as we see clouds at times easily gather on high
  135  and mar the calm face of the world,
  136  caressing the air with their motion; for often the faces of Giants
  137  seem to fly and draw a shadow far and wide,
  138  at times great mountains and rocks torn from
  139  the mountains seem to go before and pass across the sun,
  140  then a beast to drag and draw on other storm-clouds.
  141  Nor do they cease, melting, to change their likeness
  142  and turn into the outlines of forms of every kind.

  143  Now, by how easy and how swift a principle these are produced,
  144  and how they flow perpetually from things and slip off and withdraw,

  145  for always there is something streaming off the surface of things
  146  for them to cast away. And when this reaches other things,
  147  it passes through, as above all through cloth; but when it meets rough rocks
  148  or comes upon the matter of wood, there at once
  149  it is torn apart, so that it can give back no image.
  150  But when things that are bright and dense are set against it,
  151  as above all a mirror is, none of this befalls;
  152  for it can neither pass through them, as through cloth, nor yet
  153  be torn apart; a safety their smoothness never fails to secure.
  154  And that is why the images stream back to us from there.
  155  And however suddenly, at whatever time, you set
  156  any thing against a mirror, an image appears;
  157  so you may know that from the surface there flow perpetually
  158  thin textures of things and thin shapes.
  159  Therefore many images are produced in a brief space,
  160  so that the origin of these things may rightly be called swift.
  161  And just as the sun must send up many lights
  162  in a brief space, so that all things may be perpetually full,
  163  so likewise, on a like principle, it must be that from things
  164  in a moment of time images of things are carried,
  165  many, in many ways, in all directions on every side;
  166  since to whatever quarter we turn a mirror's face,
  167  things answer there with like shape and like color.
  168  Further, when the weather of the sky has just been at its clearest,
  169  quite suddenly it turns foully turbid,
  170  so that you would think all the darkness on every side had left
  171  Acheron and filled the great caverns of the sky.
  172  So utterly, when the hideous night of storm clouds has risen,
  173  do the faces of black Terror hang over us from above;
  174  yet how tiny a part of these an image is, there is no one
  175  who could say, or render an account of it in words.
  176  Come now, with what swift motion the images are carried,
  177  and what mobility is granted them as they swim through the air,
  178  so that a long distance is worn away in a brief hour,
  179  toward whatever place each strains with its diverse bent,
  180  I shall set out in verses sweet-spoken rather than many;
  181  as the small song of the swan is better than that clamor
  182  of cranes scattered among the high clouds of the south wind.
  183  To begin with, very often one may see that things that are light
  184  and made of minute bodies are swift.
  185  Of this kind is the light of the sun and its heat,
  186  because they are made of minute first bodies
  187  which are, as it were, hammered on, and through the interval of air
  188  do not hesitate to pass, driven on by the blow that follows;
  189  for at once light is supplied by light,
  190  and flash is goaded on by flash, as if by a team in tandem.
  191  Therefore images, on a like principle, must
  192  be able to run across a space beyond telling
  193  in a moment of time, first because there is a very small cause
  194  far behind them to carry them forward and drive them on,
  195  for the rest, since they are carried with so winged a lightness;
  196  next, because they are sent out endowed with so rare
  197  a texture that they can easily penetrate any things
  198  and, as it were, seep through the interval of air.
  199  Further, if those small bodies of things that from deep within
  200  and from far inside are sent out, like the sun's light
  201  and heat, are seen in a moment of the day to glide
  202  and spread themselves through the whole space of heaven
  203  and fly across sea and lands and flood the sky,
  204  what then of those that stand ready at the very front,
  205  when they are cast off and nothing hinders their release?
  206  Do you not see how much more swiftly and farther they must go
  207  and run through many times the extent of space in the same
  208  time in which the sun's lights spread through heaven.
  209  This too above all seems a true proof
  210  of how swift the motion is with which images of things are carried:
  211  that as soon as a gleaming sheet of water is laid down
  212  under the open sky, at once, when the sky is starry, the calm
  213  stars of the world answer, radiant in the water.
  214  Now do you see, then, in what a moment of time an image
  215  falls from the shores of the ether to the shores of the earth?
  216  Therefore again and again you must confess that bodies
  217  are sent out that strike the eyes and provoke the sight.
  218  And from certain things odors flow perpetually,
  219  as cold from rivers, heat from the sun, surge from the waves
  220  of the sea, eater of walls along the shores,
  221  and various voices never cease to fly through the air.
  222  Finally, a moisture of salty flavor often comes into the mouth
  223  when we walk beside the sea, and when we watch
  224  wormwood being diluted and mixed, its bitterness touches us.
  225  So true is it that from all things each thing is carried off
  226  in a stream, and sent away in all directions on every side,
  227  and no delay, no rest is granted in the flowing,
  228  since we perpetually feel, and may always
  229  see all things, smell them, and sense them sounding.
  230  Further, since a certain shape, handled with the hands
  231  in the dark, is known to be the same as the one
  232  seen in the light and the clear brightness, it must be
  233  that touch and sight are moved by a like cause.
  234  Now then, if we feel a square thing and it
  235  stirs us in the dark, what thing in the light could
  236  fall square upon our sight, if not its image?
  237  Therefore the cause of seeing is seen to lie
  238  in images, and without them no thing can be seen.
  239  Now these which I call images of things are carried
  240  everywhere, and are cast out spread in all directions;
  241  but because we can see with the eyes alone,
  242  it comes about that wherever we turn our gaze, all things
  243  there strike against it with shape and color.
  244  And how far each thing stands away from us, the image
  245  makes us see, and takes care that we distinguish it;
  246  for when it is sent off, at once it thrusts and drives before it
  247  whatever air is placed between itself and the eyes,
  248  and so all this air glides through our eyes
  249  and, as it were, sweeps the pupils, and so passes through.
  250  That is why we see how far off each thing
  251  stands. And the more air is driven on before it,
  252  and the longer the breeze that sweeps our eyes,
  253  the farther off each thing is seen to stand removed.
  254  Of course these things come about on a principle supremely swift,
  255  so that we see what a thing is, and at once how far off it stands.
  256  In these matters it must not be thought at all a wonder
  257  why, though the images that strike the eyes cannot
  258  be seen one by one, the things themselves are perceived.
  259  For when the wind too lashes us little by little, and when
  260  sharp cold flows, we are not used to feel each single
  261  small part of the wind and of its cold,
  262  but rather the whole at once, and we see blows come about
  263  then on our body just as if some thing were
  264  lashing it and giving a sense of its own body outside.
  265  Again, when we strike a stone with a finger, we touch
  266  the very outermost surface of the rock, its uppermost color,
  267  yet we do not feel that by touch; rather we feel
  268  the hardness itself, deep within the rock, far down.

  269  Come now, learn why the image is seen beyond the mirror;
  270  for certainly it is seen withdrawn deep within.
  271  It is like those things outside that are truly seen through
  272  when a doorway offers through itself an open view
  273  and lets many things outside be seen from within the house;
  274  for that sight too comes about through a double, twin air.
  275  For first, on this side of the doorposts, the air is discerned,
  276  next the doors themselves follow, to right and left,
  277  after that an outer light sweeps through the eyes, and a second
  278  air, and those things outside that are truly seen through.
  279  So, when the image of the mirror first has cast itself forth,
  280  while it comes to our sight, it thrusts and drives along
  281  all the air that is set between itself and our eyes,
  282  and brings it about that we can perceive all this air
  283  before the mirror; but when we have perceived the mirror too,
  284  at once the image that is carried from us into it
  285  arrives, and, thrown back, revisits our eyes,
  286  and, driving another air before it, rolls it on,
  287  and makes us see this air before itself, and for that reason
  288  it seems to stand removed from the mirror by just so much.
  289  Therefore again and again it is right to wonder not at all

  290  at those that give back a sight from the level surface of mirrors,
  291  since in either case the thing is brought about by two airs.
  292  Now, that part of our limbs which is on the right
  293  comes to be seen in mirrors on the left, because
  294  when the image, coming, strikes against the mirror's flat,
  295  it is not turned about unharmed, but is dashed straight
  296  backward, as if someone, before it was dry,
  297  should dash a clay mask against a pillar or a beam,
  298  and it should at once keep its features straight in front
  299  and, dashed back, should press out its own form in reverse.
  300  It will come about that the eye that was the right before
  301  is now the left, and the left in exchange the right.
  302  It comes about too that an image is passed from mirror to mirror,
  303  so that even five or six images are accustomed to be made.
  304  For whatever things lie hidden back in the inner part of a house,
  305  even so, however winding and deep withdrawn the way,
  306  all can be brought out through crooked passages
  307  and by many mirrors be seen to be within the house.
  308  So thoroughly does the image shine across from mirror to mirror,
  309  and when it is handed on as left, it becomes right again,
  310  then it turns back again and returns to the same.
  311  Further, whatever little sides of mirrors there are
  312  endowed with a curvature like that of our own side
  313  send back to us images that are right on that account,
  314  either because the image is carried across from mirror to mirror
  315  and from there, dashed twice, flies to us, or else because
  316  the image is whirled around as it comes, since
  317  the curved shape of the mirror teaches it to turn toward us.
  318  Further, images walk forward in step with us, and set
  319  their foot down with ours, you would believe, and imitate our gesture,
  320  because, from whatever part of the mirror you withdraw,
  321  from there at once the images cannot return;
  322  since nature compels all things to be carried back
  323  and to leap back from things, returned at equal angles.
  324  Further, the eyes shun bright things and avoid looking at them.
  325  The sun even blinds them, if you persist in straining toward it,
  326  because its own force is great, and from on high
  327  images are carried through the clear air
  328  and strike the eyes, throwing their structure into confusion.
  329  Further, whatever brightness is keen often scorches
  330  the eyes, because it holds many seeds of fire
  331  which, working their way in, beget pain in the eyes.
  332  Further, all things turn sallow that are looked upon
  333  by the jaundiced, since seeds of sallowness from their body
  334  flow forth in numbers to meet the images of things,
  335  and many, finally, are mingled in their eyes,
  336  which with their contagion paint all things with pallor.
  337  Again, from darkness we see the things that are in light
  338  because, when the nearer air of the gloom,
  339  the black air, enters the eyes first and takes hold of them open,
  340  at once the shining, bright air follows after,
  341  which as it were purges them and scatters the black shadows
  342  of that air; for this one is by many degrees
  343  more mobile, by many finer, and more powerful.
  344  As soon as it has filled the eyes' passages with light
  345  and opened those that before the air had laid siege to,
  346  the black air, at once there follow the images of things
  347  that are placed in light, and provoke us to see.
  348  This, contrarily, we cannot do from light into darkness,
  349  because the air of gloom, coming after,
  350  follows thicker, and fills up all the openings
  351  and besieges the eyes' passages, so that the images
  352  of any things, cast in, cannot be stirred to motion.
  353  And when from far off we discern the square towers of a city,
  354  it comes about that they often seem round
  355  for this reason: every angle is seen blunted at a distance,
  356  or rather is not seen at all, and its blow
  357  is lost, and its stroke does not glide through to our sight,
  358  because while the images are carried through much air,
  359  the air by frequent collisions forces the blow to grow dull.
  360  When in this way every angle at once has escaped the sense,
  361  it comes about that the stone structures look as if turned on a lathe;
  362  not, though, like things that are near at hand and truly round,
  363  but they seem a little like them, as if in shadowy sketch.
  364  A shadow too seems to us to move in the sun
  365  and to follow our footsteps and imitate our gesture,
  366  if you believe that air deprived of light can
  367  walk forward, following the motions and gesture of men;
  368  for it can be nothing else but air bereft of light,
  369  this thing that we are accustomed to call shadow.
  370  Doubtless, because the earth, in places in fixed order,
  371  is deprived of the sun's light wherever we, moving,
  372  get in its way, and is filled again where we have left it,
  373  it comes about that what was the shadow of the body
  374  seems to have followed us all along in the same direction.
  375  For always new light of rays pours itself forth,
  376  and the first perishes, as if wool were drawn into fire.
  377  Therefore the earth is easily both stripped of light
  378  and filled again, and washes its black shadows from itself.

  379  Yet we do not grant that the eyes are deceived one whit in this.
  380  For to see in what place there is light and shadow
  381  is their task; but whether the lights are the same or not,
  382  and whether the shadow that was here now passes over there,
  383  or rather what we described a little before comes about,
  384  this the reasoning of the mind alone must discern,
  385  nor can the eyes come to know the nature of things.
  386  Do not, then, fasten this fault of the mind upon the eyes.
  387  The ship in which we are carried moves while it seems to stand still;
  388  the one that stays at its mooring is believed to be going past.
  389  And hills and plains seem to flee astern
  390  as we drive past them in our ship and fly under sail.
  391  The stars all seem to rest, fixed fast to the vaults of the ether,
  392  and yet all of them are in unceasing motion,
  393  since, once risen, they go back to their far-off settings
  394  when they have measured out the sky with their bright bodies.
  395  And on a like principle the sun and the moon seem to stay
  396  at rest, though the fact itself shows that they are carried on.
  397  And mountains that stand out far off from the middle of the sea,
  398  between which a wide passage lies open, free for fleets,
  399  seem nonetheless to make one island, joined together.
  400  That the halls turn round and the columns run in circles
  401  seems so strongly to children, once they themselves
  402  have stopped spinning, that they can hardly now believe
  403  the whole roof is not threatening to fall in on them.
  404  Again, when nature begins to raise on high the red radiance
  405  with its trembling fires, and to lift it above the mountains,
  406  those mountains above which the sun then seems to you to be,
  407  touching them close at hand, burning, with its own fire,
  408  are scarcely two thousand bowshots away from us,
  409  often scarcely even five hundred casts of a javelin;
  410  between them and the sun lie the immense levels
  411  of the sea, spread out beneath the vast shores of the ether,
  412  and many thousands of lands lie in between,
  413  held by various peoples and the generations of wild beasts.
  414  But a pool of water no deeper than a single finger,
  415  which stands between the stones of the paving in the streets,
  416  offers a view down beneath the earth to a depth as great
  417  as the high gulf of the sky gapes open above the earth,
  418  so that you seem to look down on clouds and to see the sky,
  419  and bodies wonderfully hidden in a sky beneath the earth.
  420  Again, when our spirited horse has stuck fast in midstream
  421  and we have looked down into the river's rushing waves,
  422  some force seems to carry the body of the standing horse
  423  sideways and to thrust it swiftly against the current,
  424  and wherever we cast our eyes, all things seem
  425  to be carried and to flow on, to us, in the same way.
  426  Again, though a colonnade runs in an even line
  427  and stands from end to end propped on equal columns,
  428  yet when it is seen, all its length, from the top end,
  429  it gradually draws into the point of a narrow cone,
  430  joining roof to floor and all the right side to the left,
  431  until it has drawn them into the dim tip of the cone.
  432  On the open sea, to sailors the sun, risen from the waves,
  433  seems to set in the waves and to hide its light;
  434  for indeed they see nothing else but water and sky;
  435  lest you lightly believe the senses are shaken on every side.
  436  But to those who do not know the sea, ships in harbor
  437  seem crippled, their stern-posts broken, as they strain against the waves.
  438  For whatever part of the oars is raised above the salt spray
  439  is straight, and the rudders are straight above;
  440  but the parts that go down sunk in the water seem broken back,
  441  all turned about and bent, returning upward, upside down,
  442  and so bent back as almost to float on the water's surface.
  443  And when the winds carry thin clouds across the sky
  444  in the night-time, then the shining constellations seem
  445  to glide against the clouds and to travel above them
  446  in a far other direction than true reasoning shows they are carried.
  447  But if by chance a hand placed beneath one eye
  448  presses it from below, by a certain feeling it comes about
  449  that all things we look at seem, as we look, to become double:
  450  double the lights of the lamps, flowering with flames,
  451  and through the whole house the furniture doubled in pairs,
  452  and men with double faces and twin bodies.
  453  Again, when sleep has bound our limbs in sweet slumber
  454  and the whole body lies in deepest rest,
  455  yet we seem to ourselves to be awake and to move
  456  our limbs, and in the blind darkness of the night
  457  we think we see the sun and the light of day,
  458  and, in a closed room, sky, sea, rivers, mountains
  459  we seem to change, and to cross the plains on foot,
  460  and to hear sounds, when the stern silences of the night
  461  hold fast on every side, and to give answers, saying nothing.
  462  We see many other wonders of this kind,
  463  all seeking, as it were, to violate our trust in the senses,
  464  in vain, since the greatest part of them deceive
  465  because of the mind's suppositions, which we add ourselves,
  466  so that things not seen by the senses pass for seen;
  467  for nothing is harder than to separate things plain
  468  from doubtful ones, which the mind straightway adds of itself.
  469  Again, if anyone thinks that nothing can be known, he does not know
  470  whether even that can be known, since he admits he knows nothing.
  471  Against this man, then, I will forbear to argue the case,
  472  who has set himself with his head where his footprints should be.
  473  And yet, granting that he knows even this, still I would ask
  474  this very thing: since he has seen nothing true in things before,
  475  from where does he know what it is to know and, in turn, not to know,
  476  what thing has created the notion of the true and the false,
  477  and what thing has proved the doubtful to differ from the certain?
  478  You will find that from the senses first was created
  479  the notion of the true, and that the senses cannot be refuted.
  480  For that must be found to deserve greater trust
  481  which can of its own accord overcome false things with true.
  482  Further, what ought to be held in greater trust than the senses?
  483  Or will reasoning, sprung from a false sense, have the power
  484  to speak against them, when it has sprung wholly from the senses?
  485  And unless they are true, all reasoning too becomes false.
  486  Or will the eyes be able to correct the ears, or the ears
  487  the touch? Or again will the mouth's taste accuse this touch,
  488  or will the nostrils confute it, or the eyes refute it?
  489  It is not so, I think. For to each a separate power
  490  has been allotted, each has its own force, and so it must be
  491  that what is soft, and cold or hot, is perceived apart,
  492  and that the various colors of things are perceived apart,
  493  and whatever things are joined to colors.
  494  Apart, likewise, the mouth's taste has its power, apart smells
  495  are born, apart sounds. And so it must be
  496  that one sense cannot convict another.
  497  Nor, further, will they be able to correct themselves,
  498  since equal trust must always be placed in them.
  499  Therefore what has seemed to them at any time is true.

  500  And if reasoning cannot untie the cause
  501  why things that were square close at hand are seen far off
  502  as round, still it is better, if you are at a loss for reasoning,
  503  to give faulty causes for each of the two shapes
  504  than to let things plain to see slip from your hands somewhere
  505  and violate the first trust and tear up all
  506  the foundations on which life and safety rest.
  507  For not only would all reasoning fall in ruin; life itself
  508  would collapse at once, unless you dared to trust the senses
  509  and avoid steep places and the other things that are
  510  to be fled in this kind, and follow what is their opposite.
  511  That whole store of words, then, is empty for you,
  512  which is drawn up and made ready against the senses.
  513  Finally, as in a building, if the first rule is crooked,
  514  and if the square is false and slips from its straight lines,
  515  and if the level limps a hair's breadth at any point,
  516  everything must be made faulty and askew,
  517  crooked, sagging, leaning forward, leaning back, the roof out of true,
  518  so that some parts seem already to want to fall, and do fall,
  519  all of it betrayed by those first false judgments;
  520  so then your reasoning of things must be crooked
  521  and false, whatever reasoning has risen from false senses.
  522  Now, by what means each of the other senses perceives
  523  its own object, the reasoning left is by no means rugged.
  524  First of all, sound and every voice are heard when, working their way
  525  into the ears, they have struck the sense with their body.
  526  For we must admit that voice too, and sound, are bodily,
  527  since they are able to strike upon the senses.
  528  Further, the voice often scrapes the throat, and shouting
  529  makes the windpipe rougher as it goes out,
  530  since, rising in a greater crowd through a narrow way,
  531  the first-beginnings of voices have begun to go out.
  532  Clearly the door of the mouth too is scraped when it is filled.
  533  There is no doubt, then, that voices and words consist
  534  of bodily beginnings, since they are able to do harm.
  535  Nor, likewise, does it escape you what body is lost, and what
  536  is drawn from men's sinews and their very strength
  537  by unbroken speech carried on to the shadow of black night
  538  from the rising brightness of the dawn,
  539  above all if it is poured out with the loudest shouting.
  540  Therefore the voice must be bodily,
  541  since one who talks much loses part of his body.
  542  Roughness of voice, again, comes from roughness
  543  of the beginnings, and smoothness likewise is made by smoothness;
  544  nor do the first-beginnings pierce the ears with like shape
  545  when the trumpet bellows deep with its low-pressed growl
  546  and the barbarous horn, roused, booms back its hoarse drone,
  547  and when the Daulian birds, born in the gardens of Helicon,
  548  raise their liquid lament with mourning voice.
  549  So when we press these voices out from deep within our body
  550  and send them out straight through the mouth,
  551  the nimble tongue, artificer of sinews, articulates them,
  552  and the shaping of the lips gives them form in its turn.
  553  When the space is not long from which each voice
  554  sets out to arrive, the words themselves too must
  555  be heard plainly and distinguished syllable by syllable;
  556  for the voice keeps its shaping and keeps its form.
  557  But if the space set between is longer than is fit,
  558  the words must be confused passing through much air
  559  and the voice thrown into disorder as it flies through the air.
  560  So it comes about that you can perceive a sound but cannot
  561  make out what the meaning of the words is;
  562  so confused and tangled does the voice arrive.
  563  Further, a single word often rouses the ears
  564  of all in a crowd, sent out from a crier's mouth.
  565  One voice, then, suddenly scatters into many voices,
  566  since it divides itself among separate ears,
  567  stamping on the words their form and clear sound.
  568  But the part of the voices that does not fall on the ears themselves
  569  is carried past and perishes, spread out in vain through the breezes.
  570  Part, dashed against solid places, is thrown back, returns
  571  a sound, and at times deceives us with the echo of a word.
  572  When you see this well, you can give an account
  573  to yourself and to others of how, through lonely places,
  574  rocks give back the matching forms of words in order,
  575  when we search among the shadowed mountains for straying comrades
  576  and call them, scattered, with a great voice.
  577  I have seen places give back even six or seven voices
  578  when you threw out one: so the hills themselves, to hills
  579  beating the words back, repeated the sayings as they returned.
  580  These places, the neighbors fancy, goat-footed Satyrs
  581  and Nymphs hold, and they say there are Fauns,
  582  by whose night-wandering din and jesting play
  583  they declare the speechless silences are commonly broken,
  584  and sounds of strings arise, and the sweet laments
  585  that the pipe pours out when struck by the players' fingers;
  586  and that the race of farmers hears it far and wide when Pan,
  587  shaking the pine coverings of his half-beast head,
  588  often runs his curved lip over the open reeds,
  589  so that the pipe will not cease pouring out its woodland music.
  590  Other marvels and portents of this kind they tell,
  591  so that they may not be thought to hold lonely places
  592  deserted even by the gods. So they toss out wonders in their talk,
  593  or they are led by some other reason, as the whole
  594  human race is all too greedy for listening ears.
  595  For the rest, it is no wonder by what principle,
  596  through places where the eyes cannot make out things in plain view,
  597  voices come through these places and assail the ears;
  598  we often see a conversation through closed doors as well;
  599  no doubt because a voice can pass unharmed through the winding
  600  passages of things, while images refuse;
  601  for they are torn apart unless they swim through straight passages,
  602  such as those of glass, through which every appearance flies.
  603  Further, a voice is divided in every direction,
  604  since voices are born one from another, once a single one
  605  has sprung forth and leapt apart into many, as a spark of fire
  606  often spreads itself out into its own fires.
  607  Therefore places hidden away behind are filled with voices,
  608  which all around are astir and roused with sound.
  609  But images all travel along straight paths
  610  once they are sent out; and so no one can see
  611  over a wall, but can take in voices from beyond it.
  612  And yet this very voice too, while it passes through the closed rooms
  613  of houses, is blunted and enters the ears confused,
  614  and we seem to hear a sound rather than words.

  615  As for how we perceive flavor, the tongue and palate
  616  hold a little more reasoning in them, and more labor.
  617  To begin with, we perceive flavor in the mouth when, chewing,
  618  we press it out of food, as with a sponge full of water,
  619  if someone should start to squeeze it in the hand and dry it.
  620  Then all we press out is spread through the palate's passages
  621  and through the winding pores of the loose-woven tongue.
  622  So when the bodies of the oozing flavor are smooth,
  623  they touch sweetly, and sweetly handle all
  624  the moist precincts sweating round about the tongue;
  625  but on the contrary they prick the sense and tear it as they come,
  626  the more so the more each is filled with roughness.
  627  Next, the pleasure from flavor lies within the bounds of the palate;
  628  but when it has plunged down through the throat,
  629  there is no pleasure, while it is all being spread into the limbs;
  630  nor does it matter at all on what food the body is fed,
  631  so long as what you take you can digest and spread
  632  through the limbs, and keep the stomach's full and even tenor.
  633  Now, so we may see why one food suits some, another others,
  634  I shall explain, and why what is harsh and bitter to some
  635  can yet seem to others exceedingly sweet,
  636  and why in these things there is such distance and difference
  637  that what is food to some is to others sharp poison;
  638  for indeed there is a snake which, touched by a man's spittle,
  639  perishes and destroys itself by gnawing at itself.
  640  Further, hellebore is sharp poison to us,
  641  but to goats and to quails it adds fat.
  642  So that you can learn by what means this comes about,
  643  first it is fitting to remember what we said before,
  644  that seeds are held mixed in things in many ways.
  645  Further, all living creatures whatever that take food,
  646  just as they are unlike outwardly, and, kind by kind,
  647  the outer contour of their limbs confines them,
  648  so too they consist of seeds and vary in their shape.
  649  Again, since the seeds differ, it must be that the intervals
  650  and the paths, which we call pores, differ too
  651  in all the limbs, and in the mouth and the palate itself.
  652  Therefore some must be smaller and some larger,
  653  for some creatures they must be three-cornered, for others square,
  654  many round, some many-angled in many ways.
  655  For as the order of the shapes and their motions demand,
  656  so the shapes of the pores must differ,
  657  and the paths vary, according as the texture confines them.
  658  So when what is sweet to some becomes bitter to others,
  659  for the one to whom it is sweet the smoothest bodies must
  660  enter the passages of the palate caressingly,
  661  but for those to whom the same thing is bitter within,
  662  rough and hooked bodies, doubtless, make their way into the throat.
  663  Now it is easy from these things to understand each case.
  664  For indeed when fever has risen in someone, the bile prevailing,
  665  or the force of disease has been roused by some other means,
  666  then the whole body is thrown into disorder, and all
  667  the positions of the beginnings are changed;
  668  so it comes about that bodies which before suited the sense
  669  now do not suit it, and others are more fitted,
  670  which, making their way in, can beget a bitter sense;
  671  for both kinds are mixed together in the flavor of honey,
  672  a thing we have often shown you above already.
  673  Come now, how the touch of odor strikes the nostrils
  674  I shall set out. First, there must be many things
  675  from which a varied stream of odors rolls flowing,
  676  and we must suppose they flow and are sent out and scattered everywhere;
  677  but one odor is more fitted to some creatures, another to others,
  678  because of their unlike forms. And so through the air
  679  bees are drawn, however far, by the odor of honey,
  680  and vultures by carcasses; then, where the cloven hoof of beasts
  681  has carried its steps, the far-reaching force of hounds
  682  leads on, and the human smell is scented from far away
  683  by the savior of the citadel of Romulus' sons, the white goose.
  684  So one scent given to some, another to others, leads each
  685  to its own pasture, and makes it spring back from foul poison,
  686  and in this way the generations of wild beasts are preserved.
  687  This odor itself, then, whatever it is that provokes the nostrils,
  688  is such that one can be sent farther than another;
  689  but still none of them is carried as far
  690  as sound, as voice, not to speak now of the things
  691  that strike the sight of the eyes and provoke vision.
  692  For it comes wandering and slowly, and perishes on the way,
  693  little by little, easily torn apart into the breezes of air;
  694  first, because it is barely sent out from deep within the thing;
  695  for that odors flow and withdraw from the depths of things
  696  is shown by this, that all things seem to smell more when broken,
  697  when crushed, when dissolved by fire.
  698  Next, you may see that it is made of larger
  699  beginnings than voice, since through walls of stone
  700  it does not pass, where voice and sound are commonly carried.
  701  Therefore you will see as well that what smells is not so easy
  702  to track down, in what region it is placed;
  703  for the blow cools as it lingers through the air,
  704  and the messengers of things do not run down hot to the sense.
  705  And so dogs often go astray and search for the tracks.
  706  Nor yet is this found only in odors and in the class
  707  of flavors; likewise the appearances and colors of things
  708  do not all so suit the senses of all
  709  that some are not harsher to some to look at.
  710  Indeed, the cock, who with clapping wings drives out the night
  711  and is wont to call the dawn with ringing voice,
  712  ravening lions are not able to stand against
  713  and look upon: so instantly do they remember flight.
  714  Doubtless because there are in the body of cocks certain
  715  seeds which, when they are sent into the eyes of lions,
  716  bore into the pupils and give sharp pain,
  717  so that, fierce as they are, they cannot hold out against them,
  718  although these things cannot harm our sight at all,
  719  either because they do not get in, or because, once they do,
  720  a free way out of the eyes is given them, so that by lingering
  721  they cannot harm the eyes in any part.
  722  Come now, hear what things move the mind, and from where
  723  the things that come into the mind come, learn in a few words.
  724  First, I say this: that images of things wander,
  725  many, in many ways, in all directions on every side,
  726  thin images, which easily join with one another in the air
  727  when they meet, like a spider's web or a leaf of gold.
  728  For indeed these are much thinner in texture
  729  than those that take hold of the eyes and provoke vision,
  730  since these pass through the loose openings of the body and stir
  731  the thin nature of the mind within, and provoke sense.
  732  And so we see Centaurs and the limbs of Scyllas
  733  and the dog-faces of Cerberus, and images of those
  734  whose bones, after death, the earth embraces;
  735  since images of every kind are carried everywhere,
  736  some that come to be of their own accord in the air itself,
  737  some that withdraw from all manner of things,
  738  and those that are made by combining the shapes of these.
  739  For certainly no image of a Centaur comes from a living one,
  740  since no such nature of living thing ever existed;
  741  but when by chance the images of horse and man meet,
  742  they easily cling together at once, as we said before,
  743  because of their subtle nature and thin texture.
  744  Other things of this kind are created by the same principle.
  745  And since these move nimbly with the utmost lightness,
  746  as I showed before, any one subtle image easily
  747  moves our mind with a single blow;
  748  for the mind is thin, and wonderfully mobile itself.
  749  That these things happen as I tell, you may easily learn from this:
  750  insofar as the one is like the other, what we see with the mind
  751  and with the eyes, it must come about by a like principle.
  752  Now, then, since I have shown that I see lions, say,
  753  by means of images, whatever images provoke the eyes,
  754  you may know that the mind is moved by a like principle
  755  by images of lions and the rest, which it sees just as
  756  well as the eyes, except that it perceives thinner ones.
  757  Nor by any other principle, when sleep has spread out the limbs,
  758  does the thought of the mind keep watch, except that the same images
  759  provoke our minds as when we are awake,
  760  so much so that we seem surely to see one
  761  whom, life left behind, death and the earth now possess.
  762  Nature compels this to happen because all
  763  the senses of the body, blocked, rest throughout the limbs,
  764  and cannot refute the false with things that are true.
  765  Further, memory lies idle and languishes in sleep,
  766  and does not protest that this one, in the grip of death and doom
  767  long since, is not the one the mind believes it sees alive.
  768  For the rest, it is no wonder that images move
  769  and throw their arms in rhythm, and their other limbs;
  770  for it happens that in dreams an image seems to do this.
  771  For indeed, when the first perishes and a second is born after
  772  in another posture, the earlier seems to have changed its gesture.
  773  Of course we must suppose that this happens by a swift principle:
  774  so great is the mobility, and so great the abundance of things,
  775  and so great, in any one moment we can perceive,
  776  the abundance of tiny parts, that it can supply them.

  777  And many questions arise in these matters, and much we must
  778  make clear, if we wish to set out the matter plainly.
  779  It is asked first of all why, whatever has come into anyone's
  780  fancy, the mind thinks of that very thing at once.
  781  Do the images keep watch upon our will,
  782  and does an image meet us the moment we wish,
  783  if our heart is set on the sea, on the earth, or finally the sky?
  784  Assemblies of men, a procession, banquets, battles,
  785  does nature create them all at a word and make them ready?
  786  And this especially when, in the same region and place,
  787  the minds of others are thinking of things all far unlike.
  788  What further, when we see the images advancing in rhythm
  789  in our sleep, and moving their supple limbs,
  790  when nimbly they fling out their supple arms by turns
  791  and repeat the gesture to our eyes with a matching foot?
  792  No doubt the images are steeped in art and wander well taught,
  793  so that they can put on shows in the time of night.
  794  Or will this rather be the truth? Because in a single time,
  795  when we perceive, that is, when a single sound is uttered,
  796  many times lie hidden, which reasoning finds to exist,
  797  therefore it comes about that in any time whatever
  798  every image is at hand, ready in every place.
  799  So great is the mobility, and so great the abundance of things.
  800  Here, when the first perishes and a second is born after it
  801  in another posture, the earlier seems to have changed its gesture.
  802  And because they are thin, the mind cannot sharply see
  803  any but those it strains toward; and so all that are
  804  beyond these perish, except those it has made itself ready for.
  805  And it makes itself ready, and hopes it will come about
  806  that it sees what follows on each thing: and so it does.
  807  Do you not see that the eyes too, when things that are thin
  808  beyond these perish, except those it has made itself ready for,
  809  they have begun to look at, strain and make themselves ready,
  810  and that without this we cannot see sharply?
  811  And yet even with things in plain view you can recognize
  812  that if you do not turn your mind to them, it is just as if
  813  they were all the time set apart and far removed.
  814  Why then is it a wonder if the mind loses all else
  815  except the things to which it is itself given over?
  816  Then, from small signs we form the greatest conjectures
  817  and lead ourselves into the snare of self-deception.
  818  It happens too at times that the image supplied
  819  is not of the same kind, but what was a woman before
  820  seems to be there in our hands, made into a man,
  821  or one face and one age follow after another.
  822  That we do not wonder at this, sleep and forgetfulness see to it.
  823  In these matters there is a fault I earnestly want you
  824  to flee, an error to avoid with foresight:
  825  do not think that the bright lights of the eyes were created
  826  so that we might look before us, and that, so we could take
  827  long strides, the tapering lengths of calf and thigh,
  828  set firm upon the feet, are for that reason able to bend;
  829  then too, that the forearms were fitted to strong upper arms,
  830  and hands were given as servants on either side,
  831  so that we could do what would be of use for life.
  832  All other things of this kind which men expound, whatever they are,
  833  are all backward, by reasoning turned upside down,
  834  since nothing was born in the body so that we could use
  835  it, but what is born creates its use.
  836  Nor was there seeing before the lights of the eyes were born,
  837  nor pleading with words before the tongue was created;
  838  rather, the origin of the tongue came long before
  839  speech, and the ears were created long before
  840  sound was heard, and finally all the limbs
  841  existed, I think, before there was any use for them;
  842  they cannot, therefore, have grown for the sake of using.
  843  But, by contrast, to join hand to hand in the contests of battle,
  844  to mangle the frame and befoul the limbs with gore,
  845  came long before shining weapons flew,
  846  and nature compelled men to avoid a wound
  847  before the left arm, by art, held up the shield against it.
  848  And surely to give the tired body over to rest
  849  is far older than the soft coverings of a bed,
  850  and to quench thirst came before cups were born.
  851  These things, then, may be believed devised for the sake
  852  of using, since they were found out from use and life.
  853  But all those stand apart which, born first themselves,
  854  afterwards gave us the notion of their usefulness.
  855  Of this kind above all we see the senses and the limbs;
  856  therefore, again and again, you are far from able to believe
  857  that they could have been created for the service of usefulness.
  858  This too is no cause for wonder, that the nature
  859  of the body of each living creature seeks food.
  860  For indeed I have taught that many bodies flow and withdraw
  861  from things in many ways; but most must go
  862  from living creatures; for since they are worked by motion,
  863  many are breathed out through the mouth when, weary, they pant,
  864  and many are pressed from deep within and carried off in sweat.
  865  By these things, then, the body grows thin, and its whole
  866  nature is undermined, and pain follows on this.
  867  Food therefore is taken, to prop up the frame
  868  and, given within, to renew the strength, and to stop up
  869  the gaping desire for eating through limbs and veins.
  870  Moisture likewise goes off into all the places
  871  that call for moisture; and the many bodies of heat
  872  that have massed together and kindle fires in our stomach
  873  the liquid, coming, scatters and puts out like fire,
  874  so that the dry heat can burn the frame no longer.
  875  In this way, then, the panting thirst is washed out
  876  of our body, in this way the hungry desire is filled.
  877  Now how it comes about that we can take steps forward
  878  when we wish, and why it is given us to move our limbs,
  879  and what thing has grown used to pushing forward this great weight
  880  of our body, I shall tell: you, take in my words.
  881  I say that first images of walking come
  882  to our mind and strike the mind, as we said before.
  883  From there will arises; for no one begins to do
  884  anything before the mind has foreseen what it wills.
  885  Of what it foresees, an image of that thing exists.
  886  Therefore, when the mind so stirs itself that it wills to go
  887  and step forward, at once it strikes the force of spirit
  888  that is scattered through the whole body, through limbs and frame;
  889  and that is easy to do, since it is held joined with it.
  890  Then this in turn strikes the body, and so the whole
  891  mass is little by little pushed forward and moved.
  892  Further, the body then grows loose in texture too, and air,
  893  as surely it must, being always quick to move,
  894  comes through the opened passages and enters them in plenty,
  895  and so is dispersed into every smallest part
  896  of the body. Here, then, by two things working from two sides,
  897  the body is carried like a ship by sails and wind.
  898  Nor yet is it a thing to wonder at in these matters
  899  that bodies so tiny can turn so great a body
  900  about and swing round the whole of our weight;
  901  for indeed the wind, thin with its fine body,
  902  drives on and pushes a great ship with great momentum,
  903  and one hand rules it, going at whatever speed,
  904  and one rudder turns it wherever it pleases,
  905  and a machine, by pulleys and treadwheels, moves many things
  906  of great weight and lifts them up with slight effort.

  907  Now in what ways sleep floods the limbs with rest
  908  and loosens the cares of the mind from the breast,
  909  I shall set out in verses sweet-spoken rather than many,
  910  as the small song of the swan is better than that clamor
  911  of cranes scattered among the high clouds of the south wind.
  912  You, give me fine ears and a keen mind,
  913  lest you deny that what I say can come to be, and go off
  914  with a breast that thrusts the true words back,
  915  when you yourself are at fault and cannot see it.
  916  To begin with, sleep comes about when the force of the spirit
  917  is scattered through the frame, and part, thrown out of doors, has gone,
  918  and part, crowded together, has withdrawn deeper inside;
  919  for only then are the limbs loosened and go slack.
  920  For there is no doubt that it is by the work of the spirit
  921  that this sense is in us; and when slumber hinders it from being,
  922  then we must think that our spirit has been thrown into disorder
  923  and cast out of doors, though not all; for then the body would lie
  924  steeped in the eternal chill of death.
  925  Indeed, if no part of the spirit stayed hidden
  926  in the limbs, as fire lies hidden, buried under much ash,
  927  from where could sense be kindled again through the limbs on a sudden,
  928  as flame rises up out of hidden fire?
  929  But by what things this change is brought about, and from where
  930  the spirit can be thrown into disorder and the body grow languid,
  931  I shall explain: you, see I do not pour my words to the winds.
  932  To begin with, on its outer side the body must,
  933  since it is touched by the neighboring breezes of the air,
  934  be beaten and battered by their constant blows,
  935  and that is why nearly all things are covered either with hide
  936  or else with shells or with hard skin or with bark.
  937  On the inner side too, as we breathe, the air
  938  strikes this same body, as it is drawn in and breathed out.
  939  Therefore, since the body is beaten on both sides, and since
