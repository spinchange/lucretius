    1  Who has the strength of heart to build a song worthy
    2  of the majesty of things and of these discoveries?
    3  Or who is so strong in words that he could shape praises
    4  to match the merits of the one who left us such prizes,
    5  brought forth and sought out by his own heart?
    6  No one, I think, who is made of a mortal body.
    7  For if we must speak as the known majesty of things
    8  itself demands, he was a god, a god, renowned Memmius,
    9  who first discovered that reasoning of life which
   10  is now called wisdom, and who by his art
   11  took life out of such great waves and such great darkness
   12  and set it in so calm a place and in so clear a light.
   13  For compare the godlike discoveries of others long ago.
   14  For Ceres is said to have established grain, and Liber
   15  for mortals the liquid of the vine-born juice;
   16  and yet life could go on without these things,
   17  as report says some nations live even now.
   18  But a good life was not possible without a pure heart;
   19  so with all the more right this man seems to us a god,
   20  from whom even now, spread abroad through great nations,
   21  the sweet consolations of life soothe our minds.
   22  But if you think the deeds of Hercules stand above his,
   23  you will be carried much farther from true reasoning.
   24  For what harm to us now would that great gaping mouth
   25  of the Nemean lion do, or the bristling Arcadian boar?
   26  And how much could they hinder us, the birds that haunt Stymphalus?
   27  Finally, what could the bull of Crete do, or the plague of Lerna,
   28  the hydra, palisaded round with venomous snakes?
   29  Or what the triple-breasted strength of three-bodied Geryon,
   30  and the horses of Diomedes, breathing fire from their nostrils
   31  in Thrace, near the Bistonian lands and Ismarus,
   32  and, guarding the gleaming golden apples of the Hesperides,
   33  fierce, with savage glare, the serpent of enormous body
   34  coiled round the tree's trunk? What harm, finally, would it do
   35  there by the Atlantic shore and the stern stretches of the sea,
   36  where no one of ours goes and no barbarian dares?
   37  And the other monsters of this kind that have been destroyed,
   38  if they had not been conquered, what harm, pray, would they do alive?
   39  None, I think: for even now the earth swarms, to the full,
   40  with wild beasts, and is filled with trembling terror
   41  through groves and great mountains and deep forests,
   42  places which for the most part it is in our power to avoid.
   43  But unless the heart is purged, what battles then
   44  and dangers must work their way into us against our will!
   45  How great then are the sharp cares of desire that tear
   46  a troubled man apart, and how great, too, the fears!
   47  Or pride, filth, and wantonness, what of them? How great
   48  the disasters they bring about! What of luxury and sloth?
   49  Therefore the man who has subdued all these and from the mind
   50  driven them out with words, not arms, will it not be fitting
   51  that this man be deemed worthy of a place among the gods?
   52  Especially since he was used to give, well and divinely,
   53  many sayings from the gods to mortals themselves
   54  and to lay open in his words the whole nature of things.
   55  Treading in his footsteps, while I follow out his reasonings
   56  and teach in my words by what covenant all things
   57  are created, and how they must endure in it,
   58  and cannot annul the strong laws of time,
   59  in which kind, first of all, the nature of the mind was found
   60  to consist, first, of a body that had birth,
   61  and not able to endure unharmed through long ages,
   62  but that images are used to deceive the mind in sleep
   63  when we seem to see one whom life has left;
   64  for the rest, the order of reasoning has now brought me to this,
   65  that I must give an account of how the world consists
   66  of a mortal body and at the same time had a birth;
   67  and in what ways that coming-together of matter
   68  founded the earth, the sky, the sea, the stars, the sun,
   69  and the globe of the moon; then what living things
   70  arose from the earth, and which were at no time born;
   71  and in what way the human race, with varied speech,
   72  began to make use, among themselves, of the names of things;
   73  and in what ways that fear of the gods worked its way into
   74  our hearts, which over the round of the earth keeps holy
   75  shrines, lakes, groves, altars, and images of the gods.
   76  Further, the courses of the sun and the wanderings of the moon,
   77  I shall explain by what force steering nature bends them,
   78  so that we may not think these, between heaven and earth,
   79  run their everlasting courses freely, of their own accord,
   80  obliging the growth of crops and of living things,
   81  nor suppose they are rolled round by some reasoning of the gods.
   82  For those who have learned well that the gods lead a life without care,
   83  if yet meanwhile they wonder by what principle
   84  each thing can be carried on, above all in those things
   85  that are seen overhead in the shores of the ether,
   86  are carried back again into the old religions
   87  and take on harsh masters, whom the wretches believe
   88  can do all things, ignorant of what can come to be
   89  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded,
   90  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep.
   91  For the rest, not to keep you longer with promises,
   92  first of all look on the seas and lands and sky;
   93  their threefold nature, their three bodies, Memmius,
   94  three appearances so unlike, three such weavings,
   95  one day will give to destruction, and, upheld through many years,
   96  the mass and machinery of the world will fall.
   97  Nor does it escape my mind how new and strange a thing
   98  befalls the mind, the coming destruction of heaven and earth,
   99  and how hard it is for me to prove it in words;
  100  as happens when you bring to the ears a thing unheard of before
  101  yet cannot put it under the sight of the eyes
  102  nor lay it in the hands, by which the paved road of belief
  103  leads nearest to the human heart and the precincts of the mind.
  104  But still I will speak out. Perhaps the fact itself will give belief
  105  to my words, and you will see, when violent earthquakes arise,
  106  all things shaken to pieces in a little time.
  107  May steering fortune turn this far away from us,
  108  and may reasoning rather than the event itself persuade
  109  that all things can be overcome and fall with a dreadful crash.
  110  Before I approach this subject, to pour out pronouncements
  111  more holy and with far surer reasoning than
  112  the Pythia, who speaks from the tripod and the laurel of Phoebus,
  113  I shall set out for you many consolations in learned words,
  114  so that, reined in by religion, you may not think
  115  that the lands and sun and sky, sea, stars, moon,
  116  must remain eternal, being of divine body,
  117  and so suppose it right that, like the Giants,
  118  all those should pay the penalty for monstrous crime
  119  who by their reasoning shake the walls of the world
  120  and would put out the glorious sun of heaven,
  121  branding immortal things with mortal speech;
  122  although these are so very far removed from divine power
  123  and so unworthy to be counted among the gods
  124  that they may rather be thought to give us a notion
  125  of what is removed from vital motion and sense.
  126  For indeed it cannot be that with any body whatever
  127  the nature of the mind and judgment be thought able to exist.
  128  Just as there can be no tree in the upper air, no clouds
  129  in the deep sea, nor can fish live in the fields,
  130  nor blood be in wood, nor sap in stones;
  131  it is fixed and ordered where each thing grows and is:
  132  so the nature of the mind cannot arise without the body,
  133  alone, nor exist far off from sinews and blood.
  134  For if it could, the force of the mind itself, much sooner,
  135  could be in the head or shoulders or the lowest heels,
  136  and would be wont to be born in any part you please,
  137  and still remain in the same man and the same vessel.
  138  And since even in our body it is evidently fixed
  139  and ordered, it seems, where each can be and grow,
  140  spirit and mind, each apart, all the more must we deny
  141  that, wholly outside the body and the living form,
  142  they could endure in crumbling clods of earth or the sun's fire
  143  or in water or the high shores of the ether.
  144  Therefore these things do not exist endowed with divine sense,
  145  since they cannot be alive and quickened with the breath of life.

  146  Again, you cannot possibly believe that the holy
  147  dwellings of the gods exist in any parts of the world.
  148  For the nature of the gods is thin, and far removed
  149  from our senses, and is scarcely seen by the thought of the mind;
  150  and since it slips away from the touch and stroke of hands,
  151  it must touch nothing that is tangible to us;
  152  for what may not itself be touched cannot touch.
  153  Therefore their dwellings too must also be unlike
  154  our dwellings, thin, in keeping with their bodies;
  155  and this I shall prove to you later in ample discourse.
  156  Further, to say that for the sake of men they willed to prepare
  157  the glorious nature of the world, and that therefore
  158  it is fitting to praise the praiseworthy work of the gods,
  159  and to think that it will be eternal and immortal,
  160  and that it is sin that what, by the gods' ancient reasoning,
  161  was founded for the races of men to last through endless age,
  162  ever to be shaken by any force from its seat,
  163  or harried with words and overturned from top to bottom,
  164  and to invent and add other things of this kind, Memmius,
  165  is folly. For what profit could our gratitude
  166  bestow upon immortal and blessed beings,
  167  that for our sake they should set about doing anything?
  168  Or what new thing could, so long after, lure beings at peace before
  169  to desire to change their former life?
  170  For it seems that one should rejoice in new things
  171  whom the old things harm; but one who met with nothing grievous
  172  in the time gone by, while he spent his life in beauty,
  173  what could kindle in such a one a love of novelty?
  174  Or what harm would it have been to us never to have been created?
  175  Did life, I suppose, lie in darkness and sorrow
  176  until the generative origin of things dawned?
  177  For whoever has been born must want to remain
  178  in life, so long as soft pleasure holds him;
  179  but for one who never tasted the love of life
  180  and was never counted, what harm is it not to have been created?
  181  Further, the pattern for creating things, and the very
  182  notion of men, from where was it first planted in the gods,
  183  so that they knew and saw in their mind what they wished to make,
  184  or in what way was the power of the beginnings ever known,
  185  and what they could do with their order changed among themselves,
  186  if nature herself did not give a likeness of creation?
  187  For so many first-beginnings of things, in so many ways,
  188  driven by blows from infinite time till now,
  189  and roused by their own weights, have been used to move
  190  and to meet in every way and to try out all things
  191  that they could create by meeting with one another,
  192  so it is no wonder if they fell into such arrangements
  193  and come, as well, into such courses
  194  as those by which this sum of things is now carried on by renewal.
  195  But even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  196  this, still, from the very order of heaven I would dare
  197  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  198  that by no means for us, by divine power, was prepared
  199  the nature of things: so great the flaws it stands endowed with.
  200  First, of all that the vast sweep of heaven covers,
  201  a greedy portion the mountains and the forests of wild beasts
  202  have taken; rocks hold it, and vast marshes,
  203  and the sea, which keeps the shores of the lands wide apart.
  204  Then, further, nearly two parts the burning heat
  205  and the unceasing fall of frost take away from mortals.
  206  What is left of plowland, even that nature by her own force
  207  would choke with brambles, did human force not resist,
  208  used for life's sake to groan over the strong mattock
  209  and to cut through the earth with the plow pressed down.
  210  If we did not, turning the fertile clods with the plowshare
  211  and working the soil of the earth, rouse them to birth,
  212  of their own accord they could not come out into the clear air.
  213  And even so, at times, when things won with great labor
  214  are now in leaf throughout the lands and all in flower,
  215  either the sun in the ether scorches them with too much heat,
  216  or sudden rains destroy them, and chill frosts,
  217  and blasts of wind harry them with violent whirlwind.
  218  Further, the race of wild beasts, bristling with horror,
  219  hostile to the human kind on land and sea,
  220  why does nature feed and increase it? Why do the seasons bring
  221  diseases? Why does untimely death go wandering?
  222  Then again the child, like a sailor cast up by savage waves,
  223  lies naked on the ground, speechless, in want of every
  224  help for life, as soon as into the shores of light
  225  nature has poured him out with throes from his mother's womb,
  226  and he fills the place with mournful wailing, as is right
  227  for one who has so much of evil left to pass through in life.
  228  But the various flocks grow up, and herds, and wild beasts,
  229  and they need no rattles, nor must any of them be given
  230  the coaxing, broken baby-talk of a kindly nurse,
  231  nor do they look for changing clothes to suit the weather;
  232  finally, they need no arms, no high walls,
  233  to guard their own, since for all of them all things abundantly
  234  the earth herself brings forth, and nature, artificer of things.
  235  First, since the body of the earth, and moisture,
  236  and the light breaths of air, and the hot vapors,
  237  of which this sum of things is seen to consist,
  238  all consist of a body that is born and mortal,
  239  the whole nature of the world must be thought to be of the same.
  240  For indeed, things whose parts and members we see
  241  to be of a body that is born and of mortal shapes,
  242  these same we see to be, as a rule, mortal
  243  and born as well. Therefore, since I see the greatest
  244  members and parts of the world consumed and born again,
  245  we may know that heaven too, and likewise earth, have had
  246  some time of beginning, and will have their destruction.
  247  In these matters, do not think that I have taken too much
  248  for granted, in assuming earth and fire to be mortal,
  249  and in not doubting that moisture and the airs perish,
  250  and in saying that the same are born and grow again.
  251  First, no small part of the earth, scorched through
  252  by constant suns, beaten by the force of many feet,
  253  breathes out a haze of dust and flying clouds,
  254  which strong winds scatter through all the air.
  255  Part of the clods too is called back to flood
  256  by rains, and rivers graze and gnaw their banks.
  257  Further, whatever feeds and increases another, in its share
  258  is given back; and since beyond doubt it is seen
  259  that the mother of all is also the common tomb of things,
  260  therefore the earth, you see, is drawn upon and, increased, grows back.

  261  For the rest, that the sea, the rivers, the springs always brim
  262  with new moisture, and that the waters flow without end,
  263  there is no need of words: the great downrush of waters
  264  declares it everywhere. But all the water that comes first
  265  is taken off, and so in all the moisture never overflows,
  266  partly because the strong winds sweeping the levels of the sea
  267  diminish it, and the sun in the ether, unweaving with rays,
  268  partly because it is spread out beneath, through all the lands;
  269  for the brine is strained out, and the matter of moisture
  270  oozes back, and all of it gathers at the heads of the rivers,
  271  and from there flows over the lands in a column of sweet water
  272  where the way, once cut, has carried the waves down with liquid foot.
  273  Now therefore I shall speak of air, which in its whole body
  274  is changed in numberless ways with every single hour.
  275  For always whatever flows off from things, all of it
  276  is carried into the great sea of air; and unless air in turn
  277  gave bodies back to things and renewed them as they flow,
  278  all things would by now have been dissolved and turned to air.
  279  Air therefore never ceases to be born from things and to fall
  280  back into things, since it is certain that all things constantly flow.
  281  So too the bounteous fount of liquid light, the sun in the ether,
  282  floods the sky constantly with fresh brightness
  283  and at once supplies light with new light.
  284  For whatever brightness comes first is lost to it,
  285  wherever it falls. You may learn this from the fact
  286  that as soon as clouds begin to pass beneath the sun
  287  and, as it were, to break through between the rays of light,
  288  at once the lower part of those rays is all lost
  289  and the earth is shadowed wherever the storm clouds are carried;
  290  so that you may know things always need new brilliance,
  291  and each first cast of brightness perishes,
  292  and that in no other way can things be seen in the sun
  293  unless the very fountainhead of light supplied it without end.
  294  Indeed, even the lights of night, the lights that are of the earth,
  295  hanging lamps, and torches bright with flashing
  296  flares and rich with much murky smoke,
  297  hurry by a like principle, with heat as their servant,
  298  to supply new light; they press on to flicker with fire,
  299  press on, and the light leaves no place as if broken between:
  300  so hurriedly is its destruction, from all its fires,
  301  hastened on by the swift birth of flame.
  302  So therefore we must think that sun and moon and stars
  303  cast out their light from one fresh rising after another
  304  and always lose whatever flames come first,
  305  so that you may not perhaps believe these thrive inviolable.
  306  Again, do you not see that even stones are conquered by age,
  307  that high towers fall and rocks crumble,
  308  that the gods' shrines and images grow weary and crack,
  309  and the holy divinity cannot push forward the bounds
  310  of fate, nor struggle against nature's covenants?
  311  Again, do we not see the monuments of men fall in ruin,
  312  asking, in their turn, whether you believe that they too grow old,
  313  and flints torn from the high mountains rushing down,
  314  unable to bear and endure the strong forces of an age
  315  that is finite? For they would not fall, torn suddenly away,
  316  if from infinite time they had borne up against
  317  all the torments of age without breaking.
  318  Again, look now at this, which round and above
  319  holds the whole earth in its embrace: if it creates from itself
  320  all things, as some relate, and takes them back when they perish,
  321  the whole consists of a body that is born and mortal.
  322  For whatever increases and feeds other things from itself
  323  must be diminished, and renewed when it takes things back.
  324  Further, if there was no generative beginning
  325  of earth and heaven, and they have always been eternal,
  326  why, beyond the Theban war and the deaths of Troy,
  327  have not other poets also sung of other things?
  328  Where have so many deeds of men so often fallen, and nowhere
  329  flower, grafted on the eternal monuments of fame?
  330  But, as I think, the whole is new, and the nature
  331  of the world is recent, and not long ago took its beginnings.
  332  And so even now some arts are being polished,
  333  even now are growing; now much has been added
  334  to ships; lately players have given birth to melodious sounds;
  335  finally, this nature of things and its reasoning were found
  336  only lately, and I myself am now found the very first
  337  who can turn it into the speech of my fathers.
  338  But if perhaps you believe that all these same things were before,
  339  but that the generations of men perished in burning heat,
  340  or cities fell in some great upheaval of the world,
  341  or that after constant rains ravening rivers
  342  went out across the lands and buried the towns,
  343  all the more must you, conquered, confess
  344  that destruction of earth and heaven too will come;
  345  for when things were assailed by such diseases and such
  346  dangers, then, if a grimmer cause had pressed upon them,
  347  they would have spread disaster and great ruin far and wide.
  348  Nor in any other way do we seem to be mortal,
  349  among ourselves, save that we sicken with the same diseases
  350  as those whom nature has removed from life.
  351  Further, whatever things endure eternal must, of necessity,
  352  either, because they are of solid body, repel blows
  353  and suffer nothing to enter them that could tear apart
  354  their close-knit parts within, as the bodies of matter
  355  are, whose nature we have shown before;
  356  or be able to last through all time for this reason,
  357  that they are exempt from blows, as the void is,
  358  which stays untouched and is not affected one whit by a stroke;
  359  or else because there is no supply of place around them
  360  into which things could, as it were, part and be dissolved,
  361  as the sum of sums is eternal, nor is there outside it
  362  any place into which they might scatter, nor are there bodies that
  363  could fall upon them and dissolve them with a strong blow.
  364  But neither, as I have taught, is the nature of the world
  365  of solid body, since void is mixed in things,
  366  nor yet is it like the void; nor indeed are bodies lacking
  367  that might perhaps gather together out of the infinite
  368  and overthrow this sum of things with a violent whirlwind
  369  or bring upon it some other disaster of danger,
  370  nor further is the nature of place and the space of the deep
  371  lacking, into which the walls of the world could be scattered,
  372  or, driven by any other force, perish.
  373  The door of death, therefore, is not shut to the sky,
  374  nor to sun and earth, nor to the deep waters of the sea,
  375  but stands open and looks toward them with its vast and monstrous gape.
  376  Therefore you must confess that these same things
  377  were born; for things that are of mortal body
  378  could not, from infinite time until now,
  379  have scorned the strong forces of immeasurable age.

  380  Finally, since the greatest members of the world
  381  fight so among themselves, stirred by a war in no way holy,
  382  do you not see that some end of their long contest
  383  can be granted them, either when the sun and all heat
  384  have drunk up all the moisture and prevailed?
  385  This they strive to do, and have not yet carried out their attempt;
  386  so much do the rivers supply, and they threaten besides
  387  to flood all things from the deep gulf of the sea:
  388  in vain, since the winds that sweep the levels of the sea
  389  diminish it, and the sun in the ether, unweaving with rays,
  390  and they trust that they can dry all things up
  391  before the water can reach the end of its undertaking.
  392  Breathing such war in an equal contest,
  393  they strive to settle great matters between them,
  394  though once, meanwhile, fire has been the stronger,
  395  and once, as the story goes, water reigned over the fields.
  396  For fire prevailed and, ranging around, burned up much,
  397  when the ravening force of the sun's horses swept Phaethon
  398  off the road through the whole ether and over all the lands.
  399  But the father almighty, roused then with bitter anger,
  400  with a sudden stroke of the thunderbolt dashed great-hearted
  401  Phaethon from the horses to the earth, and the Sun, meeting him
  402  as he fell, caught up the eternal lamp of the world,
  403  and brought back the scattered horses and yoked them, trembling,
  404  then, steering them on their own road, restored all things,
  405  as, to be sure, the old poets of the Greeks have sung.
  406  But this is driven very far away from true reasoning.
  407  For fire can prevail when more bodies of matter
  408  have risen up out of the infinite;
  409  then its forces fall, overcome by some principle,
  410  or else things perish, burned up by the scorching air.
  411  Water likewise once rose up and began to prevail,
  412  as report says, when it drowned the lives of men in its waves;
  413  then, when that force was turned aside by some principle and withdrew,
  414  whatever of it had risen up out of the infinite,
  415  the rains stopped and the rivers lessened their force.
  416  But by what means that gathering of matter
  417  founded the earth and the sky and the depths of the sea,
  418  the courses of the sun and the moon, I shall set out in order.
  419  For certainly the first-beginnings of things did not by design
  420  set themselves each in its order with a shrewd mind,
  421  nor, surely, did they bargain what motions each should give;
  422  but because many first-beginnings of things in many ways,
  423  driven by blows from infinite time till now,
  424  and roused by their own weights, have been used to move
  425  and to meet in every way and to try out all things
  426  that they could create by meeting with one another,
  427  therefore it comes about that, spread abroad through a great age,
  428  by trying every kind of union and motion
  429  at last those come together which, suddenly thrown together,
  430  often become the origins of great things,
  431  of earth and sea and sky and the race of living things.
  432  At that time neither could the sun's wheel be seen with its ample light
  433  flying on high, nor the stars of the great world,
  434  nor sea nor sky nor, finally, earth nor air,
  435  nor could anything be seen like the things we know,
  436  but a kind of strange storm and a mass, risen up.
  437  Then parts began to fly apart from one another, and like things
  438  to join with like, and to mark off the world,
  439  and to divide its members and arrange its great parts
  440  out of beginnings of every kind, whose discord confounded
  441  intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows,
  442  collisions, motions, stirring up battles among them
  443  because of their unlike forms and varied shapes,
  444  since not all, joined thus, could stay together
  445  nor give one another fitting motions:
  446  that is, to separate the high sky from the earth,
  447  and the sea apart, to spread with its moisture set apart,
  448  and apart too the fires of the pure and separate ether.
  449  For indeed, first, all the bodies of earth,
  450  because they were heavy and entangled, came together
  451  in the middle and all took the lowest seats;
  452  and the more, entangled, they came together among themselves,
  453  the more they squeezed out those which would make the sea, the stars, the sun
  454  and the moon and the walls of the great world;
  455  for all these are made more of smooth and round
  456  seeds and of much smaller elements
  457  than the earth is. And so through the open pores of the earth,
  458  breaking out in parts, the ether first lifted itself up,
  459  fire-bearing, and being light carried off many fires with it,
  460  in a way not far different from what we often see
  461  when first, golden, through the grasses jeweled with dew,
  462  the morning lights of the rayed sun grow red,
  463  and the lakes and the ever-flowing rivers breathe out mist,
  464  and as at times the earth itself is seen to smoke;
  465  and when all these are gathered together above, on high,
  466  their body thickened, they weave clouds beneath the sky.
  467  So then at that time the light and spreading ether,
  468  its body thickened, set round on every side, fenced itself in,
  469  and spread out wide into all parts on every side,
  470  so fenced in all other things in its greedy embrace.
  471  After it followed the origins of the sun and moon,
  472  whose globes turn in the air between the two;
  473  these neither the earth nor the greatest ether claimed for itself,
  474  because they were neither so heavy that they settled, pressed down,

 1412  For what is at hand, unless we have known something before
 1413  sweeter, pleases above all and seems to prevail,
 1414  and as a rule the better thing found later
 1415  destroys it and changes our feelings toward all things of old.
 1416  So began disgust for the acorn, so those beds were abandoned,
 1417  strewn and heaped up with grass and leaves.
 1418  So too the clothing of wild beasts' skin fell into contempt;
 1419  which, I believe, when first found, stirred such envy
 1420  that he who first wore it met his death by ambush,
 1421  and yet, torn apart among them with much bloodshed,
 1422  it was wholly lost and could not be turned to any profit.
 1423  Then, therefore, it was skins; now gold and purple with their cares
 1424  harass the life of men and wear it out with war;
 1425  and in this, I think, the greater guilt resides with us.
 1426  For the cold tormented the naked, without their skins,
 1427  the earth-born; but it does us no harm to go without a robe
 1428  of purple, fitted out with gold and great embroidered figures,
 1429  so long as there is still a common one that can protect us.
 1430  Therefore the race of men labors to no purpose, in vain,
 1431  forever, and spends its life in empty cares,
 1432  doubtless because it has not learned what the limit is
 1433  of having, and how far at all true pleasure can grow;
 1434  and this, little by little, has carried life out into the deep
 1435  and stirred up from the bottom the great tides of war.
 1436  But the watchmen, traversing the world's great revolving precinct,
 1437  the sun and moon, encircling it with their light,
 1438  taught men thoroughly that the seasons of the years turn round
 1439  and that things go by a sure principle and in a sure order.
 1440  Already they lived their lives fenced by strong towers,
 1441  and the earth was divided, parceled out, and tilled;
 1442  then the sea was in flower with sail-winged ships,
 1443  already they had auxiliaries and allies by treaties struck,
 1444  when poets began in their songs to hand down
 1445  the deeds done; and not long before that were the elements found.
 1446  Therefore what was done before, our age
 1447  cannot look back on, except where reasoning shows the traces.
 1448  Ships and the tilling of fields, walls, laws,
 1449  arms, roads, clothes, and all the rest of this kind,
 1450  rewards, and all the luxuries of life without exception,
 1451  songs, pictures, and artful polished statues,
 1452  practice, together with the experience of the unflagging mind,
 1453  taught little by little as men advanced step by step.
 1454  So, little by little, time draws out each several thing
 1455  into the open, and reasoning raises it to the shores of light;
 1456  for they saw one thing after another grow clear in their hearts,
 1457  until by their arts they came to the highest peak.
