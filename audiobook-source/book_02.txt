    1  Sweet it is, when on the great sea the winds are churning the waters,
    2  to watch from land the great toil of another;
    3  not because anyone's distress is a delightful pleasure,
    4  but because it is sweet to see what evils you yourself are free of.
    6  Sweet, too, to look upon the great contests of war
    5  drawn up across the plains, with no share of your own in the danger;
    7  but nothing is sweeter than to hold, well fortified,
    8  by the teaching of the wise, the serene precincts set on high,
    9  from where you can look down on others and see them everywhere
   10  wandering and, straying here and there, seeking the road of life,
   11  contending in talent, competing in nobility of birth,
   12  striving night and day with surpassing labor
   13  to rise to the height of wealth and gain the mastery of things.
   14  O miserable minds of men, O blind hearts!
   15  In what darkness of life and in how great dangers
   16  this span of life, whatever it is, is passed! Do you not see
   17  that nature barks for nothing else for herself than this, that
   18  pain be parted from the body and be absent, and the mind enjoy
   19  a pleasant sense of things, set apart from care and fear?
   20  Therefore we see that for the body's nature few things
   21  are needed at all: such things as take away pain,
   22  and such that they can also spread out many delights for us,
   23  more welcome now and then; nor does nature herself require,
   24  if there are no golden images of youths about the halls
   25  holding fire-bearing lamps in their right hands
   26  so that light may be supplied for feasts by night,
   27  nor the house gleam with silver and shine with gold,
   28  nor lyres echo through paneled and gilded rafters,
   29  when still, lying together on the soft grass
   30  beside a stream of water under the boughs of a tall tree,
   31  with no great wealth they tend their bodies pleasantly,
   32  above all when the weather smiles and the seasons
   33  of the year sprinkle the greening grass with flowers.
   34  Nor do hot fevers leave the body any faster
   35  if you are tossed on woven pictures and blushing purple
   36  than if you must lie beneath a common man's coverlet.
   37  Therefore, since in our body treasures are of no profit,
   38  nor nobility, nor the glory of a kingdom,
   39  for the rest, we must think they do the mind no good either;
   40  unless perhaps, when you see your legions across the Campus
   41  seething as they stir up the images of war,
   42  made firm with great reserves and a force of wealth,
   43  your ships fitted out with arms, all alike full of spirit,
   44  then, before these things, the religions in their terror
   45  flee panic-stricken from your mind, and the fears of death
   46  then leave your heart empty and loosed from care.
   47  But if we see that these things are laughable and a mockery,
   48  and in truth the fears of men and the cares that dog them
   49  fear neither the clash of arms nor savage weapons,
   50  and boldly move among kings and the masters of things,
   51  and do not stand in awe of the glitter that comes from gold
   52  nor the bright splendor of a purple robe,
   53  why do you doubt that this power belongs wholly to reasoning,
   54  especially when all life labors in darkness?
   55  For just as children tremble and fear everything
   56  in blind darkness, so we in the light are afraid
   57  at times of things that are no more to be feared than
   58  what children in the dark shudder at and imagine will come.
   59  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness
   60  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
   61  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
   62  Come now, by what motion the generative bodies of matter
   63  beget the various things and dissolve them once begotten,
   64  and by what force they are compelled to do it, and what
   65  mobility is given them for travelling through the great void,
   66  I shall explain; you, remember to give yourself to my words.
   67  For certainly matter does not cling together, packed close
   68  in a mass, since we see each thing diminish,
   69  and we perceive all things flowing away, as it were, over long age,
   70  and age withdrawing them from our eyes,
   71  while yet the sum is seen to remain unimpaired,
   72  because the bodies that depart from each thing
   73  diminish what they leave, and on what they come to bestow increase.
   74  They force the one to grow old and the other in turn to flower,
   75  and do not linger there. So the sum of things is ever
   76  renewed, and mortals live by exchange among themselves.
   77  Some races increase, others are diminished,
   78  and in a brief space the generations of living things are changed
   79  and like runners pass on the torch of life.
   80  If you think the first-beginnings of things can stand still
   81  and by standing still beget new motions of things,
   82  you wander far astray from true reasoning.
   83  For since they wander through the void, all the first-beginnings
   84  of things must be carried either by their own weight
   85  or perhaps by the blow of another. For when, swift, they have often
   86  met and clashed, it comes about that suddenly they spring
   87  apart in different ways; and no wonder, since they are very hard,
   88  solid in their weight, and nothing blocks them from behind.
   89  And so that you may see more clearly that all the bodies
   90  of matter are tossed about, remember that the whole
   91  has no bottom in the sum, and the first bodies have no place
   92  to come to rest, since space is without end or limit,
   93  and spreads immeasurable in all directions everywhere
   94  I have shown in many words and proved by sure reasoning.
   95  And since this is settled, surely no rest
   96  is given to the first bodies through the deep void,
   97  but rather, driven by unceasing and varied motion,
   98  some, when they have collided, leap back over great intervals,
   99  and some are harried by the blow within short spaces.
  100  And all those that, in a denser union,
  101  brought together, rebound across small intervals,
  102  entangled by their own interlocking shapes,
  103  these form the strong roots of rock and the savage bodies
  104  of iron, and the other things of this kind.
  105  Those few others that wander on through the great void
  106  leap far apart and run far back again
  107  across great intervals; these furnish us the thin air
  108  and the shining light of the sun.
  109  And many besides wander through the great void
  110  which have been thrown out of the unions of things and nowhere
  111  have been able, even when let in, to join their motions together.
  112  Of this thing, as I tell, an image and picture
  113  is always turning before our eyes and pressing on us.
  114  For look closely, whenever the sun's light is poured
  115  by rays let in through the darkened places of houses:
  116  you will see many minute bodies in many ways, through the void,
  117  mingling in the very light of the rays,
  118  and as though in everlasting conflict waging wars and battles,
  119  fighting troop by troop, and giving no pause,
  120  driven on by frequent unions and partings;
  121  so that from this you may guess what it is for the first-beginnings
  122  of things to be tossed forever in the great void.
  123  So far as it goes, a small thing can give
  124  a pattern of great things and the tracks of knowledge.
  125  For this reason, too, it is more fitting that you attend to these
  126  bodies that are seen to be in turmoil in the sun's rays,
  127  because such turmoils signify that there are motions
  128  of matter, too, hidden and blind, beneath them.
  129  For you will see many there, struck by blind blows,
  130  change their course and, driven back, return,
  131  now this way, now that, in all directions everywhere.
  132  This wandering, you may be sure, all comes from the beginnings.
  133  For first the first-beginnings of things move of themselves;
  134  then those bodies that are formed of a small union
  135  and are, as it were, nearest to the strength of the beginnings
  136  are set going, struck by their blind blows,
  137  and they in turn assail bodies a little larger.
  138  So motion rises from the beginnings and comes out
  139  little by little to our senses, so that those too
  140  are moved which we can make out in the light of the sun,
  141  though by what blows they do it does not plainly appear.

  142  Now what mobility has been given to the bodies
  143  of matter, Memmius, you may learn from this in a few words.
  144  First, when dawn sprinkles the lands with new light,
  145  and the many-colored birds, flitting through the pathless woods,
  146  fill the places through the soft air with liquid voices,
  147  how suddenly the sun, once risen, is used at such a time
  148  to clothe all things, flooding them with his light,
  149  we see to be ready to hand and plain for all.
  150  But that heat which the sun sends out, and its calm light,
  151  does not travel through empty void; and so it is forced
  152  to go more slowly, as it beats its way, as it were, through waves of air;
  153  nor do the little bodies of heat travel one by one,
  154  but tangled with one another and massed together;
  155  and so at once they are dragged back by one another and hindered
  156  from outside, so that they are forced to go more slowly.
  157  But the first-beginnings that are of solid singleness,
  158  when they travel through empty void and nothing delays them
  159  from outside, and they themselves, one whole from their own parts,
  160  are borne, straining, toward the one place they began to move to,
  161  must surely surpass in mobility
  162  and be carried far more swiftly than the light of the sun,
  163  and run through many times the extent of space in the same
  164  time in which the sun's flashes spread through heaven.
  165  nor to follow out each single first-beginning,
  166  to see by what principle each thing comes about.
  167  But some, against this, ignorant of matter,
  168  hold that nature cannot, without the power of the gods,
  169  so closely fitted, in such measure, to human needs,
  170  change the seasons of the years and create the crops
  171  and all the other things that mortals are urged to approach,
  172  and to which divine pleasure herself, the guide of life, leads them,
  173  coaxing them through the works of Venus to propagate their generations,
  174  so that the human race should not perish. When they imagine
  175  the gods have ordered all things for men's sake, in every way
  176  they seem to have slipped greatly from true reasoning.
  177  For even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  178  this, still, from the very order of heaven I would dare
  179  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  180  that by no means for us, by divine power, was created
  181  the nature of the world: so great the flaws it stands endowed with.
  182  These things, Memmius, I will make plain to you later;
  183  now I will set out what remains concerning motions.
  184  Now is the place, I think, in these matters to prove this too
  185  to you: that no bodily thing can by its own force
  186  be carried upward and travel upward.
  187  Do not let the bodies of flames deceive you in this;
  188  for they are born upward and take on their increase,
  189  and upward the shining crops and the trees grow,
  190  though all weights, as far as in them lies, are carried downward.
  191  Nor, when fires leap up to the roofs of houses
  192  and with swift flame lick the rafters and beams,
  193  must we think they do this of themselves, with no force thrust beneath.
  194  Just so the blood let out from our body
  195  spurts leaping on high and scatters gore.
  196  Do you not see, too, with what force the water's moisture
  197  spits out rafters and beams? For the more we have pushed them deep,
  198  straight down, and many with great force have pressed them, straining,
  199  the more eagerly it thrusts them back up and sends them out,
  200  so that they rise and leap out by more than half their length.
  201  And yet we do not doubt, I think, that these, as far as in them lies,
  202  are all carried downward through the empty void.
  203  So in the same way flames too must be able, through the breezes
  204  of air, squeezed out, to climb upward, although
  205  their weights, as far as in them lies, fight to draw them down.
  206  And the night torches of heaven, flying aloft,
  207  do you not see them draw long trails of flame
  208  toward whatever quarter nature has given them passage?
  209  Do you not see stars and constellations falling to the earth?
  210  The sun also from the summit of heaven scatters its heat
  211  to every side and sows the fields with light;
  212  to the earth, therefore, the sun's heat also tends.
  213  And you see thunderbolts fly slantwise through the rains;
  214  now from here, now from there, fires broken from the clouds
  215  rush together; the flaming force falls everywhere upon the earth.
  216  This too, in these matters, we long for you to know:
  217  that when bodies are carried straight down through the void
  218  by their own weights, at quite uncertain times
  219  and uncertain places they push off a little from their course,
  220  just so much that you could call it a change of motion.
  221  For if they were not used to swerve, all things would fall
  222  downward like drops of rain through the deep void,
  223  and no collision would arise, no blow be created
  224  for the beginnings; so nature would never have created anything.
  225  But if perhaps someone believes that heavier bodies,
  226  because they are carried more swiftly straight through the void,
  227  can fall from above upon the lighter, and so beget
  228  blows that could give rise to generative motions,
  229  he strays far away, off the road, from true reasoning.
  230  For whatever falls through water and thin air,
  231  these must quicken their fall in proportion to their weights,
  232  because the body of water and the thin nature
  233  of air cannot delay each thing equally,
  234  but give way more quickly, overcome by heavier things;
  235  but the empty void, by contrast, cannot on any side or at any
  236  time, hold up anything at all,
  237  but must go on giving way, as its nature seeks;
  238  therefore all things must, through the calm void,
  239  be carried at equal speed, though driven by unequal weights.
  240  Therefore heavier things will never be able to fall
  241  from above upon lighter ones, nor of themselves beget the blows
  242  that vary the motions by which nature carries on things.
  243  Therefore, again and again, bodies must incline
  244  a little; but no more than the least, so that we do not seem to invent
  245  slanting motions, and the true facts refute it.
  246  For this we see to be ready to hand and plain,
  247  that weights, as far as in them lies, cannot travel slantwise
  248  when they plunge down from above, so far as you can see;
  249  but that nothing at all, from the straight line of its path,
  250  swerves of itself, who is there that could perceive?
  251  Finally, if every motion is always linked,
  252  and the new always arises from the old in a fixed order,
  253  and the first-beginnings do not by swerving make
  254  some beginning of motion to break the covenants of fate,
  255  so that cause should not follow cause from infinity,
  256  from where does it arise, free, for living things throughout the earth,
  257  from where, I ask, is this will wrested away from the fates,
  258  by which we go forward wherever pleasure leads each one,
  259  and likewise swerve our motions, at no fixed time
  260  and at no fixed place, but where the mind itself has carried us?
  261  For beyond doubt in these things each one's own will
  262  gives the beginning, and from it motions flow out through the limbs.
  263  Do you not see too that, when in an instant of time the starting-gates
  264  are thrown open, the eager strength of the horses still cannot
  265  burst forth as suddenly as their mind itself longs to?
  266  For all the matter throughout the whole body, its whole
  267  store, must be roused, so that, roused through all the limbs,
  268  it may strain together and follow the mind's desire;
  269  so you may see that the start of motion is created by the heart,
  270  and that it goes forth first from the will of the mind,
  271  and is then passed on through the whole body and limbs.
  272  Nor is it the same as when we go forward pushed by a blow,
  273  by another's great strength and great compulsion;
  274  for then all the matter of the whole body,
  275  it is clear, goes and is swept along against our will,
  276  until the will reins it back throughout the limbs.
  277  Do you see now, then, that although an outside force drives many
  278  and often compels them against their will to go forward
  279  and be swept headlong, still there is in our breast
  280  something that can fight against it and resist?
  281  At whose command the store of matter too
  282  is forced at times to turn through the limbs and joints,
  283  and, thrown forward, is reined back and settles back again.
  284  Therefore in the seeds too you must admit the same,
  285  that there is besides blows and weights another cause
  286  of motions, from which this power is born in us,
  287  since we see that nothing can come to be from nothing.
  288  For weight prevents all things from coming about by blows,
  289  as by a force from outside; but that the thing itself should not
  290  hold an inward necessity in doing all things,
  291  and be compelled, as if conquered, to bear and suffer,
  292  this is brought about by the tiny swerve of the beginnings
  293  at no fixed place and at no fixed time.

  294  Nor was the store of matter ever more tightly packed
  295  than now, nor again spread apart by greater intervals;
  296  for nothing is added to it, and nothing perishes from it.
  297  Therefore the motion in which the bodies of the beginnings
  298  are now is the same motion they were in through ages gone by,
  299  and hereafter they will always be carried in like manner,
  300  and what has been wont to be born will be born under the same
  301  condition, and will be, and grow, and be strong in their strength,
  302  as much as is granted to each by nature's covenants.
  303  Nor can any force change the sum of things;
  304  for there is nothing outside into which any kind of matter
  305  could flee out of the whole, nor any source from which, into the whole,
  306  a new force could rise and burst in and change the whole
  307  nature of things and turn its motions aside.
  308  In these matters it is no wonder why,
  309  although all the first-beginnings of things are in motion,
  310  the sum nonetheless seems to stand in utmost rest,
  311  except where some thing makes motions with its own body.
  312  For the whole nature of the first bodies lies far below
  313  our senses, out of reach; therefore, since you cannot see
  314  the bodies themselves, their motions too must steal away from sight;
  315  the more so since even things that we can see still often hide
  316  their motions when withdrawn from us by a distance of place.
  317  For often on a hill, cropping the glad pastures,
  318  the woolly flocks creep on wherever, calling each one,
  319  the grasses invite them, jeweled with fresh dew,
  320  and the lambs, filled full, play and gently butt their heads;
  321  yet all these, seen from far off, seem to us blurred together,
  322  like a whiteness standing still on a green hill.
  323  Again, when great legions fill the spaces of the plains
  324  with their running, stirring up the images of war,
  325  when the glitter lifts itself to the sky and all the earth around
  326  shines back with bronze, and beneath, from the force of men,
  327  a sound is roused by their feet, and the mountains, struck
  328  by the shouting, throw back the voices to the stars of the world,
  329  and horsemen wheel around and suddenly cross through
  330  the middle of the plains, shaking them with their strong charge;
  331  and yet there is some place on the high mountains from which
  332  they seem to stand still, a glitter resting on the plains.
  333  Come now, next learn of what sort the origins
  334  of all things are, and how far they differ in their forms,
  335  how varied they are, in shapes of many kinds;
  336  not that few are endowed with a like form,
  337  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  338  Nor is it a wonder; for since their store is so great
  339  that there is neither end, as I have taught, nor any sum to it,
  340  it must surely be that not all are in every way
  341  alike to all, of the same contour and of similar shape.
  342  Further, the human race, and the mute swimming flocks
  343  of scaly creatures, and the glad herds, and wild beasts,
  344  and the various birds, those that throng the gladdening
  345  watering-places around the banks and springs and lakes,
  346  and those that flit abroad through the pathless woods:
  347  go on and take any one of these you will, kind by kind;
  348  you will find that they still differ from one another in shape.
  349  Nor in any other way could offspring know its mother,
  350  nor mother offspring; yet we see that they can,
  351  and that they are known to one another no less than men are.
  352  For often before the fair shrines of the gods a calf
  353  falls slaughtered beside the incense-burning altars,
  354  breathing out a warm river of blood from its breast;
  355  but the bereaved mother, wandering through the green glades,
  356  knows the prints pressed into the ground by cloven hooves,
  357  searching every place with her eyes, if she can anywhere
  358  catch sight of her lost young, and, stopping, fills
  359  the leafy grove with her lament, and often goes back
  360  to the stall, pierced through with longing for her calf;
  361  nor can tender willows, nor grasses quickened with dew,
  362  nor any rivers gliding level with their banks
  363  delight her mind and turn aside the sudden care,
  364  nor can the sight of other calves in the glad pastures
  365  draw her mind away and lighten it of care;
  366  so much does she seek something her own and known.
  367  Again, the tender kids with trembling voices
  368  know their horned mothers, and the butting lambs
  369  the bleating flocks; so, as nature demands,
  370  each runs, for the most part, to its own udder of milk.
  371  Finally, take any grain you will: you will still not see
  372  that every grain in its kind is like the others,
  373  without some difference of form running between them.
  374  And by a like principle we see the race of shells
  375  paint the lap of the earth, where with soft waves
  376  the sea beats the thirsty sand of the curving shore.
  377  Therefore again and again by like reasoning it must be,
  378  since they exist by nature and were not made by the hand
  379  of one, to a fixed form, the first-beginnings of things,
  380  that some of them fly about with shapes unlike each other.
  381  It is very easy for us, by the mind's reasoning, to explain
  382  why the fire of the thunderbolt is far more piercing
  383  than ours, which flows from torches, born of the earth;
  384  for you could say that the heavenly fire of the thunderbolt
  385  is subtler, consisting of small shapes,
  386  and so passes through openings that this fire of ours,
  387  born from wood and created from the torch, cannot.
  388  Again, light passes through horn, but rain
  389  is thrown back. Why so, unless those bodies of light
  390  are smaller than the ones that make the kindly liquid of the waters?
  391  And although we see wine in an instant pass
  392  through a strainer, yet on the other hand oil lingers slow,
  393  either because, surely, it is made of larger elements,
  394  or ones more hooked and tangled with one another,
  395  and so it happens that they cannot be so suddenly drawn apart
  396  from one another that the first-beginnings, each one,
  397  might, one by one, seep through the openings of each thing.
  398  Add to this that the liquids of honey and of milk
  399  are handled in the mouth with a pleasant feeling on the tongue;
  400  but on the other hand the loathsome nature of wormwood and of wild
  401  centaury twists the mouth with its foul taste;
  402  so that you can easily recognize that of smooth and round bodies
  403  are made the things that can touch the senses pleasantly,
  404  but on the other hand whatever seems bitter and harsh,
  405  these are held bound together by more hooked ones,
  406  and for that reason are used to tear their way into our
  407  senses, and to burst through the body as they enter.
  408  Finally, all things good to the senses and bad to the touch
  409  are at war with one another, made of unlike shape;
  410  lest you should perhaps think that the harsh shudder
  411  of the screeching saw consists of elements as smooth
  412  as the Muses' melodies, which musicians, along the strings,
  413  wake with nimble fingers and give form to;
  414  nor should you think that first-beginnings of like form pierce
  415  into men's nostrils when foul corpses are burning
  416  and when the stage is freshly sprinkled with Cilician saffron
  417  and the altar nearby breathes out the perfumes of Panchaea;
  418  nor suppose the pleasant colors of things are made of like
  419  seed, the colors that can feast the eyes,
  420  and those that prick the sight and force it to weep,
  421  or look foul and ugly with their foul appearance.
  422  For every thing whatsoever that caresses the senses
  423  was not created without some smoothness in its beginnings;
  424  but on the other hand whatever is troublesome and harsh
  425  is not found without some roughness of matter.
  426  There are also things that are rightly thought neither to be smooth
  427  nor altogether hooked with bent points,
  428  but rather made with little angles jutting out a little, such as
  429  can tickle the senses rather than hurt them;
  430  of which kind are wine-lees and the flavors of elecampane.
  431  Finally, that hot fires and icy frost
  432  are toothed in unlike ways and so prick the senses
  433  of the body, the touch of each gives us proof.
  434  For touch, touch, by the holy powers of the gods,
  435  is the sense of the body, whether when a thing from outside
  436  works its way in, or when a thing born in the body hurts it,
  437  or gives delight going out through the generative acts of Venus,
  438  or when, from a collision, they riot within the body itself,
  439  and the seeds, roused, throw the sense into confusion among themselves;
  440  as if perhaps with your hand you yourself were to strike
  441  any part of your body you please and make the trial.
  442  Therefore the forms of the beginnings must differ far,
  443  since they can give rise to various sensations.
  444  Finally, the things that seem to us hardened and dense,
  445  these must consist of elements more hooked together
  446  and be held fast, compacted deep, as if by branches.
  447  In this class, first of all, stand rocks of adamant
  448  in the front line, used to scorning blows,
  449  and strong flints, and the strength of hard iron,
  450  and bronze that shrieks as it holds out against the bolts.
  451  Those indeed must be made rather of smooth and round bodies,
  452  the things that stand as liquids, with a fluid body.
  453  For a scoop of poppy-seed flows as easily as one of water;
  454  for the little globes do not hold to one another,
  455  and, once disturbed, it runs as readily down a slope.
  456  Finally, all things that you see in a point of time
  457  scatter, like smoke and mists and flames, it must be that,
  458  if they are not all made of smooth and round bodies,
  459  yet they are not hampered by tangled ones,
  460  so that they can prick the body and pierce stones
  461  and yet not stick together; so that whatever we see
  462  to be toothed to the senses, you can easily know
  463  is made not of tangled but of sharp elements.
  464  But that you see the same things bitter that are fluid,
  465  as the sweat of the sea is, ought to be no wonder at all
  466  For because it is fluid, it is made of smooth and round bodies,
  467  but mixed in with the smooth are hooked
  468  bodies of pain. Yet these hooked ones need not be held fast:

  469  clearly they are round nonetheless, rough though they are,
  470  so that they can roll forward together and yet hurt the senses.
  471  And that you may believe the more that rough are mixed with smooth
  472  beginnings, of which the bitter body of Neptune consists,
  473  there is a way of separating them and seeing them apart:
  474  the moisture grows sweet when, through the earth, the same moisture
  475  is filtered through many times, to flow into a pit and grow mild;
  476  for it leaves behind, above, the first-beginnings of the foul brine,
  477  since, being rough, they can cling the more within the earth.
  478  Since I have taught this, I will go on to join to it a fact
  479  which, fitted to this, draws its credit: that the first-beginnings of things
  480  vary within a finite range of shapes.
  481  For if it were not so, then again certain seeds
  482  will have to be infinite in the growth of their bodies.
  483  For within the one same smallness of any given
  484  body, the shapes cannot vary much from one another;
  485  they cannot. For suppose that of least parts, three of them,
  486  the first bodies are made, or add a few more to these;
  487  surely, when all those parts of a single body
  488  you have set at top and bottom, and changed the right for the left,
  489  and tried in every way what appearance each order gives
  490  of form to the whole of that body,
  491  for the rest, if you chance to want to vary the shapes,
  492  other parts will have to be added. From this it will follow
  493  that, on a like principle, the order will call for others,
  494  if you chance to want to vary the shapes still further.
  495  Therefore growth of body follows upon newness of forms
  496  close behind. And so there is no way you can believe
  497  that seeds exist distinguished by infinite forms,
  498  lest you force certain of them to be of monstrous greatness,
  499  which I have already shown above cannot be proved.
  500  Then barbarian robes and the gleaming Meliboean purple,
  501  dipped in the Thessalian color of the shells,
  502  the golden race of peacocks, steeped in laughing charm,
  503  would lie outdone by some new color in things,
  504  and the smell of myrrh would be scorned, and the savors of honey,
  505  and the swan's notes and the intricate strains of Phoebus' strings
  506  would fall silent, overpowered in like manner;
  507  for one thing would always rise more excellent than another.
  508  So too all things could fall back toward the worse,
  509  just as we have said they could toward the better;
  510  for one thing would also be more loathsome than another, in reverse,
  511  to nostrils, ears and eyes, and to the taste of the mouth.
  512  And since these things are not so, but a fixed limit, assigned
  513  to things, holds the sum on either side, you must admit
  514  that matter too differs in a finite number of shapes.
  515  Finally, from fires to the freezing frosts of winters
  516  the way is bounded, and measured back again in equal measure.
  517  For all heat and cold and the middle warmths
  518  lie between the two, filling out the sum in order.
  519  Therefore created things differ within a finite range,
  520  since at both ends they are marked by a two-edged point,
  521  beset by flames on this side and on that by stiff frosts.
  522  Since I have taught this, I will go on to join to it a fact
  523  which, fitted to this, draws its credit: that the first-beginnings of things,
  524  those that are made of a shape alike among themselves,
  525  are infinite in number. For since the difference
  526  of forms is finite, it must be that those that are alike
  527  are infinite, or else that the sum of matter
  528  is finite, which I have proved is not so.
  529  I will show in verses that the little bodies of matter
  530  from infinity keep the sum of things going
  531  on every side, by an unbroken relay of blows.
  532  For as to your seeing certain animals to be rarer,
  533  and discerning in those others a nature more fruitful,
  534  yet in another region and place, in far-off lands,
  535  there may be many of that kind, and the number made up;
  536  as among four-footed beasts we see, first of all,
  537  the kind of snake-handed elephants, by whose many thousands
  538  India is fortified with a rampart of ivory,
  539  so that it cannot be entered deep: so great is the force
  540  of these beasts, of which we see very few examples.
  541  But still, to concede this too, let there be, as you please,
  542  some single thing, alone in the body it was born with,
  543  with nothing like it in the whole round of the lands;
  544  yet unless the store of matter is infinite,
  545  from which it may be conceived and brought forth, it could not
  546  be created, nor, for the rest, grow up and be nourished.
  547  For indeed, let others assume that a finite number
  548  of generative bodies of one thing are tossed through the whole:
  549  from where, where, by what force, in what way will they meet and join
  550  in so great a sea of matter, in so alien a throng?
  551  They have no means, I think, of making a union;
  552  but as, when great and many shipwrecks have arisen,
  553  the great sea is used to toss about thwarts, hulls,
  554  yards, prows, masts and swimming oars,
  555  so that along all the lands' shores the floating stern-ornaments
  556  are seen, and give mortals a warning
  557  to shun the snares of the faithless sea, its strength and guile,
  558  and never at any time to trust it,
  559  when the sly enticement of the calm sea laughs,
  560  so, if once you establish certain first-beginnings as finite,
  561  they will have to be scattered through all time
  562  and tossed apart by the conflicting tides of matter,
  563  so that they can never be driven together into union,
  564  nor linger in union, nor grow by increase;
  565  and that both of these happen, the plain fact openly shows,
  566  that things are brought forth and, once born, can grow.
  567  Therefore in any kind you please the first-beginnings of things
  568  are infinite, that is plain, from which all things are furnished.
  569  And so neither can the motions of destruction prevail
  570  forever, nor bury safety for eternity,
  571  nor again can the motions that beget and increase things
  572  keep forever what they have created.
  573  So in an equal struggle of the beginnings is waged
  574  a war entered on from infinite time.
  575  Now here, now there the life-giving powers of things conquer
  576  and are conquered in turn. With the funeral is mixed the wail
  577  that infants raise as they look on the shores of light;
  578  and no night has followed day, no dawn the night,
  579  that has not heard, mixed with the sick wailing of infants,
  580  the lamentations that attend on death and the black funeral.

  581  This too it is fitting, in these matters, to keep sealed up
  582  and to hold, entrusted to a mind that remembers:
  583  that there is nothing whose nature is seen plainly before us
  584  that consists of one kind of beginnings alone,
  585  nothing that is not made of mingled seed.
  586  And whatever possesses more forces in itself
  587  and more powers, the more it shows that it holds the most kinds
  588  of beginnings in itself, and the most varied shapes.
  589  First of all, the earth holds in itself first bodies
  590  from which springs, rolling out their coolness, constantly renew
  591  the immeasurable sea; it holds those from which fires rise;
  592  for in many places the ground of the earth is kindled and burns,
  593  and from its depths the onrush of Etna rages with fire.
  594  Then further, shining crops and glad orchards
  595  it has the means to raise up for the races of men,
  596  and the means to offer rivers, leaves, and glad pastures
  597  to the mountain-roaming race of wild beasts.
  598  Therefore the great mother of the gods, and mother of wild beasts,
  599  and begetter of our body, she alone has been called.
  600  Of her the learned poets of the Greeks of old have sung
  601  that, seated in a chariot, she drives a yoke of lions,
  602  teaching by this that the great earth hangs in the space
  603  of air, and that earth cannot rest upon earth.
  604  They yoked wild beasts to her because offspring, however wild,
  605  must be softened, won over by the kindnesses of parents.
  606  And they bound the top of her head with a crown of walls,
  607  because, fortified in choice places, she bears up cities.
  608  Adorned now with this emblem, through the great lands
  609  the image of the divine mother is carried, inspiring dread.
  610  Her the various peoples, by the ancient custom of their rites,
  611  call the Idaean Mother, and give her bands of Phrygians
  612  as her companions, because from those regions first, they say,
  613  crops began to be created through the circles of the earth.
  614  They assign her Galli, because, of those who have outraged
  615  the Mother's power and been found ungrateful to their parents,
  616  they wish to signify that they must be thought unworthy
  617  to bring forth living offspring into the shores of light.
  618  Taut drums thunder under their palms, and the hollow cymbals
  619  all around, and horns threaten with harsh-sounding song,
  620  and the hollow pipe goads their minds with Phrygian measure,
  621  and they carry weapons before them, signs of violent frenzy,
  622  so that the ungrateful minds and impious hearts of the crowd
  623  may be terrified with fear by the goddess's power.
  624  And so, as soon as she is carried in through great cities
  625  and silently enriches mortals with a wordless blessing,
  626  they strew the whole road of her way with bronze and silver,
  627  enriching it with bountiful alms, and snow down rose
  628  blossoms, shading the mother and the band of her companions.
  629  Here an armed troop, whom the Greeks speak of by the name
  630  of Curetes, when they happen to sport among the Phrygian bands
  631  and leap in measure, glad of blood,
  632  shaking the terrifying crests on their heads with their nodding,
  633  recall the Dictaean Curetes, who are said once to have hidden
  634  that famous wailing of Jupiter in Crete,
  635  when, boys around the boy, in their nimble dance,
  636  armed and in measure in their nimble dance
  637  armed, in measure, they beat bronze upon bronze,
  638  so that Saturn might not catch him and give him to his jaws
  639  and deal the Mother an eternal wound beneath her breast.
  640  For this reason they attend the great mother armed,
  641  or else because they signify that the goddess proclaims that with arms
  642  and courage men should be willing to defend their native land
  643  and make ready to be a protection and an honor to their parents.
  644  These things, however well and excellently they are set out,
  645  are nonetheless driven far from true reasoning.
  646  For the whole nature of the gods must, of itself, by necessity
  647  enjoy immortal life in utmost peace,
  648  removed from our affairs and separated far away;
  649  for free from every pain, free from dangers,
  650  strong in its own resources, needing nothing of ours,
  651  it is neither won by services nor touched by anger.
  652  The earth, indeed, is at all times without sense,
  653  and because it possesses the first-beginnings of many things,
  654  it brings forth many in many ways into the light of the sun.
  655  Here, if anyone resolves to call the sea Neptune, and to call
  656  the crops Ceres, and would rather misuse the name of Bacchus
  657  than speak the proper word for that liquor,
  658  let us grant that he may keep declaring the round of the earth
  659  to be the mother of the gods, if only in truth he himself
  680  refrains from tainting his mind with foul religion.
  660  Thus often, cropping grass from a single field,
  661  wool-bearing flocks and the warlike offspring of horses
  662  and horned herds, beneath the same covering of sky,
  663  quenching their thirst from a single stream of water,
  664  live unlike in appearance, and keep their parents'
  665  nature, and each, kind by kind, copies their ways.
  666  So great in any kind of grass is the unlike order
  667  of matter, so great in any river too.
  668  Again, any one living creature, whichever of them all,
  669  bones, blood, veins, heat, moisture, flesh, and sinews
  670  make up, which are again far distant from one another,
  671  formed of unlike shapes of beginnings.
  672  Then further, all things that are set aflame by fire and burn,
  673  if nothing else, still carry these in their body:
  674  the means by which they can throw out fire and send up light,
  675  and drive off sparks and scatter ash far and wide.
  676  Going through the rest by a like reasoning of mind,
  677  you will therefore find that they hide the seeds of many things
  678  in their body, and contain varied shapes.
  679  Finally, you see many things in which color and taste together
  681  are given along with smell; first of all, most fruits.
  682  These, therefore, must consist of varied shapes;
  683  for smell gets into our limbs where color does not go,
  684  color, likewise, apart, and taste apart, works its way
  685  into our senses; so you may know they differ in their first shapes.
  686  Therefore unlike forms come together into one mass,
  687  and things are made up out of mingled seed.
  688  Indeed, everywhere in these very verses of ours
  689  you see many elements common to many words,
  690  though you must still admit that verses and words
  691  are made, one and another, from different elements;
  692  not because few letters run in common among them,
  693  or no two are made out of letters entirely the same,
  694  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  695  So likewise in other things, since many are common
  696  to many things, the first-beginnings of things
  697  can nonetheless make up, among themselves, unlike wholes;
  698  so that it may rightly be said that the human race
  699  and crops and glad orchards are made of different things.
  700  Yet we must not think that all things can be joined
  701  in every way; for you would see monsters everywhere come to be,
  702  forms half beast, half man spring up, and tall
  703  branches at times put forth from a living body,
  704  and many limbs of land creatures joined to those of the sea,
  705  and then Chimaeras, breathing flame from their foul mouths,
  706  and nature feeding them across the all-mothering lands.
  707  It is plain that none of this happens, since all things
  708  created from fixed seeds and by a fixed mother,
  709  can, we see, preserve their kind as they grow.
  710  Clearly this must come about by a fixed principle.
  711  For to each, out of all its foods, its own bodies pass
  712  within, into the limbs, and, once joined, produce
  713  the fitting motions; but against this we see nature
  714  cast out onto the earth what is alien, and many things, in unseen
  715  bodies, flee out of the body, driven by blows,
  716  which could neither be joined anywhere nor, within,
  717  feel with the vital motions and imitate them.
  718  But lest you think that only living things are held
  719  by these laws, a certain principle bounds all things.
  720  For just as things begotten are unlike one another
  721  in their whole nature, each of them, so each must
  722  be made of unlike shapes of beginnings;
  723  not that few are endowed with a like form,
  724  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  725  Further, since the seeds differ, there must be differences in
  726  intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows,
  727  collisions, motions; which not only divide living bodies
  728  from one another, but keep the lands and the whole sea
  729  apart, and hold back the whole heaven from the lands.

  730  Come now, take in these words, sought out by my sweet labor,
  731  so that you do not by chance suppose the white things are from white
  732  beginnings, the things you see shining before your eyes,
  733  or that the things that are black are born of black seed;
  734  or that whatever is steeped in any other color
  735  bears it because the bodies of matter
  736  are dyed with a color just like its own;
  737  for the bodies of matter have no color
  738  at all, neither like things nor, finally, unlike them.
  739  And if it seems to you perhaps that upon these bodies no casting
  740  of the mind can be made, you wander far out of the way.
  741  For since the blind from birth, who have never looked upon
  742  the light of the sun, still come to know bodies by touch
  743  that from the beginning of life were joined with no color,
  744  you may know that bodies can also come into the notice
  745  of our mind, though smeared about with no dye.
  746  Finally, whatever we ourselves touch in blind darkness
  747  we do not perceive as dyed with any color.
  748  Since I prove that this can be, I shall now show that it is so.
  749  For every color changes, all of it, into every other;
  750  this the first-beginnings must by no means do;
  751  for something unchangeable must survive,
  752  so that things are not all brought back utterly to nothing;
  753  for whatever is changed and passes out of its own bounds,
  754  this at once is the death of what it was before.
  755  Therefore take care not to touch the seeds of things with color,
  756  so that all things do not return utterly to nothing for you.
  757  Further, if no nature of color has been given
  758  to the beginnings, and they are endowed with various forms,
  759  from which they beget colors of every kind and vary them,
  760  therefore, since it matters greatly for each of the seeds
  761  with what and in what position they are held together,
  762  and what motions they give and receive among themselves,
  763  at once you could most easily give the reason
  764  why things that were of a black color a little before
  765  can suddenly become of a marble whiteness,
  766  as the sea, when great winds have stirred its levels,
  767  is turned into white waves of shining marble;
  768  for you could say that what we often see as black,
  769  when its matter has been mixed through, and the order
  770  of the beginnings changed, and certain things added and taken away,
  771  at once comes to be seen as shining and white.
  772  But if the levels of the sea consisted of blue
  773  seeds, they could in no way grow white;
  774  for however you stir up things that are blue,
  775  they can never pass over into the color of marble.
  776  But if the seeds are dyed with one color and another
  777  that make the sea's one pure brightness,
  778  as often from other forms and various shapes
  779  something square and of a single shape is made,
  780  it would be fitting that, as we see in the square
  781  unlike forms, so we should see in the level of the sea,
  782  or in any other single and pure brightness,
  783  colors widely unlike one another and various.
  784  Further, unlike shapes do not hinder or stand in the way
  785  of the whole being square on the outside;
  786  but the various colors of things hinder and prevent
  787  the whole thing from being of a single brightness.
  788  Then again the cause that leads and lures us to assign
  789  colors at times to the beginnings of things
  790  falls dead, since white things are not created from white,
  791  nor things called black from black, but from various.
  792  For indeed white things will arise much more readily
  793  born of no color than of black
  794  or of any other that fights against them and stands in the way.
  795  Further, since colors cannot be without light,
  796  and the first-beginnings of things do not come out into the light,
  797  you may know that they are veiled with no color;
  798  for what color can there be in blind darkness?
  799  Indeed, color is changed by the light itself, according as
  800  it shines back, struck by a straight or a slanting light;
  801  as the plumage of doves is seen in the sun,
  802  the plumage set about the nape that crowns the neck;
  803  for at times it comes to be red with bright fire-bronze,
  804  at times, to a certain way of seeing, it comes to seem
  805  to mingle green emeralds with the blue.
  806  And the peacock's tail, when it is filled with plentiful light,
  807  changes its colors by a like principle as it turns;
  808  and since these colors are begotten by a certain stroke of light,
  809  you may know it must be thought they cannot come to be without it.
  810  And since the pupil receives into itself a certain kind of blow
  811  when it is said to perceive the color white,
  812  and again another when it perceives black and the rest,
  813  and it does not matter with what color the things you touch
  814  may chance to be endowed, but rather with what shape they are fitted,
  815  you may know that the beginnings have no need of color,
  816  but give out various touches by their various forms.
  817  Further, since no fixed nature of color belongs to fixed
  818  shapes, and all the formings of the beginnings
  819  can be in any brightness whatever,
  820  why are the things composed of them not likewise
  821  steeped in colors of every sort, in every kind of thing?
  822  For it would be fitting that ravens too, as they fly, should often
  823  throw off a white color from white feathers,
  824  and that swans should become black from black seed,
  825  or of any other color, single or varied.
  826  Moreover, the more each thing is pulled apart into tiny parts,
  827  the more it is that you can see
  828  the color fade little by little and be quenched;
  829  as happens when purple cloth is torn into small parts:
  830  the purple, and the scarlet, much the brightest of colors,
  831  when pulled apart thread by thread, is wholly lost;
  832  so that from this you may learn that all their color is breathed out
  833  by the particles before they part into the seeds of things.
  834  Lastly, since you grant that not all bodies send out
  835  a voice, nor a smell, it comes about, therefore,
  836  that you do not assign sounds and smells to all:
  837  so, since we cannot perceive all things with our eyes,
  838  you may know that some things are as bereft of color
  839  as some are without any smell and removed from sound,
  840  and that the keen mind can come to know these no less
  841  than it can mark the things deprived of other qualities.

  842  But in case you should think that, stripped of color alone,
  843  the first bodies remain so, they are also set apart from warmth
  844  and from cold entirely, and from burning heat;
  845  they go barren of sound and starved of flavor,
  846  and give off from their body no odor of their own.
  847  Just as, for the soothing liquor of marjoram and of myrrh
  848  and the flower of nard, which breathes nectar to the nostrils,
  849  when you set about making them, it is right first to seek,
  850  as far as you may and can find it, an odorless olive oil,
  851  whose nature sends out no breath at all to the nostrils,
  852  so that it may least of all spoil the odors mixed in its body
  853  and blended there, by touching them with its own rankness,
  854  for the same reason the first-beginnings of things must
  855  not lend their own odor to the things they beget,
  856  nor sound, since they can send out nothing from themselves,
  857  nor, on a like principle, finally, any flavor at all,
  858  nor cold, nor likewise hot and tepid heat,
  859  and all the rest: things which are still such as to be mortal,
  860  pliant with soft, brittle with crumbling, hollow with porous body,
  861  all these must be kept apart from the beginnings,
  862  if we wish to set beneath things foundations immortal,
  863  on which the whole of their safety may rest;
  864  so that all things do not return utterly to nothing for you.
  865  Now all the things we see to have sense, whatever they are,
  866  you must confess are, for all that, made up of senseless
  867  beginnings. Nor do plain facts refute this
  868  or fight against it, the things known and ready to hand;
  869  rather they themselves lead us by the hand and compel belief
  870  that, as I say, living creatures are born from senseless things.
  871  Indeed, you can see living worms come forth
  872  out of foul dung, when a rottenness has come over
  873  the sodden earth from untimely rains.
  874  Further, all things in like manner turn themselves into others.
  875  Rivers turn themselves into leaves, and glad pastures
  876  into cattle; cattle turn into our bodies
  877  their nature, and from our body often the strength of wild beasts
  878  grows, and the bodies of the strong-winged birds.
  879  Therefore nature turns all foods into living bodies
  880  and from them brings forth all the senses of living things,
  881  on a principle not far other than that by which
  882  it unfolds dry logs into flames and turns them all to fire.
  883  Do you see now, therefore, that for the first-beginnings of things
  884  it matters greatly in what order each is set
  885  and mixed with what, and what motions they give and take?
  886  Then further, what is it that strikes the mind itself,
  887  that moves it and compels it to give out various senses,
  888  so that you do not believe the sentient is born from the senseless?
  889  Surely it is that stones and wood and earth, mixed
  890  together, still cannot give back the sense of life.
  891  In these matters, then, it will be fitting to remember this:
  892  not from any and all things whatever that create
  893  sentient things do I say that senses are at once begotten,
  894  but that it matters greatly, first, how small are the things
  895  that make the sentient, and with what shape they are endowed,
  896  and finally what they are in motions, orders, positions.
  897  Of these things we see none in logs and clods;
  898  and yet these, when they are as it were rotted by the rains,
  899  give birth to little worms, because the bodies of matter,
  900  stirred from their old orders by a new event,
  901  are united as they must be for living things to be born.
  902  Next, those who would have it that from sentient things the sentient
  903  can be created, and these in turn from others used to sensing,
  904  when they make them soft; for all sense is bound up
  905  with flesh, sinews, veins, all of which we see
  906  are soft, fashioned of mortal body.
  907  But still, grant now that these can remain eternal:
  908  surely, even so, they must either have the sense of a part
  909  or be thought to be like whole living creatures.
  910  But it must be that parts cannot have sense by themselves;
  911  for the mind rejects all the senses of the limbs,
  912  nor can a hand cut off from us, nor any
  913  part of the body at all, hold sense alone.
  914  It remains that they be likened to whole living creatures,
  915  so that they can feel in concert, everywhere, with the sense of life.
  916  How then can they be called first-beginnings of things
  917  and shun the roads of death, since they are living creatures,
  918  and living creatures are one and the same as mortal beings?
  919  Yet even granting that they could, still by their meeting and union
  920  they will make nothing but a crowd and throng of living things,
  921  just as, surely, men, herds, and wild beasts cannot
  922  beget any other thing among themselves by coming together.
  923  In the same way, what we sense must itself sense.
  924  But if perhaps they put off their own sense from their body
  925  and take on another, what need was there to grant what
  926  is taken away? Then further, as we set out before,
  927  inasmuch as into living chicks the eggs
  928  of birds, we see, are turned, and worms swarm out of the earth
  929  when rottenness has seized it after untimely rains,
  930  we may know that senses can be born from what is not sense.
  931  But if perhaps someone says that sense can only arise
  932  from what is not sense by a process of change,
  933  or by a kind of birth, by which it is brought forth outward,
  934  for him it will be enough to make clear and prove this:
  935  that no birth happens unless a union has first been formed,
  936  nor does anything change without a uniting.
  937  To begin with, there can be no senses of any body
  938  before the nature of the living creature itself is born,
  939  surely because the matter is held scattered
  940  in air, rivers, earth, and the things born of earth,
  941  and has not, meeting, brought together in fitting manner
  942  the vital motions among themselves, by which the all-watching
  943  senses, kindled, watch over each living creature.
  944  Further, any living creature a blow too great
  945  for its nature to bear strikes down suddenly, and goes on
  946  to throw into confusion all the senses of body and mind.
  947  For the positions of the beginnings are dissolved,
  948  and the vital motions are hindered deep within,
  949  until the matter, shaken through all the limbs,
  950  loosens the knots of the vital spirit from the body
  951  and casts it out, scattered abroad, through all the passages;
  952  for what else should we think a blow can do,
  953  when it is dealt, but shatter and dissolve each thing?
  954  It happens too that, when a blow is dealt less sharply,
  955  the vital motions that remain will often win,
  956  win and calm the vast tumults of the stroke,
  957  and call each part back again into its own paths,
  958  and the motion of death, already, as it were, master in the body,
  959  shake apart, and kindle the senses almost lost;
  960  for by what means could they rather, from death's very threshold,
  961  gather their mind and come back to life
  962  than go on and depart where their course is almost run?
  963  Further, since pain exists when bodies of matter,
  964  by some force, through the living flesh, through the limbs,
  965  are troubled and tremble within, in their own seats,
  966  and when they move back into place a soothing pleasure comes,
  967  we may know that the first-beginnings can be assailed
  968  by no pain, and take no pleasure from themselves;
  969  since they are not made out of any bodies of beginnings,
  970  bodies, through the novelty of whose motions they might suffer
  971  or reap some fruit of nourishing sweetness.
  972  They ought not, therefore, to be endowed with any sense.

  973  Finally, so that each kind of living creature can feel,
  974  if sense must now be granted to their beginnings,
  975  what of those from which the human race is properly made?
  976  No doubt they too must shake with trembling laughter and guffaw,
  977  and sprinkle their faces and cheeks with dewy tears,
  978  and be skilled at saying much about the mixture of things,
  979  and go on to ask what their own first-beginnings are;
  980  since, made in the likeness of whole mortals,
  981  they too in their turn must consist of other elements,
  982  and those again of others, so that you never dare to stop;
  983  for I shall press you: whatever you say can speak and laugh
  984  and be wise must be made of others that do these same things.
  985  But if we see that this is raving and madness,
  986  and a man can laugh though not made of laughing things,
  987  and be wise and give an account in learned words
  988  though not made of seeds that are wise and eloquent,
  989  why should the things we see have sense be any less able
  990  to be of seeds mixed together that lack sense in every part?
  991  Finally, we are all sprung from heavenly seed;
  992  all have that same father, from whom the nourishing mother,
  993  earth, when she has received the liquid drops of moisture,
  994  teems and bears the shining grain and the glad trees
  995  and the human race, bears all the generations of wild beasts,
  996  since she provides the pastures on which all feed their bodies
  997  and lead a sweet life and propagate their offspring;
  998  therefore she has rightly gained the name of mother.
  999  Likewise what came from the earth before goes back
 1000  into the earth, and what was sent from the shores of the ether
 1001  is carried back again and received by the precincts of heaven.
 1002  Nor does death so destroy things as to annihilate
 1003  the bodies of matter, but it scatters their union;
 1004  then it joins one to another and brings it about that all
 1005  things change their shapes and alter their colors
 1006  and take on sense and in an instant give it up;
 1007  so that you may know it matters, for the first-beginnings of things,
 1008  with what and in what position they are held together,
 1009  and what motions they give and receive among themselves,
 1010  and may not think that there can reside in eternal
 1011  first bodies what we see floating on the surfaces
 1012  of things, and at times being born and suddenly perishing.
 1013  Indeed, even in my verses themselves it matters
 1014  with what and in what order each is placed;
 1015  for the same ones signify heaven, sea, lands, rivers, sun,
 1016  the same signify grain, trees, living creatures;
 1017  if not all, yet by far the greatest part
 1018  are alike; but by their position the things differ.
 1019  So too in things themselves, when of matter
 1020  the intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows,
 1021  collisions, motions, order, position, shapes
 1022  are changed, the things too must be changed.
 1023  Now apply your mind, for me, to true reasoning.
 1024  For a thing strikingly new is struggling to reach
 1025  your ears, and a new face of things to show itself.
 1026  But there is no thing so easy that it does not at first
 1027  prove harder to believe, and likewise
 1028  nothing so great, nothing so wonderful,
 1029  that all do not little by little lessen their wonder at it.
 1030  Consider first the clear and pure color of the sky,
 1031  and all it holds within it, the stars wandering everywhere,
 1032  and the moon, and the sun's brightness with its glorious light;
 1033  all of which, if now for the first time to mortals,
 1034  if they were suddenly thrown before them unforeseen,
 1035  what could be called more wonderful than these things,
 1036  or what would the nations less have dared beforehand to believe could be?
 1037  Nothing, I think: so wondrous would this appearance have been.
 1038  Yet now, wearied with the surfeit of seeing, no one
 1039  deigns to look up at the shining precincts of heaven.
 1040  Cease, therefore, frightened by mere newness,
 1041  to spit reasoning out of your mind, but rather with keen
 1042  judgment weigh it, and if it seems true to you,
 1043  give in; or, if it is false, gird yourself against it.
 1044  For the mind seeks reasoning, since the sum of place
 1045  is infinite outside, beyond these walls of the world,
 1046  of what is there further, where thought longs to look ever onward
 1047  and where the free cast of the mind itself flies through.
 1048  First of all, for us in every direction all around,
 1049  on either side, above and below, through the whole,
 1050  there is no limit; as I have taught, and the thing itself
 1051  cries aloud, and the nature of the deep shines clear.
 1052  In no way, then, must it be thought likely,
 1053  since space lies empty, infinite, in every direction,
 1054  and seeds in numberless number in the deep sum
 1055  fly about in many ways, driven by eternal motion,
 1056  that this one orb of lands and this sky alone were created,
 1057  and those so many bodies of matter outside do nothing;
 1058  especially since this world was made by nature, and the seeds
 1059  of things themselves, of their own accord, colliding by chance,
 1060  driven together in many ways, at random, vainly, to no purpose,
 1061  at last grew together into those which, suddenly thrown together,
 1062  might become forever the origins of great things,
 1063  of earth and sea and sky and the race of living things.
 1064  Therefore again and again you must confess
 1065  that there are elsewhere other gatherings of matter
 1066  such as this is, which the ether holds in its greedy embrace.
 1067  Further, when much matter is made ready,
 1068  when place is at hand and no thing and no cause
 1069  delays, things must surely be carried on and come to be.
 1070  As it is, if there is such a store of seeds as
 1071  the whole life of living things could not count out,
 1072  and the same nature remains, which can throw the seeds of things
 1073  together into every place in the same way
 1074  as they have been thrown together here, you must confess
 1075  that in other regions there are other orbs of lands
 1076  and various races of men and generations of wild beasts.
 1077  Add to this that in the sum there is no one thing
 1078  that is born unique and grows unique and alone,
 1079  but is of some generation, and very many are of the same
 1080  kind. First, among living creatures, with your mind as guide,
 1081  you will find it so with the mountain-roaming race of wild beasts,
 1082  so with the twin offspring of men, so finally with the mute
 1083  herds of the scaly ones and all the bodies of flying things.
 1084  Therefore you must confess, on the same principle, that sky
 1085  and earth and sun, moon, sea, and all else that is,
 1086  are not unique, but rather of number beyond numbering;
 1087  since the boundary-stone of life, set deep,
 1088  awaits these as surely, and they are of a body as born
 1089  as every kind that abounds here, kind by kind, in such things.
 1090  If you know these things well and hold them, nature is seen
 1091  to be free at once, stripped of proud masters,
 1092  doing all things of herself, by her own accord, without the gods.
 1093  For by the holy hearts of the gods, which in tranquil peace
 1094  pass a placid age and a serene life,
 1095  who can rule the sum of the immeasurable, who hold in hand
 1096  the strong reins of the deep and guide them,
 1097  who can turn all the heavens alike, and warm
 1098  all the fruitful lands with fires of the ether,
 1099  or be present in all places at all times,
 1100  to make darkness with clouds and shake the calm of heaven
 1101  with sound, then hurl thunderbolts, and often
 1102  throw down his own shrines, and, withdrawing into deserts,
 1103  rage there, exercising his bolt, which often passes the guilty
 1104  by, and strikes dead the unworthy and the undeserving?

 1105  And many bodies, after the birth-time of the world
 1106  and the first-born day of sea and earth and the rising of the sun,
 1107  were added from outside, and added round about
 1108  were seeds, which the great whole brought together by hurling them,
 1109  from which the sea and the lands could increase, and from which
 1110  the space of heaven's house could appear, and lift its lofty roof
 1111  far from the lands, and the air rise up.
 1112  For each to its own, from every place, all bodies by blows
 1113  are distributed, and go back to their own kinds:
 1114  moisture to moisture; earth grows from earthy body,
 1115  and fires forge fire, and ether forges ether,
 1116  until to the farthest limit of growth, bringing all to completion,
 1117  the creating nature of things has led all things on;
 1118  as happens when nothing more is now given within
 1119  the vital veins than flows away and withdraws.
 1120  Here the age of all things must come to a stand,
 1121  here nature reins in growth with her own strength.
 1122  For whatever things you see grow big with cheerful increase
 1123  and little by little climb the steps of grown age
 1124  take more bodies into themselves than they send out from themselves,
 1125  while food is easily put into all their veins, and while
 1126  they are not spread so widely that they give back much
 1127  and make more waste than their age feeds upon.
 1128  For certainly we must grant that many bodies flow
 1129  and withdraw from things; but more must come in,
 1130  until they have touched the topmost peak of growing.
 1131  From then, little by little, age breaks the strength
 1132  and grown vigor, and melts away into the worse part.
 1133  For indeed, the larger a thing is, once its increase is taken away,
 1134  and the wider it is, into all directions on every side
 1135  the more bodies it scatters now and sends out from itself,
 1136  nor is food easily distributed into all its veins,
 1137  nor is there enough, for the lavish tides it pours out,
 1138  from which it could spring up anew and supply as much.
 1139  Rightly, therefore, they perish, when they have grown rare
 1140  by flowing, and all things give way beneath blows from outside,
 1141  since in great age food at last runs short,
 1142  and the bodies battering from outside never cease
 1143  to wear a thing down and subdue it with hostile blows.
 1144  So therefore the walls of the great world round about
 1145  will also be stormed, and give way to decay and crumbling ruin;
 1146  for food must make all things whole by renewing them,
 1147  food must prop them up, food must sustain all things,
 1148  in vain, since neither do the veins endure to take in
 1149  what is enough, nor does nature supply as much as is needed.
 1150  And indeed even now the age is broken, and the worn-out earth
 1151  scarcely creates small animals, she who created all
 1152  kinds, and gave birth to the huge bodies of wild beasts.
 1153  For it was not, I think, that the mortal kinds from on high
 1154  a golden rope let down from heaven into the fields,
 1155  nor did the sea and the waves beating on the rocks create them,
 1156  but the earth bore them, the same that now feeds them from herself.
 1157  Further, the shining crops and the glad vineyards
 1158  she herself, of her own accord, first created for mortals,
 1159  she herself gave sweet fruits and glad pastures;
 1160  which now scarcely grow great, increased by our labor,
 1161  and we wear out the oxen and the strength of the farmers,
 1162  we wear down the iron share, scarcely supplied by the fields:
 1163  so much do they stint their fruits and increase the labor.
 1164  And now the aged plowman shakes his head and sighs
 1165  more often that his great labors have come to nothing,
 1166  and when he compares the present times with times
 1167  gone by, he often praises his father's fortunes.
 1168  Gloomily too the planter of the old and shriveled vine
 1169  blames the drift of time and wearies the age with complaint,
 1170  and grumbles how the race of old, filled with piety,
 1171  bore life very easily within narrow bounds,
 1172  though the measure of land for each man was far smaller before;
 1173  nor does he grasp that all things waste away by degrees and go
 1174  to the bier, worn out by the ancient span of age.
