[DRAFT] (2.1105) Lucretius's argument is that the world's growth came from bodies added "from outside" (extrinsecus) and gathered by the whole's own hurling (iaculando). The same intake from outside later reverses into loss, and that sets up the decay of the world at 1144–1145. "The great whole" renders omne, i.e. the sum of things.

[DRAFT] (2.1112) The principle is that like bodies are sorted to like by blows (plagis): moisture to moisture, earth to earth, fire to fire, and ether to ether. These are the four masses of the world (sea, land, fire, air or ether). "Kinds" renders saecla, and in this passage it means the categories of stuff each body belongs to.

[DRAFT] (2.1115) The play on procudunt ("forge") and the repeated nouns (ignem ignes, aetheraque aether) are in the Latin. The English keeps "forge" for both fire and ether, so the repetition shows.

[DRAFT] (2.1117) "The creating nature of things" renders rerum natura creatrix, a variant of the poem's title phrase. It appears here with a feminine agent noun, which makes nature a maker.

[DRAFT] (2.1121) Nature "reins in" growth (refrenat) at a fixed point. This is the doctrine of 2.1105–1174 that every composite thing has a limit set by its own balance of intake and loss. Lucretius states it here before applying it to living things and then to the world.

[DRAFT] (2.1144) The argument turns from living things to the world as a whole. The "walls of the great world" (moenia mundi) echo the "flaming walls of the world" of 1.73, here "stormed" (expugnata) like a besieged city. The conclusion that this world will decay is the reading of the passage as the poet states it, and it belongs with 2.1150's claim that the earth is already exhausted.

[DRAFT] (2.1148) The clause ne quiquam ("in vain") hangs awkwardly after the "must" of 1146–1147. It means that this supply of food is bound to fail, since the veins cannot take in enough and nature does not provide enough. The English keeps the loose attachment, as the Latin has it.

[DRAFT] (2.1150) The "worn-out earth" (effeta tellus) is a mother past childbearing, in the image Lucretius uses for the earth. The claim is that the earth once produced all the kinds of animals and now produces only small ones. Its fuller form, the earth as mother of living things, comes in Book 5.

[DRAFT] (2.1154) Homer's golden chain (Iliad 8.19) is the rope Zeus claims he could let down from heaven to haul up gods and men. Lucretius uses it, with *funis* ("rope") where Homer has seira, to deny that heaven sent mortals down. Some readers add the Stoic image of a linked chain of causes, but the sentence itself does not say so.

[DRAFT] (2.1169) The reading followed is momen ("drift", "movement", from movimen), which gives "blames the drift of time". Some editors read nomen, giving "the name of the times", which is easier to explain but a less natural object for incusat. The translation takes the harder reading, and the note on it should keep that choice open.

[DRAFT] (2.1174) The word capulum ("bier", "coffin", cf. "go to the tomb") belongs to the language of burial, and so the plowman's father and the old vinedresser's own world are figured as dead and going to their funeral. The line is the poem's last in this stretch and brings the world's old age back to the single man who grumbles about it. This is the end of the book's argument: the old man who praises the past does not see that all things, himself and the earth included, are wearing out together.
