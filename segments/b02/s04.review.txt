  581  This too it is fitting, in these matters, to keep sealed up
  582  and to hold, entrusted to a mind that remembers:
  583  that there is nothing whose nature is seen plainly before us
  584  that consists of one kind of beginnings alone,
  585  nothing that is not made of mingled seed.
  586  And whatever possesses more forces in itself
  587  and more powers, the more it shows that it holds the most kinds
  588  of beginnings in itself, and the most varied shapes.
  589  First of all, the earth holds in itself first bodies
  590  from which springs, rolling out their coolness, constantly renew
  591  the immeasurable sea; it holds those from which fires rise;
  592  for in many places the ground of the earth is kindled and burns,
  593  and from its depths the onrush of Etna rages with fire.
  594  Then further, shining crops and glad orchards
  595  it has the means to raise up for the races of men,
  596  and the means to offer rivers, leaves, and glad pastures
  597  to the mountain-roaming race of wild beasts.
  598  Therefore the great mother of the gods, and mother of wild beasts,
  599  and begetter of our body, she alone has been called. {{NOTE: the Great Mother (Magna Mater), Cybele; the long description of her cult that follows is set out in order to be rejected (2.644-645)}}
  600  Of her the learned poets of the Greeks of old have sung
  601  that, seated in a chariot, she drives a yoke of lions,
  602  teaching by this that the great earth hangs in the space
  603  of air, and that earth cannot rest upon earth.
  604  They yoked wild beasts to her because offspring, however wild,
  605  must be softened, won over by the kindnesses of parents.
  606  And they bound the top of her head with a crown of walls,
  607  because, fortified in choice places, she bears up cities.
  608  Adorned now with this emblem, through the great lands
  609  the image of the divine mother is carried, inspiring dread.
  610  Her the various peoples, by the ancient custom of their rites,
  611  call the Idaean Mother, and give her bands of Phrygians {{NOTE: from Mount Ida in Phrygia, the center of her cult}}
  612  as her companions, because from those regions first, they say,
  613  crops began to be created through the circles of the earth.
  614  They assign her Galli, because, of those who have outraged {{NOTE: the Galli, her eunuch priests}}
  615  the Mother's power and been found ungrateful to their parents,
  616  they wish to signify that they must be thought unworthy
  617  to bring forth living offspring into the shores of light.
  618  Taut drums thunder under their palms, and the hollow cymbals
  619  all around, and horns threaten with harsh-sounding song,
  620  and the hollow pipe goads their minds with Phrygian measure,
  621  and they carry weapons before them, signs of violent frenzy,
  622  so that the ungrateful minds and impious hearts of the crowd
  623  may be terrified with fear by the goddess's power.
  624  And so, as soon as she is carried in through great cities
  625  and silently enriches mortals with a wordless blessing,
  626  they strew the whole road of her way with bronze and silver,
  627  enriching it with bountiful alms, and snow down rose
  628  blossoms, shading the mother and the band of her companions.
  629  Here an armed troop, whom the Greeks speak of by the name
  630  of Curetes, when they happen to sport among the Phrygian bands {{NOTE: the Curetes, armed dancers of Cretan myth}}
  631  and leap in measure, glad of blood,
  632  shaking the terrifying crests on their heads with their nodding,
  633  recall the Dictaean Curetes, who are said once to have hidden {{NOTE: Dictaean: of Mount Dicte in Crete, where the infant Jupiter was hidden from his father Saturn}}
  634  that famous wailing of Jupiter in Crete,
  635  when, boys around the boy, in their nimble dance,
  636  armed and in measure in their nimble dance {{NOTE: a garbled line in the source, mixing 635 and 637, probably to be deleted; rendered as printed}}
  637  armed, in measure, they beat bronze upon bronze,
  638  so that Saturn might not catch him and give him to his jaws
  639  and deal the Mother an eternal wound beneath her breast.
  640  For this reason they attend the great mother armed,
  641  or else because they signify that the goddess proclaims that with arms
  642  and courage men should be willing to defend their native land
  643  and make ready to be a protection and an honor to their parents.
  644  These things, however well and excellently they are set out,
  645  are nonetheless driven far from true reasoning.
  646  For the whole nature of the gods must, of itself, by necessity {{NOTE: 2.646-651 repeat 1.44-49 verbatim}}
  647  enjoy immortal life in utmost peace,
  648  removed from our affairs and separated far away;
  649  for free from every pain, free from dangers,
  650  strong in its own resources, needing nothing of ours,
  651  it is neither won by services nor touched by anger.
  652  The earth, indeed, is at all times without sense,
  653  and because it possesses the first-beginnings of many things,
  654  it brings forth many in many ways into the light of the sun.
  655  Here, if anyone resolves to call the sea Neptune, and to call
  656  the crops Ceres, and would rather misuse the name of Bacchus
  657  than speak the proper word for that liquor,
  658  let us grant that he may keep declaring the round of the earth
  659  to be the mother of the gods, if only in truth he himself
  680  refrains from tainting his mind with foul religion. {{NOTE: line 680 printed after 659 by editorial transposition, following the source}}
  660  Thus often, cropping grass from a single field,
  661  wool-bearing flocks and the warlike offspring of horses
  662  and horned herds, beneath the same covering of sky,
  663  quenching their thirst from a single stream of water,
  664  live unlike in appearance, and keep their parents'
  665  nature, and each, kind by kind, copies their ways.
  666  So great in any kind of grass is the unlike order
  667  of matter, so great in any river too.
  668  Again, any one living creature, whichever of them all,
  669  bones, blood, veins, heat, moisture, flesh, and sinews
  670  make up, which are again far distant from one another,
  671  formed of unlike shapes of beginnings.
  672  Then further, all things that are set aflame by fire and burn,
  673  if nothing else, still carry these in their body:
  674  the means by which they can throw out fire and send up light,
  675  and drive off sparks and scatter ash far and wide.
  676  Going through the rest by a like reasoning of mind,
  677  you will therefore find that they hide the seeds of many things
  678  in their body, and contain varied shapes.
  679  Finally, you see many things in which color and taste together
  681  are given along with smell; first of all, most fruits.
  682  These, therefore, must consist of varied shapes;
  683  for smell gets into our limbs where color does not go,
  684  color, likewise, apart, and taste apart, works its way
  685  into our senses; so you may know they differ in their first shapes.
  686  Therefore unlike forms come together into one mass,
  687  and things are made up out of mingled seed.
  688  Indeed, everywhere in these very verses of ours
  689  you see many elements common to many words,
  690  though you must still admit that verses and words
  691  are made, one and another, from different elements;
  692  not because few letters run in common among them,
  693  or no two are made out of letters entirely the same,
  694  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  695  So likewise in other things, since many are common
  696  to many things, the first-beginnings of things
  697  can nonetheless make up, among themselves, unlike wholes;
  698  so that it may rightly be said that the human race
  699  and crops and glad orchards are made of different things.
  700  Yet we must not think that all things can be joined
  701  in every way; for you would see monsters everywhere come to be,
  702  forms half beast, half man spring up, and tall
  703  branches at times put forth from a living body,
  704  and many limbs of land creatures joined to those of the sea,
  705  and then Chimaeras, breathing flame from their foul mouths,
  706  and nature feeding them across the all-mothering lands.
  707  It is plain that none of this happens, since all things
  708  created from fixed seeds and by a fixed mother,
  709  can, we see, preserve their kind as they grow.
  710  Clearly this must come about by a fixed principle.
  711  For to each, out of all its foods, its own bodies pass
  712  within, into the limbs, and, once joined, produce
  713  the fitting motions; but against this we see nature
  714  cast out onto the earth what is alien, and many things, in unseen
  715  bodies, flee out of the body, driven by blows,
  716  which could neither be joined anywhere nor, within,
  717  feel with the vital motions and imitate them.
  718  But lest you think that only living things are held
  719  by these laws, a certain principle bounds all things.
  720  For just as things begotten are unlike one another
  721  in their whole nature, each of them, so each must
  722  be made of unlike shapes of beginnings;
  723  not that few are endowed with a like form,
  724  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  725  Further, since the seeds differ, there must be differences in
  726  intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows,
  727  collisions, motions; which not only divide living bodies
  728  from one another, but keep the lands and the whole sea
  729  apart, and hold back the whole heaven from the lands.
