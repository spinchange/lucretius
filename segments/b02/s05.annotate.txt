[DRAFT] (2.730) The argument that the first-beginnings have no color begins here and runs to 841. "Matter" (materiai) in 735 and 737 is the same thing as the first-beginnings; the English keeps both words for the Latin's two.

[DRAFT] (2.740) "Casting of the mind" (animi iniectus) renders the Epicurean epibole tes dianoias, the mind's direct application to an object that the senses cannot reach. The English keeps the phrase fixed so that it can recur wherever Lucretius uses it again.

[DRAFT] (2.745) The blind from birth (caecigeni) are Lucretius's test case: touch gives knowledge of bodies without any color, so knowledge of the colorless is possible. The step from touch to the mind's grasp of the atomic is the argument's real claim.

[DRAFT] (2.748) "Quod quoniam vinco fieri, nunc esse docebo" says that having proved colorless knowledge possible (vinco fieri), he will now show it actual (esse). The English keeps the two verbs distinct.

[DRAFT] (2.753–754) These lines repeat 1.670–671 and 1.792–793, where the same argument is made against Heraclitus and the monists: if a thing changes it leaves its own bounds, and that is the death of what it was. The English must match Book 1 word for word.

[DRAFT] (2.755) "Seeds of things" (semina rerum) is a register term for the same entities called "first-beginnings" (principiis) a few lines before; the two are used interchangeably in this passage, so the shift of word does not mark a shift of subject.

[DRAFT] (2.766–767) The simile of the dark sea turning to white foam under wind (canos... fluctus, candenti marmore) is used as an example of color change without any change in the underlying seeds; "marble" carries "shining" more than "hard".

[DRAFT] (2.778–779) The analogy of the square built from unlike forms is a mosaic image: the whole shows one shape (quadratum) though its parts differ. Lucretius uses it to argue that if colored seeds composed a uniformly colored sea, the varied colors would show through, as varied shapes would show within a square.

[DRAFT] (2.801–805) The dove's neck and the peacock's tail (806–807) are Lucretius's examples of structural or iridescent color, which changes with the angle of light and so cannot belong to the thing itself. A modern reader may hear an anticipation of the wave theory of light; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.803) Pyropus (pyropo) is a red-gold bronze alloy, not a gemstone, here. The English "fire-bronze" follows the Greek roots, "fire-eyed".

[DRAFT] (2.805) "Zmaragdos" (smaragdos) is the Greek-derived Latin for emerald, or green gemstones generally, so "emeralds" is approximate.

[DRAFT] (2.810) "Blow" (plagae) is the Epicurean term for impacts of atoms; the pupil receives different "blows" from different atomic configurations. The English "blow" is kept here and "stroke" (ictu) in 808 for the different Latin word.

[DRAFT] (2.829) The Latin text prints "austrum" (not ostrum, "purple dye"), but the sense requires the dye or purple cloth, since filatim in 831 means "thread by thread". The translation follows the sense of ostrum; "gold" (aurum) is a less likely conjecture.

[DRAFT] (2.830) "Poeniceus" (poeniceus color) means Phoenician-red, a scarlet or crimson that is a dye color. The English "scarlet" renders it, but it is the same family as "purple" (purpura), so the pair is almost a doublet.

[DRAFT] (2.833) "Particulas" (particles, "particulas") is here a non-technical word for the small parts of a composite, distinct from the semina rerum. The English "particles" is acceptable for this ordinary sense but should not be used for primordia (register: never "particles" for corpora).
