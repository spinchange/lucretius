  142  Now what swiftness of motion has been given to the bodies
  143  of matter, Memmius, you may learn from this in a few words.
  144  First, when dawn sprinkles the lands with new light,
  145  and the many-colored birds, flitting through the pathless woods,
  146  fill the places through the soft air with liquid voices,
  147  how suddenly the sun, once risen, is used at such a time
  148  to clothe all things, flooding them with his light,
  149  we see to be ready to hand and plain for all.
  150  But that heat which the sun sends out, and its calm light,
  151  does not travel through empty void; and so it is forced {{NOTE: inane vacuum, "empty void": Lucretius doubles the term for emphasis; "void" stays fixed}}
  152  to go more slowly, as it beats its way, as it were, through waves of air;
  153  nor do the little bodies of heat travel one by one,
  154  but tangled with one another and massed together;
  155  and so at once they are dragged back by one another and hindered
  156  from outside, so that they are forced to go more slowly.
  157  But the first-beginnings that are of solid singleness,
  158  when they travel through empty void and nothing delays them
  159  from outside, and they themselves, one whole from their own parts,
  160  are borne, straining, toward the one place they began to move to,
  161  must surely surpass in mobility
  162  and be carried far more swiftly than the light of the sun,
  163  and run through many times the extent of space in the same
  164  time in which the sun's flashes spread through heaven.
 164a  * * * {{NOTE: the source marks lost text here; the sense picks up mid-sentence, apparently about people who do not follow out the motions of the atoms}}
  165  nor to follow out each single first-beginning,
  166  to see by what principle each thing comes about.
  167  But some, against this, ignorant of matter,
  168  hold that nature cannot, without the power of the gods,
  169  so closely fitted, in such measure, to human needs,
  170  change the seasons of the years and create the crops
  171  and all the other things that mortals are urged to approach,
  172  and to which divine pleasure herself, the guide of life, leads them,
  173  coaxing them through the works of Venus to propagate their generations,
  174  so that the human race should not perish. When they imagine
  175  the gods have ordered all things for men's sake, in every way
  176  they seem to have fallen far from true reasoning.
  177  For even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  178  this, still, from the very order of heaven I would dare
  179  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  180  that by no means for us, by divine power, was created
  181  the nature of the world: so great the flaws it stands endowed with. {{NOTE: the argument is given at 5.195-234}}
  182  These things, Memmius, I will make plain to you later;
  183  now I will set out what remains concerning motions.
  184  Now is the place, I think, in these matters to prove this too
  185  to you: that no bodily thing can by its own force
  186  be carried upward and travel upward.
  187  Do not let the bodies of flames deceive you in this;
  188  for they are born upward and take on their increase,
  189  and upward the shining crops and the trees grow,
  190  though all weights, as far as in them lies, are carried downward.
  191  Nor, when fires leap up to the roofs of houses
  192  and with swift flame lick the rafters and beams,
  193  must we think they do it of their own accord, with no force thrusting from beneath.
  194  Just so the blood let out from our body
  195  spurts leaping on high and scatters gore.
  196  Do you not see, too, with what force the water's moisture
  197  spits out rafters and beams? For the more we have pushed them deep,
  198  straight down, and many of us with great force have pressed them, straining,
  199  the more eagerly it thrusts them back up and sends them out,
  200  so that they rise and leap out by more than half their length.
  201  And yet we do not doubt, I think, that these, as far as in them lies,
  202  are all carried downward through the empty void.
  203  So in the same way flames too must be able, through the breezes
  204  of air, squeezed out, to climb upward, although
  205  their weights, as far as in them lies, fight to draw them down.
  206  And the night torches of heaven, flying aloft, {{NOTE: meteors}}
  207  do you not see them draw long trails of flame
  208  toward whatever quarter nature has given them passage?
  209  Do you not see stars and constellations falling to the earth?
  210  The sun also from the summit of heaven scatters its heat
  211  to every side and sows the fields with light;
  212  to the earth, therefore, the sun's heat also tends.
  213  And you see thunderbolts fly slantwise through the rains;
  214  now from here, now from there, fires broken from the clouds
  215  rush together; the flaming force falls everywhere upon the earth.
  216  This too, in these matters, we long for you to know:
  217  that when bodies are carried straight down through the void
  218  by their own weights, at quite uncertain times
  219  and uncertain places they push a little from their course,
  220  just so much that you could call it a change of motion.
  221  For if they were not used to swerve, all things would fall {{NOTE: declinare, "swerve": first statement of the swerve; the noun clinamen comes at 2.292}}
  222  downward like drops of rain through the deep void,
  223  and no collision would arise, no blow be created
  224  for the beginnings; so nature would never have created anything. {{NOTE: principia, "beginnings": Lucretius's synonym for primordia, kept distinct from "first-beginnings"}}
  225  But if perhaps someone believes that heavier bodies,
  226  because they are carried more swiftly straight through the void,
  227  can fall from above upon the lighter, and so beget
  228  blows that could give rise to generative motions,
  229  he strays far away, off the road, from true reasoning.
  230  For whatever falls through water and thin air,
  231  these must quicken their fall in proportion to their weights,
  232  because the body of water and the thin nature
  233  of air cannot delay each thing equally,
  234  but give way more quickly, overcome by heavier things;
  235  but on the other hand the empty void cannot, on any side or at any
  236  time, hold up anything at all,
  237  but must go on giving way, as its nature seeks;
  238  therefore all things must, through the calm void,
  239  be carried at equal speed, though driven by unequal weights.
  240  Therefore heavier things will never be able to fall
  241  from above upon lighter ones, nor of themselves beget the blows
  242  that vary the motions by which nature carries on things.
  243  Therefore, again and again, bodies must incline
  244  a little; but no more than the least, so that we do not seem to invent
  245  slanting motions, and the true facts refute it.
  246  For this we see to be ready to hand and plain,
  247  that weights, as far as in them lies, cannot travel slantwise
  248  when they plunge down from above, so far as you can see;
  249  but that nothing at all, from the straight line of its path,
  250  swerves of itself, who is there that could perceive?
  251  Finally, if every motion is always linked,
  252  and the new always arises from the old in a fixed order,
  253  and the first-beginnings do not by swerving make
  254  some beginning of motion to break the covenants of fate, {{NOTE: fati foedera, "the covenants of fate": set against nature's covenants (foedera naturae)}}
  255  so that cause should not follow cause from infinity,
  256  from where does it arise, free, for living things throughout the earth,
  257  from where, I ask, is this will wrested away from the fates,
  258  by which we go forward wherever pleasure leads each one,
  259  and likewise swerve our motions, at no fixed time
  260  and at no fixed place, but where the mind itself has carried us?
  261  For beyond doubt in these things each one's own will
  262  gives the beginning, and from it motions flow out through the limbs.
  263  Do you not see too that, when in an instant of time the starting-gates
  264  are thrown open, the eager strength of the horses still cannot
  265  burst forth as suddenly as their mind itself longs to?
  266  For all the matter throughout the whole body, its whole
  267  store, must be roused, so that, roused through all the limbs,
  268  it may strain together and follow the mind's desire;
  269  so you may see that the start of motion is created by the heart,
  270  and that it goes forth first from the will of the mind,
  271  and is then passed on through the whole body and limbs.
  272  Nor is it the same as when we go forward pushed by a blow,
  273  by another's great strength and great compulsion;
  274  for then all the matter of the whole body,
  275  it is clear, goes and is swept along against our will,
  276  until the will reins it back throughout the limbs.
  277  Do you see now, then, that although an outside force drives many
  278  and often compels them against their will to go forward
  279  and be swept headlong, still there is in our breast
  280  something that can fight against it and resist?
  281  At whose command the store of matter too
  282  is forced at times to turn through the limbs and joints,
  283  and, thrown forward, is reined back and settles back again.
  284  Therefore in the seeds too you must admit the same,
  285  that there is besides blows and weights another cause
  286  of motions, from which this power is born in us,
  287  since we see that nothing can come to be from nothing.
  288  For weight prevents all things from coming about by blows,
  289  as by a force from outside; but that the thing itself should not {{NOTE: res ipsa, "the thing itself", as in the source; many editors read mens ipsa, "the mind itself"}}
  290  hold an inward necessity in doing all things,
  291  and be compelled, as if conquered, to bear and suffer,
  292  this is brought about by the tiny swerve of the beginnings {{NOTE: clinamen, "swerve": the poem's only use of the noun}}
  293  at no fixed place and at no fixed time.
