  842  But in case you should think that, stripped of color alone,
  843  the first bodies remain so, they are also set apart from warmth
  844  and from cold entirely, and from burning heat;
  845  they go barren of sound and starved of flavor,
  846  and give off from their body no odor of their own.
  847  Just as, for the soothing liquor of marjoram and of myrrh {{NOTE: amaracinum and stacte, perfumed oils of marjoram and of myrrh}}
  848  and the flower of nard, which breathes nectar to the nostrils,
  849  when you set about making them, it is right first to seek,
  850  as far as you may and can find it, an odorless olive oil,
  851  whose nature sends out no breath at all to the nostrils,
  852  so that it may least of all spoil the odors mixed in its body
  853  and blended there, by touching them with its own rankness,
  854  for the same reason the first-beginnings of things must
  855  not lend their own odor to the things they beget,
  856  nor sound, since they can send out nothing from themselves,
  857  nor, on a like principle, finally, any flavor at all,
  858  nor cold, nor likewise hot and tepid heat,
  859  and all the rest: things which are still such as to be mortal,
  860  pliant with soft, brittle with crumbling, hollow with porous body,
  861  all these must be kept apart from the beginnings, {{NOTE: principia, Lucretius's other name for the primordia; rendered "beginnings"}}
  862  if we wish to set beneath things foundations immortal,
  863  on which the whole of their safety may rest;
  864  so that all things do not return utterly to nothing for you.
  865  Now all the things we see to have sense, whatever they are,
  866  you must confess are, for all that, made up of senseless
  867  beginnings. Nor do plain facts refute this
  868  or fight against it, the things known and ready to hand;
  869  rather they themselves lead us by the hand and compel belief
  870  that, as I say, living creatures are born from senseless things.
  871  Indeed, you can see living worms come forth
  872  out of foul dung, when a rottenness has come over
  873  the sodden earth from untimely rains.
  874  Further, all things in like manner turn themselves into others.
  875  Rivers turn themselves into leaves, and glad pastures
  876  into cattle; cattle turn into our bodies
  877  their nature, and from our body often the strength of wild beasts
  878  grows, and the bodies of the strong-winged birds.
  879  Therefore nature turns all foods into living bodies
  880  and from them brings forth all the senses of living things,
  881  on a principle not far other than that by which
  882  it unfolds dry logs into flames and turns them all to fire.
  883  Do you see now, therefore, that for the first-beginnings of things
  884  it matters greatly in what order each is set
  885  and mixed with what, and what motions they give and take?
  886  Then further, what is it that strikes the mind itself,
  887  that moves it and compels it to give out various senses,
  888  so that you do not believe the sentient is born from the senseless?
  889  Surely it is that stones and wood and earth, mixed
  890  together, still cannot give back the sense of life.
  891  In these matters, then, it will be fitting to remember this:
  892  not from any and all things whatever that create
  893  sentient things do I say that senses are at once begotten,
  894  but that it matters greatly, first, how small are the things
  895  that make the sentient, and with what shape they are endowed,
  896  and finally what they are in motions, orders, positions.
  897  Of these things we see none in logs and clods;
  898  and yet these, when they are as it were rotted by the rains,
  899  give birth to little worms, because the bodies of matter,
  900  stirred from their old orders by a new event,
  901  are united as they must be for living things to be born.
  902  Next, those who would have it that from sentient things the sentient
  903  can be created, and these in turn from others used to sensing,
 903a  * * * {{NOTE: one or more lines lost here; the sense of the gap is uncertain}}
  904  when they make them soft; for all sense is bound up
  905  with flesh, sinews, veins, all of which we see
  906  are soft, fashioned of mortal body.
  907  But still, grant now that these can remain eternal:
  908  surely, even so, they must either have the sense of a part
  909  or be thought to be like whole living creatures.
  910  But it must be that parts cannot have sense by themselves;
  911  for the mind rejects all the senses of the limbs, {{NOTE: text uncertain; the point is that a limb's sense belongs to the whole creature}}
  912  nor can a hand cut off from us, nor any
  913  part of the body at all, hold sense alone.
  914  It remains that they be likened to whole living creatures,
  915  so that they can feel in concert, everywhere, with the sense of life.
  916  How then can they be called first-beginnings of things
  917  and shun the roads of death, since they are living creatures,
  918  and living creatures are one and the same as mortal beings?
  919  Yet even granting that they could, still by their meeting and union
  920  they will make nothing but a crowd and throng of living things,
  921  just as, surely, men, herds, and wild beasts cannot
  922  beget any other thing among themselves by coming together.
  923  In the same way, what we sense must itself sense.
  924  But if perhaps they put off their own sense from their body
  925  and take on another, what need was there to grant what
  926  is taken away? Then further, as we set out before,
  927  inasmuch as into living chicks the eggs
  928  of birds, we see, are turned, and worms swarm out of the earth
  929  when rottenness has seized it after untimely rains,
  930  we may know that senses can be born from what is not sense.
  931  But if perhaps someone says that sense can only arise
  932  from what is not sense by a process of change,
  933  or by a kind of birth, by which it is brought forth outward,
  934  for him it will be enough to make clear and prove this:
  935  that no birth happens unless a union has first been formed,
  936  nor does anything change without a uniting.
  937  To begin with, there can be no senses of any body
  938  before the nature of the living creature itself is born,
  939  surely because the matter is held scattered
  940  in air, rivers, earth, and the things born of earth,
  941  and has not, meeting, brought together in fitting manner
  942  the vital motions among themselves, by which the all-watching
  943  senses, kindled, watch over each living creature.
  944  Further, any living creature a blow too great
  945  for its nature to bear strikes down suddenly, and goes on
  946  to throw into confusion all the senses of body and mind.
  947  For the positions of the beginnings are dissolved,
  948  and the vital motions are hindered deep within,
  949  until the matter, shaken through all the limbs,
  950  loosens the knots of the vital spirit from the body
  951  and casts it out, scattered abroad, through all the passages;
  952  for what else should we think a blow can do,
  953  when it is dealt, but shatter and dissolve each thing?
  954  It happens too that, when a blow is dealt less sharply,
  955  the vital motions that remain will often win,
  956  win and calm the vast tumults of the stroke,
  957  and call each part back again into its own paths,
  958  and the motion of death, already, as it were, master in the body,
  959  shake apart, and kindle the senses almost lost;
  960  for by what means could they rather, from death's very threshold,
  961  gather their mind and come back to life
  962  than go on and depart where their course is almost run?
  963  Further, since pain exists when bodies of matter,
  964  by some force, through the living flesh, through the limbs,
  965  are troubled and tremble within, in their own seats,
  966  and when they move back into place a soothing pleasure comes,
  967  we may know that the first-beginnings can be assailed
  968  by no pain, and take no pleasure from themselves;
  969  since they are not made out of any bodies of beginnings,
  970  bodies, through the novelty of whose motions they might suffer
  971  or reap some fruit of nourishing sweetness.
  972  They ought not, therefore, to be endowed with any sense.
