[DRAFT] (2.973-990) This is the last stretch of the argument that the first-beginnings cannot themselves have sense (the "laughing atoms" argument). Lucretius reasons that if sense is granted to the beginnings, they must laugh, weep, and reason, and so be made of further beginnings that do the same, without end (nusquam consistere ut ausis). Since that is absurd, sensing things must come from seeds that lack sense (seminibus carentibus undique sensu). The translation renders *principia* (974) as "beginnings", and *elementa* (981) as "elements" and *semina* (988, 990) as "seeds", following the register. Here the three words name the same things.

[DRAFT] (2.991) Sky (caelesti semine) is father and earth is mother, an old pairing that Lucretius takes over here. He then gives it a physical sense: rain is the father's seed that the earth receives, and the earth's feeding of her creatures earns her the name of mother (maternum nomen, 998). Greek and Roman myth and cult both knew the pairing, and the poet uses it in a purely natural sense.

[DRAFT] (2.1000-1001) The ether (aetheris oris) and "the precincts of heaven" (caeli templa) are given the same meaning. Lucretius is following the doctrine that the finer parts of the human body (the spirit and the seeds from the sky) return to the upper air at death, as the grosser parts return to earth. *Templa* is rendered "precincts" in the register, not "temples". The passage is often compared with an epitaph preserved from the Athenian dead at Potidaea in 432 BC, which says much the same.

[DRAFT] (2.1002-1006) Death does not annihilate things (nec... interemit) but scatters their union (coetum dissupat). The matter is rejoined (aliis aliud coniungit) so that things take on new shapes, colors, and sense (capiant sensus). This is the Epicurean doctrine that nothing comes from nothing and nothing returns to nothing, stated compressedly. A modern reader may hear conservation of matter in it; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.1013-1018) The alphabet analogy, with its pun on *elementa* ("elements" and "letters"), recurs through the poem (first at 1.196-198, and again at 2.688-699). The same letters (eadem) spell out sky, sea, lands, and so on, so a difference of position (positura) makes different words and different things. Here the argument is put to the particular use of showing that the first-beginnings can differ only in arrangement and still produce things that differ in kind.

[DRAFT] (2.1019-1022) The list of nine terms (intervalla vias conexus pondera plagas concursus motus ordo positura figurae) is Lucretius's catalogue of the ways atoms can differ in relation. It is an asyndetic run, and the translation keeps that. *Plagae* is "blows"; *figurae* "shapes".

[DRAFT] (2.1023) The line opens a new section on the plurality of worlds (nunc animum nobis adhibe veram ad rationem). *Ratio* is "reasoning" in the register, and "true reasoning" is the translation of *veram rationem*. The address to Memmius is by the second person only, as elsewhere.

[DRAFT] (2.1030-1039) The argument from habituation: the sky would astonish us if we saw it for the first time, but we have grown weary of looking (fessus satiate videndi). Lucretius uses it to prepare Memmius to accept the new doctrine of many worlds, and the same thought recurs at 5.1204-1217 in a different setting. *Templa* again is "precincts".

[DRAFT] (2.1044-1047) *Moenia mundi* ("walls of the world") echoes the proem's *flammantia moenia mundi* (1.73), the edge of the world that Epicurus crossed in thought. Here the reader is asked to do the same. In the translation, "free cast of the mind" renders *animi iactus liber*, the mind's unobstructed projection (see the translator's note at 1047). *Mens* and *animus* both occur in 1046-1047 and are both "mind" or "thought" per the register.

[DRAFT] (2.1048-1051) The argument for the infinity of the universe, which Lucretius says he has already taught (uti docui, 1050), refers back to 1.958-1051 in Book 1. *Profundum* is the "deep", the unbounded extent. The line "the thing itself cries aloud" (res ipsaque per se vociferatur) is the poet's appeal to evidence over argument.

[DRAFT] (2.1052-1066) The inference from infinite space and matter to many worlds is the center of the argument. Nature made this world by chance collisions (forte offensando, temere), with no plan, so the same must be happening elsewhere (alios alibi congressus). A modern reader may hear the plurality of worlds of later cosmology; that is the reader's association, not the poet's claim.

[DRAFT] (2.1061) The text is disputed: the manuscripts have *coluerunt*, which means "cultivated" or "inhabited" and does not give sense. Many editors emend to *coaluerunt* ("grew together"), as the translation does; the translator's note at this line should say so.

[DRAFT] (2.1067-1076) A second argument for many worlds: where matter, place, and the absence of any obstacle all exist, things must come to be. *Materies* is "matter", *semina rerum* "seeds of things", and the argument supposes that the same nature (eadem natura) that threw the seeds together here can do so elsewhere.

[DRAFT] (2.1077-1089) The argument from uniqueness: nothing in the sum is unique, since every kind has many members (in summa res nulla sit una). Lucretius then applies the principle to heaven, earth, sun, moon, and sea (1084-1086), which are likewise not unique but numberless. The conclusion rests on analogy with living species, and the reader should note that it depends on the premise that worlds, like animals, are born and die (1087-1089).

[DRAFT] (2.1087) The phrase *vitae depactus terminus alte* ("the boundary-stone of life, set deep") echoes the proem's *alte terminus haerens* (1.77, also 1.596, 5.89), the boundary-stone of each thing's power. Here the boundary is mortality, applied to worlds as well as to creatures.

[DRAFT] (2.1090-1092) The conclusion of the section, the freedom of nature from the gods (dominis privata superbis), prepares the attack on divine governance. This is the doctrine of the poem in miniature: nature acts by herself (ipsa sua per se sponte) without the gods (dis... expers). It also sets up the argument of 1093-1104.

[DRAFT] (2.1093-1104) Lucretius's question runs through a series of infinitives (regere, habere, convertere, suffire, esse praesto, faciat, concutiat, mittat, disturbet, saeviat). It is an argument from the gods' own nature: beings at peace (tranquilla pectora, placidum aevom) cannot also run the cosmos or throw thunderbolts. *Aevom* is "age", the register's rendering. The thunderbolt that strikes the guilty and the innocent alike (nocentes... indignos) is a standard Epicurean argument against divine justice, and it recurs at 6.387-422 and in Book 1.
