  973  Finally, so that every living creature can feel,
  974  if sense must now be granted to their beginnings,
  975  what of those that make up the human race in particular?
  976  No doubt they too must shake with trembling laughter and guffaw,
  977  and sprinkle their faces and cheeks with dewy tears,
  978  and be skilled at saying much about the mixture of things,
  979  and go on to ask what their own first-beginnings are;
  980  since, made in the likeness of whole mortals,
  981  they too in their turn must consist of other elements,
  982  and those again of others, so that you never dare to stop;
  983  for I shall press you: whatever you say can speak and laugh
  984  and be wise must be made of others that do these same things.
  985  But if we see that this is raving and madness,
  986  and a man can laugh though not made of laughing things,
  987  and be wise and give an account in learned words
  988  though not made of seeds that are wise and eloquent,
  989  why should the things we see have sense be any less able
  990  to be of seeds mixed together that lack sense in every part?
  991  Finally, we are all sprung from heavenly seed;{{NOTE: sky as father, earth as mother; Lucretius takes over the old pairing, then at 998 explains the name naturally}}
  992  all have that same father, from whom the nourishing mother,
  993  earth, when she has received the liquid drops of moisture,
  994  teems and bears the shining grain and the glad trees
  995  and the human race, bears all the generations of wild beasts,
  996  since she provides the pastures on which all feed their bodies
  997  and lead a sweet life and propagate their offspring;
  998  therefore she has rightly gained the name of mother.
  999  Likewise what came from the earth before goes back
 1000  into the earth, and what was sent from the shores of the ether
 1001  is carried back again and received by the precincts of heaven.
 1002  Nor does death so destroy things as to annihilate
 1003  the bodies of matter, but it scatters their union;
 1004  then it joins one to another and brings it about that all
 1005  things change their shapes and alter their colors
 1006  and take on sense and in an instant give it up;
 1007  so that you may know it matters, for the first-beginnings of things,
 1008  with what and in what position they are held together,
 1009  and what motions they give and receive among themselves,
 1010  and may not think that there can reside in eternal
 1011  first bodies what we see floating on the surfaces
 1012  of things, and at times being born and suddenly perishing.
 1013  Indeed, even in my verses themselves it matters
 1014  with what and in what order each is placed;
 1015  for the same ones signify heaven, sea, lands, rivers, sun,
 1016  the same signify grain, trees, living creatures;
 1017  if not all, yet by far the greatest part
 1018  are alike; but by their position the things differ.
 1019  So too in things themselves, when of matter
 1020  the intervals, paths, connections, weights, blows,
 1021  collisions, motions, order, position, shapes
 1022  are changed, the things too must be changed.
 1023  Now apply your mind, for me, to true reasoning.
 1024  For a thing strikingly new is struggling to reach
 1025  your ears, and a new face of things to show itself.
 1026  But there is no thing so easy that it does not at first
 1027  prove harder to believe, and likewise
 1028  nothing so great, nothing so wonderful,
 1029  that all do not little by little lessen their wonder at it.
 1030  Consider first the clear and pure color of the sky,
 1031  and all it holds within it, the stars wandering everywhere,
 1032  and the moon, and the sun's brightness with its glorious light;
 1033  all of which, if now for the first time to mortals,
 1034  if they were suddenly thrown before them unforeseen,
 1035  what could be called more wonderful than these things,
 1036  or what would the nations less have dared beforehand to believe could be?
 1037  Nothing, I think: so wondrous would this appearance have been.
 1038  Yet now, wearied with the surfeit of seeing, no one
 1039  deigns to look up at the shining precincts of heaven.
 1040  Cease, therefore, frightened by mere newness,
 1041  to spit reasoning out of your mind, but rather with keen
 1042  judgment weigh it, and if it seems true to you,
 1043  give in; or, if it is false, gird yourself against it.
 1044  For the mind seeks reasoning, since the sum of place
 1045  is infinite outside, beyond these walls of the world,
 1046  of what is there further, where thought longs to look ever onward
 1047  and where the free cast of the mind itself flies through.{{NOTE: animi iactus, the mind's "cast" or projection, Lucretius's version of Epicurus' term for the mind reaching out to grasp what the senses cannot}}
 1048  First of all, for us in every direction all around,
 1049  on either side, above and below, through the whole,
 1050  there is no limit; as I have taught, and the thing itself
 1051  cries aloud, and the nature of the deep shines clear.
 1052  In no way, then, must it be thought likely,
 1053  since space lies empty, infinite, in every direction,
 1054  and seeds in numberless number in the deep sum
 1055  fly about in many ways, driven by eternal motion,
 1056  that this one orb of lands and this sky alone were created,
 1057  and those so many bodies of matter outside do nothing;
 1058  especially since this world was made by nature, and the seeds
 1059  of things themselves, of their own accord, colliding by chance,
 1060  driven together in many ways, at random, vainly, to no purpose,
 1061  at last grew together into those which, suddenly thrown together,{{NOTE: reading coluerunt as "grew together" (= coaluerunt, as many editors print)}}
 1062  might become forever the origins of great things,
 1063  of earth and sea and sky and the race of living things.
 1064  Therefore again and again you must confess
 1065  that there are elsewhere other gatherings of matter
 1066  such as this is, which the ether holds in its greedy embrace.
 1067  Further, when much matter is made ready,
 1068  when place is at hand and no thing and no cause
 1069  delays, things must surely be carried on and come to be.
 1070  As it is, if there is such a store of seeds as
 1071  the whole life of living things could not count out,
 1072  and the same nature remains, which can throw the seeds of things
 1073  together into every place in the same way
 1074  as they have been thrown together here, you must confess
 1075  that in other regions there are other orbs of lands
 1076  and various races of men and generations of wild beasts.
 1077  Add to this that in the sum there is no one thing
 1078  that is born unique and grows unique and alone,
 1079  but is of some generation, and very many are of the same
 1080  kind. First, among living creatures, with your mind as guide,
 1081  you will find it so with the mountain-roaming race of wild beasts,
 1082  so with the twin offspring of men, so finally with the mute
 1083  herds of the scaly ones and all the bodies of flying things.
 1084  Therefore you must confess, on a like principle, that sky
 1085  and earth and sun, moon, sea, and all else that is,
 1086  are not unique, but rather of number beyond numbering;
 1087  since the boundary-stone of life, set deep,{{NOTE: echoes 1.77, "its boundary-stone set deep"}}
 1088  awaits these as surely, and they are of a body as born
 1089  as every kind that abounds here, kind by kind, in such things.
 1090  If you know these things well and hold them, nature is seen
 1091  to be free at once, stripped of proud masters,
 1092  doing all things of herself, by her own accord, without the gods.
 1093  For by the holy hearts of the gods, which in tranquil peace{{NOTE: an oath, "I appeal to the gods' own calm"; the gods are invoked to witness that they could not do these things}}
 1094  pass a placid age and a serene life,
 1095  who can rule the sum of the immeasurable, who hold in hand
 1096  the strong reins of the deep and guide them,
 1097  who can turn all the heavens alike, and warm
 1098  all the fruitful lands with fires of the ether,
 1099  or be present in all places at all times,
 1100  to make darkness with clouds and shake the calm of heaven
 1101  with sound, then hurl thunderbolts, and often
 1102  throw down his own shrines, and, withdrawing into deserts,
 1103  rage there, exercising his bolt, which often passes the guilty
 1104  by, and strikes dead the unworthy and the undeserving?
