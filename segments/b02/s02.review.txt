  294  Nor was the store of matter ever more tightly packed
  295  than now, nor again spread apart by greater intervals;
  296  for nothing is added to it, and nothing perishes from it.
  297  Therefore the motion in which the bodies of the beginnings {{NOTE: principia, "beginnings", Lucretius's alternative term for the first-beginnings (primordia)}}
  298  are now is the same motion they were in through ages gone by,
  299  and hereafter they will always be carried in like manner,
  300  and what has been wont to be born will be born under the same
  301  condition, and will be, and grow, and be strong in their strength,
  302  as much as is granted to each by nature's covenants.
  303  Nor can any force change the sum of things;
  304  for there is nothing outside into which any kind of matter
  305  could flee out of the whole, nor any source from which, into the whole,
  306  a new force could rise and burst in and change the whole
  307  nature of things and turn its motions aside.
  308  In these matters it is no wonder why,
  309  although all the first-beginnings of things are in motion,
  310  the sum nonetheless seems to stand in utmost rest, {{NOTE: summa ... summa: Lucretius plays "the sum" against "utmost"}}
  311  except where some thing makes motions with its own body.
  312  For the whole nature of the first bodies lies far below
  313  our senses, out of reach; therefore, since you cannot see
  314  the bodies themselves, their motions too must steal away from sight;
  315  the more so since even things that we can see still often hide
  316  their motions when withdrawn from us by a distance of place.
  317  For often on a hill, cropping the glad pastures,
  318  the woolly flocks creep on wherever, calling each one,
  319  the grasses invite them, jeweled with fresh dew,
  320  and the lambs, filled full, play and gently butt their heads;
  321  yet all these, seen from far off, seem to us blurred together,
  322  like a whiteness standing still on a green hill.
  323  Again, when great legions fill the spaces of the plains
  324  with their running, stirring up the images of war, {{NOTE: simulacra belli, mock battle or maneuvers; "images" kept as the fixed rendering of simulacra}}
  325  when the glitter lifts itself to the sky and all the earth around
  326  shines back with bronze, and beneath, from the force of men,
  327  a sound is roused by their feet, and the mountains, struck
  328  by the shouting, throw back the voices to the stars of the world,
  329  and horsemen wheel around and suddenly cross through
  330  the middle of the plains, shaking them with their strong charge;
  331  and yet there is some place on the high mountains from which
  332  they seem to stand still, a glitter resting on the plains.
  333  Come now, next learn of what sort the origins
  334  of all things are, and how far they differ in their forms,
  335  how varied they are, in shapes of many kinds;
  336  not that few are endowed with a like form,
  337  but because, as a rule, not all are alike to all.
  338  Nor is it a wonder; for since their store is so great
  339  that there is neither end, as I have taught, nor any sum to it,
  340  it must surely be that not all are in every way
  341  alike to all, of the same contour and of similar shape.
  342  Further, the human race, and the mute swimming flocks
  343  of scaly creatures, and the glad herds, and wild beasts,
  344  and the various birds, those that throng the gladdening
  345  watering-places around the banks and springs and lakes,
  346  and those that flit abroad through the pathless woods:
  347  go on and take any one of these you will, kind by kind;
  348  you will find that they still differ from one another in shape.
  349  Nor in any other way could offspring know its mother,
  350  nor mother offspring; yet we see that they can,
  351  and that they are known to one another no less than men are.
  352  For often before the fair shrines of the gods a calf
  353  falls slaughtered beside the incense-burning altars,
  354  breathing out a warm river of blood from its breast;
  355  but the bereaved mother, wandering through the green glades,
  356  knows the prints pressed into the ground by cloven hooves,
  357  searching every place with her eyes, if she can anywhere
  358  catch sight of her lost young, and, stopping, fills
  359  the leafy grove with her lament, and often goes back
  360  to the stall, pierced through with longing for her calf;
  361  nor can tender willows, nor grasses quickened with dew,
  362  nor any rivers gliding level with their banks
  363  delight her mind and turn aside the sudden care,
  364  nor can the sight of other calves in the glad pastures
  365  draw her mind away and lighten it of care;
  366  so much does she seek something her own and known.
  367  Again, the tender kids with trembling voices
  368  know their horned mothers, and the butting lambs
  369  the bleating flocks; so, as nature demands,
  370  each runs, for the most part, to its own udder of milk.
  371  Finally, take any grain you will: you will still not see
  372  that every grain in its kind is like the others,
  373  without some difference of form running between them.
  374  And by a like principle we see the race of shells
  375  paint the lap of the earth, where with soft waves
  376  the sea beats the thirsty sand of the curving shore.
  377  Therefore again and again by like reasoning it must be,
  378  since they exist by nature and were not made by the hand
  379  of one, to a fixed form, the first-beginnings of things,
  380  that some of them fly about with shapes unlike each other.
  381  It is very easy for us, by the mind's reasoning, to explain
  382  why the fire of the thunderbolt is far more piercing
  383  than ours, which flows from torches, born of the earth;
  384  for you could say that the heavenly fire of the thunderbolt
  385  is subtler, consisting of small shapes,
  386  and so passes through openings that this fire of ours,
  387  born from wood and created from the torch, cannot.
  388  Again, light passes through horn, but rain
  389  is thrown back. Why so, unless those bodies of light
  390  are smaller than the ones that make the kindly liquid of the waters?
  391  And although we see wine in an instant pass
  392  through a strainer, yet on the other hand oil lingers slow,
  393  either because, surely, it is made of larger elements,
  394  or ones more hooked and tangled with one another,
  395  and so it happens that they cannot be so suddenly drawn apart
  396  from one another that the first-beginnings, each one,
  397  might, one by one, seep through the openings of each thing.
  398  Add to this that the liquids of honey and of milk
  399  are handled in the mouth with a pleasant feeling on the tongue;
  400  but on the other hand the loathsome nature of wormwood and of wild
  401  centaury twists the mouth with its foul taste;
  402  so that you can easily recognize that of smooth and round bodies
  403  are made the things that can touch the senses pleasantly,
  404  but on the other hand whatever seems bitter and harsh,
  405  these are held bound together by more hooked ones,
  406  and for that reason are used to tear their way into our
  407  senses, and to burst through the body as they enter.
  408  Finally, all things good to the senses and bad to the touch
  409  are at war with one another, made of unlike shape;
  410  lest you should perhaps think that the harsh shudder
  411  of the screeching saw consists of elements as smooth
  412  as the Muses' melodies, which musicians, along the strings,
  413  wake with nimble fingers and give form to;
  414  nor should you think that first-beginnings of like form pierce
  415  into men's nostrils when foul corpses are burning
  416  and when the stage is freshly sprinkled with Cilician saffron
  417  and the altar nearby breathes out the perfumes of Panchaea; {{NOTE: Panchaea, a fabled land of incense in the far east, off Arabia}}
  418  nor suppose the pleasant colors of things are made of like
  419  seed, the colors that can feast the eyes,
  420  and those that prick the sight and force it to weep,
  421  or look foul and ugly with their foul appearance.
  422  For every thing whatsoever that caresses the senses
  423  was not created without some smoothness in its beginnings;
  424  but on the other hand whatever is troublesome and harsh
  425  is not found without some roughness of matter.
  426  There are also things that are rightly thought neither to be smooth
  427  nor altogether hooked with bent points,
  428  but rather made with little angles jutting out a little, such as
  429  can tickle the senses rather than hurt them;
  430  of which kind are wine-lees and the flavors of elecampane.
  431  Finally, that hot fires and icy frost
  432  are toothed in unlike ways and so prick the senses
  433  of the body, the touch of each gives us proof.
  434  For touch, touch, by the holy powers of the gods,
  435  is the sense of the body, whether when a thing from outside
  436  works its way in, or when a thing born in the body hurts it,
  437  or gives delight going out through the generative acts of Venus,
  438  or when, from a collision, they riot within the body itself,
  439  and the seeds, roused, throw the sense into confusion among themselves;
  440  as if perhaps with your hand you yourself were to strike
  441  any part of your body you please and make the trial.
  442  Therefore the forms of the beginnings must differ far,
  443  since they can give rise to various sensations.
  444  Finally, the things that seem to us hardened and dense,
  445  these must consist of elements more hooked together
  446  and be held fast, compacted deep, as if by branches.
  447  In this class, first of all, stand rocks of adamant
  448  in the front line, used to scorning blows,
  449  and strong flints, and the strength of hard iron,
  450  and bronze that shrieks as it holds out against the bolts.
  451  Those indeed must be made rather of smooth and round bodies,
  452  the things that stand as liquids, with a fluid body.
  453  For a scoop of poppy-seed flows as easily as one of water;
  454  for the little globes do not hold to one another,
  455  and, once disturbed, it runs as readily down a slope.
  456  Finally, all things that you see in a point of time
  457  scatter, like smoke and mists and flames, it must be that,
  458  if they are not all made of smooth and round bodies,
  459  yet they are not hampered by tangled ones,
  460  so that they can prick the body and pierce stones
  461  and yet not stick together; so that whatever we see
  462  to be toothed to the senses, you can easily know
  463  is made not of tangled but of sharp elements.
