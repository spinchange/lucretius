[DRAFT] (2.142) The doctrine here is the atoms' speed (mobilitas), which the poem proves by contrast with the slower travel of sunlight through air. The "bodies of matter" (materiai corporibus) are the first bodies of the register, here named by their physical side.

[DRAFT] (2.151) Lucretius says "inane vacuum" together (non per inane meat vacuum). In Epicurean terms, light and heat are compound and meet resistance from the air, whereas a single first-beginning moving through pure void meets none. The translation keeps "void" and renders the doubled term as "empty void", as at 2.158.

[DRAFT] (2.164a) Editors mark a lacuna here, so the sentence that governs "nor to follow out" (nec persectari) is lost. The translation prints the gap unnumbered and supplies nothing. The infinitive in 165 depends on a lost main verb. The people at issue are probably those who will not trace the motions of individual atoms.

[DRAFT] (2.167) "Some, against this" (quidam contra) are never named. They hold the teleological view of nature, which the Epicureans attacked. In Roman terms the target is the Stoic doctrine of providence, though Lucretius does not name the school. The "guide of life, divine pleasure" (dux vitae dia voluptas) is Lucretius's own recasting, with the Venus of the proem hidden behind the sexual drive.

[DRAFT] (2.181) The faults of the world (tanta stat praedita culpa) are what Lucretius argues from, not the world's goodness. He answers his own promise (posterius, 182) at 5.195-234, where he lists the uninhabitable regions, the toil of farming, and the dangers of infancy and disease.

[DRAFT] (2.184) The topic is "upward" motion (sursum ferri). Flame, smoke and growing plants seem to defy weight. Lucretius argues that they rise only because something pushes them. The doctrine behind it is that every body has weight and by itself tends only downward (pondera, quantum in se est). This sets up the swerve argument at 216ff.

[DRAFT] (2.206) The "night torches of heaven" (nocturnas faces caeli) are meteors or shooting stars, which Lucretius treats as fire falling toward earth. Stars and sunlight (209-212) are offered as further instances of downward tendency. The "constellations" (sidera) are plural.

[DRAFT] (2.213) The slanting thunderbolts (transversos fulmina) are the lightning of 6.160ff, where Lucretius gives the full account. Here they serve as one more fire moving down, and across, under a force from outside.

[DRAFT] (2.216) The swerve is announced here (216-220). A reader will hear a precursor of modern indeterminacy, which is the reader's association, not the poet's claim. The Latin keeps the sense of "uncertain time and place" (incerto tempore ... incertisque locis), and the same phrase returns in the refrain at 292-293 (nec regione loci certa nec tempore certo).

[DRAFT] (2.221) The verb declinare is first used here, with its noun clinamen at 292. The register fixes both as "swerve". "Rain-drops falling through the deep void" (imbris uti guttae ... per inane profundum) pictures Epicurus's parallel fall, with no collisions and so no world.

[DRAFT] (2.224) The "beginnings" (principiis) are a variant name for the primordia, the first-beginnings. The register keeps the two English words apart for the two Latin words. The line's last clause, "nature would never have created anything", states the argument's stake.

[DRAFT] (2.225) The objection, which says heavier bodies falling faster would strike lighter ones, is answered at 230-239. Epicurus held that atoms in void move at equal speed whatever their weight (aeque ponderibus non aequis, 239). The modern reader may hear Galileo's falling bodies, which is the reader's association only.

[DRAFT] (2.254) "The covenants of fate" (fati foedera) is a Stoic idea of an unbroken chain of causes. The phrase echoes the poem's "nature's covenants" (foedera naturae) but reverses their force. Cause follows cause "from infinity" (ex infinito) in a chain the swerve would break.

[DRAFT] (2.256) "Free will" is Lucretius's argument from experience. "This will wrested from the fates" (fatis avolsa voluntas) is the first claim in the poem that living things possess a will that is more than mechanical. The line reads "libera ... voluntas" across 256-257, with "will" (voluntas) as the noun. The same argument is picked up at 2.261.

[DRAFT] (2.263) The starting-gates (carceribus) are the stalls of the Roman circus, thrown open at the start of a chariot race. The horses' bodies hold back for an instant behind the mind's wish, which is the proof that the will begins the motion and the body follows.

[DRAFT] (2.269) "Heart" (corde) is where Lucretius locates the mind in the breast. Compare "in pectore nostro" at 279. The word is plain and anatomical here, and the register keeps animus as "mind" in the next line (animi voluntate).

[DRAFT] (2.289) The manuscripts read "res ipsa", which makes "the thing itself" a general subject, while many editors since the early editions print "mens ipsa" ("the mind itself"), tying the swerve to the will of 260. The translation follows the source, the manuscript reading. The sense is fully available either way.

[DRAFT] (2.292) "Swerve" is the register's rendering of clinamen, the poem's only use of the noun. Compare declinare at 221, 253, 259. It is "tiny" (exiguum) because the swerve must not be seen as a slant (244-245), so the translation should keep the diminutive force.
