  469  clearly they are round nonetheless, rough though they are,
  470  so that they can roll forward together and yet hurt the senses.
  471  And that you may believe the more that rough are mixed with smooth
  472  beginnings, of which the bitter body of Neptune consists, {{NOTE: Neptune's body: the sea}}
  473  there is a way of separating them and seeing them apart:
  474  the moisture grows sweet when, through the earth, the same moisture
  475  is filtered through many times, to flow into a pit and grow mild;
  476  for it leaves behind, above, the first-beginnings of the foul brine,
  477  since, being rough, they can cling the more within the earth.
  478  Since I have taught this, I will go on to join to it a fact {{NOTE: 478-479 recur verbatim at 522-523}}
  479  which, fitted to this, draws its credit: that the first-beginnings of things
  480  vary within a finite range of shapes.
  481  For if it were not so, then again certain seeds
  482  will have to be infinite in the growth of their bodies.
  483  For within the one same smallness of any given
  484  body, the shapes cannot vary much from one another;
  485  they cannot. For suppose that of least parts, three of them,
  486  the first bodies are made, or add a few more to these;
  487  surely, when all those parts of a single body
  488  you have set at top and bottom, and changed the right for the left,
  489  and tried in every way what appearance each order gives
  490  of form to the whole of that body,
  491  for the rest, if you chance to want to vary the shapes,
  492  other parts will have to be added. From this it will follow
  493  that, on a like principle, the order will call for others,
  494  if you chance to want to vary the shapes still further.
  495  Therefore growth of body follows upon newness of forms
  496  close behind. And so there is no way you can believe
  497  that seeds exist distinguished by infinite forms,
  498  lest you force certain of them to be of monstrous greatness,
  499  which I have already shown above cannot be proved.
  500  Then barbarian robes and the gleaming Meliboean purple, {{NOTE: Meliboea, a town in Thessaly famed for purple dye from shellfish}}
  501  dipped in the Thessalian color of the shells,
  502  the golden race of peacocks, steeped in laughing charm,
  503  would lie outdone by some new color in things,
  504  and the smell of myrrh would be scorned, and the savors of honey,
  505  and the swan's notes and the intricate strains of Phoebus' strings {{NOTE: daedala, elsewhere "artificer"; here an adjective of the songs}}
  506  would fall silent, overpowered in like manner;
  507  for one thing would always rise more excellent than another.
  508  So too all things could fall back toward the worse,
  509  just as we have said they could toward the better;
  510  for one thing would also be more loathsome than another, in reverse,
  511  to nostrils, ears and eyes, and to the taste of the mouth.
  512  And since these things are not so, but a fixed limit, assigned
  513  to things, holds the sum on either side, you must admit
  514  that matter too differs in a finite number of shapes.
  515  Finally, from fires to the freezing frosts of winters
  516  the way is bounded, and measured back again in equal measure.
  517  For all heat and cold and the middle warmths
  518  lie between the two, filling out the sum in order.
  519  Therefore created things differ within a finite range,
  520  since at both ends they are marked by a two-edged point,
  521  beset by flames on this side and on that by stiff frosts.
  522  Since I have taught this, I will go on to join to it a fact
  523  which, fitted to this, draws its credit: that the first-beginnings of things,
  524  those that are made of a shape alike among themselves,
  525  are infinite in number. For since the difference
  526  of forms is finite, it must be that those that are alike
  527  are infinite, or else that the sum of matter
  528  is finite, which I have proved is not so.

  529  I will show in verses that the little bodies of matter
  530  from infinity keep the sum of things going
  531  on every side, by an unbroken relay of blows.
  532  For as to your seeing certain animals to be rarer,
  533  and discerning in those others a nature more fruitful, {{NOTE: crux: "in illis" taken of the commoner kinds; some editors read minus, "less", for magis}}
  534  yet in another region and place, in far-off lands,
  535  there may be many of that kind, and the number made up;
  536  as among four-footed beasts we see, first of all,
  537  the kind of snake-handed elephants, by whose many thousands
  538  India is fortified with a rampart of ivory,
  539  so that it cannot be entered deep: so great is the force
  540  of these beasts, of which we see very few examples.
  541  But still, to concede this too, let there be, as you please,
  542  some single thing, alone in the body it was born with,
  543  with nothing like it in the whole round of the lands;
  544  yet unless the store of matter is infinite,
  545  from which it may be conceived and brought forth, it could not
  546  be created, nor, for the rest, grow up and be nourished.
  547  For indeed, let others assume that a finite number
  548  of generative bodies of one thing are tossed through the whole:
  549  from where, where, by what force, in what way will they meet and join
  550  in so great a sea of matter, in so alien a throng?
  551  They have no means, I think, of making a union;
  552  but as, when great and many shipwrecks have arisen,
  553  the great sea is used to toss about thwarts, hulls,
  554  yards, prows, masts and swimming oars,
  555  so that along all the lands' shores the floating stern-ornaments
  556  are seen, and give mortals a warning
  557  to shun the snares of the faithless sea, its strength and guile,
  558  and never at any time to trust it,
  559  when the sly enticement of the calm sea laughs,
  560  so, if once you establish certain first-beginnings as finite,
  561  they will have to be scattered through all time
  562  and tossed apart by the conflicting tides of matter,
  563  so that they can never be driven together into union,
  564  nor linger in union, nor grow by increase;
  565  and that both of these happen, the plain fact openly shows,
  566  that things are brought forth and, once born, can grow.
  567  Therefore in any kind you please the first-beginnings of things
  568  are infinite, that is plain, from which all things are furnished.
  569  And so neither can the motions of destruction prevail
  570  forever, nor bury safety for eternity,
  571  nor again can the motions that beget and increase things
  572  keep forever what they have created.
  573  So in an equal struggle of the beginnings is waged
  574  a war entered on from infinite time.
  575  Now here, now there the life-giving powers of things conquer
  576  and are conquered in turn. With the funeral is mixed the wail
  577  that infants raise as they look on the shores of light; {{NOTE: register lists 2.577 under "the shining shores of light", but the Latin here lacks dias; "shining" omitted}}
  578  and no night has followed day, no dawn the night,
  579  that has not heard, mixed with the sick wailing of infants,
  580  the lamentations that attend on death and the black funeral.
