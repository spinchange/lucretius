ANALYSIS
1105-1111: L: multa corpora addita sunt (pf. pass. pl.) extrinsecus post tempus genitale…coortum; addita (sunt) semina; rel. quae: omne (subj.) contulit (pf. act.) iaculando; unde mare et terrae possent augescere (impf. subj.); unde appareret spatium (impf. subj.); caeli domus (subj.) tolleret tecta, consurgeret aer. E: many bodies were added; seeds were added; which the great whole brought together by hurling; from which sea/lands could increase; from which the house of heaven could gain room, lift its roof; air rise up. MATCH.
1112-1117: L: omnia corpora distribuuntur (pres. pass.) plagis, sua cuique, locis ex omnibus; recedunt ad sua saecla; umor (goes) ad umorem; terra crescit corpore terreno; ignes procudunt ignem; aether aethera; donique natura creatrix perfica (adj. with natura) perduxit omnia ad finem. E: all bodies distributed by blows; go back to own kinds; moisture to moisture; earth grows; fires forge fire, ether ether; until nature, bringing to completion, has led all things to the limit. MATCH.
1118-1119: L: ut fit ubi nihilo plus est quod datur intra venas quam quod fluit atque recedit. E: as happens when nothing more is given within the veins than flows away and withdraws. MATCH.
1120-1121: L: hic aetas debet consistere omnibus rebus; hic natura refrenat auctum viribus suis. E: here the age of all things must stand; here nature reins in growth with her own strength. MATCH.
1122-1127: L: quaecumque vides grandescere, scandere gradus; (ea) plura corpora adsumunt quam mittunt; dum cibus omnis inditur in venas; dum non ita dispessa sunt ut multa remittant et plus dispendi faciant quam vescitur aetas. E: whatever you see grow and climb take in more than they send out; while food is put into all their veins (omnis read as acc. pl. with venas, defensible); while not so spread that they give back much and make more waste than age feeds on. MATCH.
1128-1130: L: manus dandum est (gerundive) multa corpora fluere atque recedere rebus; sed plura accedere debent donec tetigere cacumen. E: we must grant many bodies flow and withdraw; more must come in until they have touched the peak. MATCH.
1131-1132: L: aetas (subj.) frangit vires et robur et liquitur in partem peiorem. E: age breaks strength and vigor and melts into the worse part. MATCH.
1133-1138: L: quanto res amplior (augmine adempto) et quo latior, (tanto) plura corpora dispargit et mittit; nec cibus diditur facile in venas ei; nec satis est, pro quam aestus exaestuat, unde queat tantum suboriri ac subpeditare (impersonal or with the supply as subject: from which so much may arise and be furnished). E: the larger and wider, the more bodies it scatters; nor is food distributed; nor is there enough, in proportion to the tides it pours out, from which "it" (naturally the thing) could spring up anew and supply as much. DIFF: subject of suboriri/subpeditare shifted to the thing.
1139-1143: L: iure pereunt, cum rarefacta sunt fluendo et cum omnia succumbunt plagis externis; quando cibus defit grandi aevo; nec corpora tuditantia cessant conficere rem ullam et infesta (with corpora) domare plagis. E: rightly they perish when grown rare by flowing and all things give way beneath blows; since food runs short in great age; and the battering bodies never cease to wear a thing down and subdue it with hostile blows (adjective transferred, defensible). MATCH.
1144-1149: L: moenia mundi expugnata dabunt (fut.) labem ruinasque; enim cibus debet integrare, fulcire, sustentare omnia, nequiquam, quoniam nec venae perpetiuntur quod satis est, neque natura ministrat quantum opus est. E: walls will be stormed and give way to decay; food must renew, prop, sustain, in vain, since neither veins endure to take in enough nor nature supplies as needed. MATCH.
1150-1152: L: fracta est aetas; tellus effeta vix creat animalia parva; quae creavit cuncta saecla deditque corpora ferarum partu. E: the age is broken; the worn-out earth scarcely creates small animals, she who created all kinds and gave birth to huge beasts. MATCH.
1153-1156: L: haud aurea funis demisit (pf.) saecla de caelo in arva; nec mare nec fluctus crearunt; sed tellus genuit, eadem quae nunc alit ex se. E: not that a golden rope let down the kinds from heaven into the fields; nor did sea and waves create them; but the earth bore them, the same that now feeds them. MATCH.
1157-1163: L: praeterea ipsa creavit fruges vinetaque sponte sua primum mortalibus; ipsa dedit fetus et pabula; quae nunc vix grandescunt aucta labore; conterimus boves et viris; conficimus ferrum, vix arvis suppeditati (nom. pl., = we); usque adeo parcunt fetus augentque laborem. E: further, she herself first created crops and vineyards; gave fruits and pastures; which now scarcely grow, increased by labor; we wear out oxen and farmers' strength; we wear down the iron share, scarcely supplied by the fields; so much do they stint their fruits and increase labor. MATCH.
1164-1167: L: arator quassans caput suspirat crebrius (se) cecidisse labores in cassum; cum confert tempora praesentia praeteritis, laudat fortunas parentis. E: the aged plowman shakes his head and sighs more often that his labors have come to nothing; comparing present with past times, praises his father's fortunes. MATCH.
1168-1172: L: sator vitis vetulae atque vietae incusat momen temporis, saeclum fatigat, crepat ut genus antiquum pietate repletum tolerarit (pf. subj.) aevom perfacile angustis finibus, cum minor esset modus agri viritim. E: the planter of the old and shriveled vine blames the drift of time, wearies the age with complaint, grumbles how the race of old, filled with piety, bore life very easily within narrow bounds, though the measure of land was far smaller. MATCH.
1173-1174: L: nec tenet omnia tabescere et ire ad capulum, defessa spatio aetatis vetusto. E: nor does he grasp that all things waste away and go to the tomb, worn out by the ancient span of age. MATCH.

FINDINGS
1138	AMBIGUITY	MINOR	0.55	"unde queat tantum suboriri ac subpeditare" means that there is not enough from which so great a supply could arise and be furnished, but the English "from which it could spring up anew and supply as much" makes "it" read naturally as the wasting thing itself, so that the thing, rather than the replenishment, springs up anew; "from which as much could arise again and be supplied."
