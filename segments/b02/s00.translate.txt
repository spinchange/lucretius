    1  Sweet it is, when on the great sea the winds are churning the waters,
    2  to watch from land the great toil of another;
    3  not because anyone's distress is a delightful pleasure,
    4  but because it is sweet to see what evils you yourself are free of.
    6  Sweet, too, to look upon the great contests of war {{NOTE: 2.6 printed before 2.5 in the source (editorial transposition); order kept}}
    5  drawn up across the plains, with no share of your own in the danger;
    7  but nothing is sweeter than to hold, well fortified,
    8  the serene precincts built high by the teaching of the wise,
    9  from where you can look down on others and see them everywhere
   10  wandering and, straying here and there, seeking the road of life,
   11  contending in talent, competing in nobility of birth,
   12  striving night and day with surpassing labor
   13  to rise to the height of wealth and gain the mastery of things.
   14  O miserable minds of men, O blind hearts!
   15  In what darkness of life and in how great dangers
   16  this span of life, whatever it is, is passed! Do you not see
   17  that nature barks for nothing else for herself than this, that {{NOTE: "barks" (latrare): Lucretius's own blunt word for nature's demand}}
   18  pain be parted from the body and be absent, and the mind enjoy
   19  a pleasant sense of things, set apart from care and fear?
   20  Therefore we see that for the body's nature few things
   21  are needed at all: such things as take away pain,
   22  and such that they can also spread out many delights for us,
   23  more welcome now and then; nor does nature herself require,
   24  if there are no golden images of youths about the halls
   25  holding fire-bearing lamps in their right hands
   26  so that light may be supplied for feasts by night,
   27  nor the house gleam with silver and shine with gold,
   28  nor lyres echo through paneled and gilded rafters, {{NOTE: templa here = the roof-beams or coffered ceiling of a hall, not temples}}
   29  when still, lying together on the soft grass
   30  beside a stream of water under the boughs of a tall tree,
   31  with no great wealth they tend their bodies pleasantly,
   32  above all when the weather smiles and the seasons
   33  of the year sprinkle the greening grass with flowers.
   34  Nor do hot fevers leave the body any faster
   35  if you are tossed on woven pictures and blushing purple
   36  than if you must lie beneath a common man's coverlet.
   37  Therefore, since in our body treasures are of no profit,
   38  nor nobility, nor the glory of a kingdom,
   39  for the rest, we must think they do the mind no good either;
   40  unless perhaps, when you see your legions over the spaces of the Campus {{NOTE: the Campus Martius at Rome, where troops drilled}}
   41  seething as they stir up the images of war, {{NOTE: belli simulacra: mock battles, field exercises; simulacra kept as "images"}}
   42  made firm with great reserves and a force of wealth,
   43  your ships fitted out with arms, all alike full of spirit, {{NOTE: stlattas (a broad-built ship) is the printed text of a corrupt line; readings and sense here are disputed}}
   44  then, before these things, the religions in their terror
   45  flee panic-stricken from your mind, and the fears of death
   46  then leave your heart empty and loosed from care.
   47  But if we see that these things are laughable and a mockery,
   48  and in truth the fears of men and the cares that dog them
   49  fear neither the clash of arms nor savage weapons,
   50  and boldly move among kings and the masters of things,
   51  and do not stand in awe of the glitter that comes from gold
   52  nor the bright splendor of a purple robe,
   53  why do you doubt that this power belongs wholly to reasoning,
   54  especially when all life labors in darkness?
   55  For just as children tremble and fear everything
   56  in blind darkness, so we in the light are afraid
   57  at times of things that are no more to be feared than
   58  what children in the dark shudder at and imagine will come.
   59  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness {{NOTE: 2.59-61 repeat 1.146-148 verbatim; the English repeats its own lines}}
   60  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
   61  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
   62  Come now, by what motion the generative bodies of matter
   63  beget the various things and dissolve them once begotten,
   64  and by what force they are compelled to do it, and what
   65  mobility is given them for travelling through the great void,
   66  I shall explain; you, remember to give yourself to my words.
   67  For certainly matter does not cling together, packed close
   68  in a mass, since we see each thing diminish,
   69  and we perceive all things flowing away, as it were, over long age,
   70  and age withdrawing them from our eyes,
   71  while yet the sum is seen to remain unimpaired,
   72  because the bodies that depart from each thing
   73  diminish what they leave, and on what they come to bestow increase.
   74  They force the one to grow old and the other in turn to flower,
   75  and do not linger there. So the sum of things is ever
   76  renewed, and mortals live by exchange among themselves.
   77  Some races increase, others are diminished,
   78  and in a brief space the generations of living things are changed
   79  and like runners pass on the torch of life. {{NOTE: the relay torch race, in which runners handed a lit torch from one to the next}}
   80  If you think the first-beginnings of things can stand still
   81  and by standing still beget new motions of things,
   82  you wander far astray from true reasoning.
   83  For since they wander through the void, all the first-beginnings
   84  of things must be carried either by their own weight
   85  or perhaps by the blow of another. For when, swift, they have often
   86  met and clashed, it comes about that suddenly they spring
   87  apart in different ways; and no wonder, since they are very hard,
   88  solid in their weight, and nothing blocks them from behind.
   89  And so that you may see more clearly that all the bodies
   90  of matter are tossed about, remember that the whole
   91  has no bottom in the sum, and the first bodies have no place
   92  to come to rest, since space is without end or limit,
   93  and that it spreads out immeasurable in all directions everywhere
   94  I have shown in many words and proved by sure reasoning. {{NOTE: referring back to 1.951-1051}}
   95  And since this is settled, surely no rest
   96  is given to the first bodies through the deep void,
   97  but rather, driven by unceasing and varied motion,
   98  some, when they have collided, leap back over great intervals,
   99  and some are harried by the blow within short spaces.
  100  And all those that, in a denser gathering,
  101  brought together, rebound across small intervals,
  102  entangled by their own interlocking shapes,
  103  these form the strong roots of rock and the savage bodies
  104  of iron, and the other things of this kind.
  105  Those few others that wander on through the great void {{NOTE: the text of 2.105-106 is uncertain; rendered as printed}}
  106  leap far apart and run far back again
  107  across great intervals; these furnish us the thin air
  108  and the shining light of the sun.
  109  And many besides wander through the great void
  110  which have been thrown out of the unions of things and nowhere
  111  have been able, even when let in, to join their motions together.
  112  Of this thing, as I tell, an image and picture
  113  is always turning before our eyes and pressing on us.
  114  For look closely, whenever the sun's light is poured
  115  by rays let in through the darkened places of houses:
  116  you will see many minute bodies in many ways, through the void,
  117  mingling in the very light of the rays,
  118  and as though in everlasting conflict waging wars and battles,
  119  fighting troop by troop, and giving no pause,
  120  driven on by frequent unions and partings;
  121  so that from this you may guess what it is for the first-beginnings
  122  of things to be tossed forever in the great void.
  123  So far as it goes, a small thing can give
  124  a pattern of great things and the tracks of knowledge.
  125  For this reason, too, it is more fitting that you attend to these
  126  bodies that are seen to be in turmoil in the sun's rays,
  127  because such turmoils signify that there are motions
  128  of matter, too, hidden and blind, beneath them.
  129  For you will see many there, struck by blind blows,
  130  change their course and, driven back, return,
  131  now this way, now that, in all directions everywhere.
  132  This wandering, you may be sure, all comes from the beginnings. {{NOTE: error = "wandering", the literal sense; principia rendered "beginnings", distinct from primordia "first-beginnings"}}
  133  For first the first-beginnings of things move of themselves;
  134  then those bodies that are formed of a small gathering
  135  and are, as it were, nearest to the strength of the beginnings
  136  are set going, struck by their blind blows,
  137  and they in turn assail bodies a little larger.
  138  So motion rises from the beginnings and comes out
  139  little by little to our senses, so that those too
  140  are moved which we can make out in the light of the sun,
  141  though by what blows they do it does not plainly appear.
