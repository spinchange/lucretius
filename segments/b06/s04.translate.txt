  470  Further, that from the whole sea as well nature lifts very many
  471  bodies, clothes declare, when they are hung up on the shore
  472  and take into themselves a clinging of moisture.
  473  So it seems all the more that many bodies, to swell the clouds,
  474  can also rise up together from the salt surge of the ocean;
  475  for the principle of all moistures is of one blood.
  476  Further, from all the rivers, and at the same time from the earth
  477  itself, we see mists and steaming heat rise up,
  478  which are pressed out from there like a breath and so carried upward,
  479  and they suffuse the sky with their murk, and high
  480  clouds they furnish, little by little, by coming together;
  481  for the heat of the sign-bearing ether above presses on them too, {{NOTE: signifer aether, the heaven that carries the signs of the zodiac; cf. 1.2 "the gliding signs of heaven".}}
  482  and, as if by packing them close, weaves rain-cloud beneath the blue.
  483  It happens too that into this sky there come from outside those
  484  bodies that make clouds and flying storm-clouds;
  485  for I have taught that their number is past counting, and the sum
  486  of the deep infinite, and I have shown with what great speed
  487  bodies fly, and how in a moment
  488  they are used to passing across a space beyond telling.
  489  It is no wonder, therefore, if often in a short time
  490  storm and darkness, with winds so great,
  491  cover seas and lands, looming over them from above,
  492  since on every side, through all the pores of the ether,
  493  and, as it were, through the breathing-holes of the great world all around,
  494  a way out and a way in stands granted to the elements.
  495  Come now, how rainy moisture grows together in the high
  496  clouds, and how, sent down to the earth as a shower,
  497  it falls, I will explain. First, I shall prove that many seeds of water
  498  rise up at once together with the clouds themselves
  499  from all things, and that both grow alike in this way,
  500  both the clouds and the water, whatever there is in the clouds,
  501  just as in us the body grows alike together with the blood,
  502  and sweat too, and whatever moisture, finally, is in the limbs.
  503  Often, too, the clouds take in a great deal of the sea's
  504  moisture, just like hanging fleeces of wool,
  505  when the winds carry the clouds above the great sea.
  506  In like manner from all the rivers moisture
  507  is lifted into the clouds. And when in them many seeds of water
  508  have come well together in many ways, increased from every side,
  509  the crowded clouds strive to send out their moisture
  510  in two ways; for the force of the wind thrusts them together, and the very
  511  abundance of storm-clouds, driven together in a greater throng,
  512  urges and presses from above and makes the showers flow out.
  513  Further, when the clouds too are thinned out by winds
  514  or dissolved, struck from above by the heat of the sun,
  515  they send down rainy moisture and drip, as wax set over
  516  a hot fire wastes away and melts in plenty.
  517  But a violent shower comes when violently, in both ways,
  518  the clouds are pressed, by their heaped force and by the onset of the wind.
  519  And rains are used to holding on a long time and lingering long
  520  when many seeds of water are set in motion,
  521  and clouds on clouds and drenching storm-clouds
  522  are borne on over them from every quarter everywhere,
  523  and when the whole earth, steaming, breathes its moisture back.
  524  Here, when the sun with its rays amid the dark storm
  525  has shone facing it, against the spray of the rain-clouds,
  526  then the color of the bow appears in the black clouds.
  527  The other things that grow on high and are created on high,
  528  and that grow together in the clouds, all of them, utterly
  529  all, snow, winds, hail, and the chill hoarfrosts,
  530  and the great force of ice, the great hardening of waters,
  531  and the check that everywhere reins in the eager rivers,
  532  it is still very easy to find these out and see them in the mind,
  533  how all of them come about and why they are created,
  534  once you have come to know well what properties are granted to the elements.
  535  Come now, by what principle the motions of the earth come about,
  536  learn. And first of all, be sure you think the earth to be,
  537  below as well as above, full on every side of windy
  538  caverns, and to carry many lakes and many pools
  539  in its lap, and cliffs and sheer-broken rocks;
  540  and many rivers hidden beneath the back of the earth
  541  you must suppose roll on with force their sunken waves, unseen;
  542  for the thing itself demands that it be like itself everywhere.
  543  With these things, therefore, joined on and set beneath it,
  544  the earth trembles above, shaken by great collapses,
  545  when beneath it age has undermined huge caverns;
  546  indeed whole mountains fall, and from the great and sudden
  547  shock the tremors creep out widely from there.
  548  And with reason, since shaken by wagons there tremble
  549  whole houses beside the road, under no great weight,
  550  and they leap no less if anything in the road
  551  jolts up the iron-bound rims of the wheels on either side.
  552  It happens too, when into great and vast pools of water
  553  a huge clod rolls down from the earth through age,
  554  that the earth too is tossed by the wave of the water and totters;
  555  as a vessel cannot stand steady among waters unless the liquid {{NOTE: inter aquas is doubtful; many editors read interdum, "a vessel sometimes cannot stand steady".}}
  556  within has ceased to be tossed about in an unsteady wave.
  557  Further, when wind, gathered through the hollow places of the earth,
  558  leans forward from one quarter and presses,
  559  straining with great strength upon the deep caverns,
  560  the earth leans over to where the headlong force of the wind presses.
  561  Then the buildings that are built up above the earth,
  562  and the more so the higher each rises toward heaven,
  563  lean and threaten, pitched forward toward the same quarter,
  564  and the beams, thrust out, overhang, ready to go.
  565  And yet men are afraid to believe that for the nature of the great world
  566  some fatal time and overthrow is waiting,
  567  even when they see so vast a mass of lands leaning over!
  568  But unless the winds drew breath, no force could rein things in
  569  or hold them back from destruction as they go;
  570  as it is, because they draw breath by turns and grow heavy,
  571  and, as it were, gathered, come back again, and give way, driven back,
  572  for this reason the earth more often threatens collapse
  573  than causes it; for it leans over and springs back,
  574  and, having slipped forward, recovers its seat in balance.
  575  By this principle, therefore, all buildings totter,
  576  the top more than the middle, the middle than the lowest, the lowest very little.
  577  This too is a cause of that same great trembling:
  578  when wind and some very great force of air, suddenly {{NOTE: anima here and at 586, 591 is air or breath, not the vital spirit of Book 3.}}
  579  arising either from outside or from the earth itself,
  580  has hurled itself into the hollow places of the earth, and there
  581  among the great caverns first roars with tumult
  582  and is carried whirling round, and then, when its force, driven on
  583  and harried, bursts out of doors, and at the same time, splitting the deep
  584  earth, it makes a great gaping chasm.
  585  This happened at Sidon in Syria, and was so at Aegium {{NOTE: Aegium in Achaea, struck by an earthquake in 373 BC, when nearby Helice sank into the sea.}}
  586  in the Peloponnese, cities which this outbreak of air
  587  and the earthquake that rose up threw into ruin.
  588  And many walls besides have fallen through great
  589  upheavals on land, and many cities down through the sea
  590  have settled to the bottom, together with their citizens.
  591  But if it does not break forth, still the very onset of the air
  592  and the fierce force of the wind is spread through the many holes of the earth
  593  like a shudder, and from there strikes in a trembling;
  594  as when cold comes deep into our limbs
  595  it shakes them against their will, forcing them to tremble and move.
  596  So through the cities people tremble with a twofold terror:
  597  they fear the roofs above, and they dread below lest the caverns
  598  of the earth be suddenly undone by nature,
  599  or lest, torn apart, it spread its gaping chasm wide
  600  and, thrown into confusion, seek to fill it with its own ruins.
  601  Therefore, however much they may think that heaven and earth
  602  will be incorruptible, entrusted to eternal safety,
  603  yet now and then the very present force of danger
  604  sets beneath them this goad of fear as well, from some quarter,
  605  lest the earth, snatched from under their feet, be swept away
  606  into the abyss, and the sum of things, betrayed, follow
  607  utterly, and a confused ruin of the world come to be.
