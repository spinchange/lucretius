  357  And in autumn above all the high house of heaven, bright with stars,
  358  is shaken on every side, and the whole earth with it,
  359  and when the flowering seasons of spring unfold themselves.
  360  For in the cold fires are lacking, and in the heat the winds
  361  fail, nor are the clouds then of so dense a body.
  362  When, therefore, the seasons of heaven stand between the two,
  363  then all the various causes of the thunderbolt run together.
  364  For the very strait of the year mixes cold with heat, {{NOTE: fretus anni, the "strait" or narrows where two seas meet and churn; Lucretius's figure for the turning of the seasons, explained at 6.374}}
  365  and a cloud needs both of these for the forging of thunderbolts,
  366  so that there is discord among things, and with a great tumult
  367  the air, raging with fires and winds, heaves like a sea.
  368  For the first part of heat is the last part of cold;
  369  that is the season of spring; therefore unlike things
  370  must fight with one another and, once mixed, throw all into turmoil.
  371  And when the last heat, mixed with the first cold,
  372  rolls round, the season that goes by the name of autumn,
  373  here too the bitter winters clash with the summers.
  374  Therefore these must be named the straits of the year,
  375  nor is it a wonder if at that time the most thunderbolts
  376  come about, and a turbulent storm is stirred up in the sky,
  377  since it is troubled on both sides by a two-fronted war,
  378  on this side by flames, on that by winds and moisture mixed.
  379  This is to see through the very nature of the fire-bearing
  380  thunderbolt, and to see by what force it does each thing,
  381  not to unroll the Tyrrhenian chants backward, to no purpose, {{NOTE: the Etruscan books of thunder-lore, used by the haruspices to read lightning as signs from the gods}}
  382  searching out the signs of the hidden mind of the gods:
  383  from what quarter the flying fire has come, or into which half
  384  it has turned from here, in what way through walled-in places
  385  it has slipped in, and how, having held sway, it has carried itself out,
  386  or what harm the stroke of the thunderbolt from heaven can do.
  387  But if Jupiter and the other gods shake the shining
  388  precincts of heaven with terrifying sound
  389  and hurl fire wherever each one has the will,
  390  why do they not make those who have some heedless, loathsome crime
  391  be struck, and breathe out the flames of lightning
  392  from a breast run through, a sharp lesson to mortals,
  393  and why instead does one conscious of nothing shameful
  394  roll in the flames, innocent, and find himself entangled,
  395  seized suddenly by the whirlwind and fire of heaven?
  396  Why, too, do they aim at empty places and labor in vain?
  397  Or are they then training their arms and hardening their muscles?
  398  And why do they let their father's weapon be blunted
  399  on the earth? Why does he himself allow it, not spare it for foes?
  400  Finally, why does Jupiter never, with the sky clear all round,
  401  hurl a thunderbolt on the earth and pour out its sounds?
  402  Or as soon as the clouds have come up, does he himself then
  403  go down into them, to aim his weapon's stroke from close at hand?
  404  By what principle, again, does he send it to sea? What charge
  405  has he against the waves, the liquid mass, the floating plains?
  406  Further, if he wants us to beware the thunderbolt's stroke,
  407  why does he hesitate to let us see it when it is sent?
  408  But if he wants to crush us with fire when we do not expect it,
  409  why does he thunder from that quarter, so that we can avoid it,
  410  why does he first stir up darkness and roarings and rumblings?
  411  And how could you believe that he sends it into many places
  412  at once? Or would you dare to argue that it has never happened
  413  that several strokes were made at one and the same time?
  414  But it has often happened, and must happen,
  415  that just as it rains and showers fall in many regions,
  416  so many thunderbolts come about at one time.
  417  Finally, why does he shatter the holy shrines of the gods
  418  and his own illustrious seats with hostile thunderbolt,
  419  and break the well-made images of the gods, and strip {{NOTE: simulacra here in the ordinary sense, statues of the gods, not the films of Book 4}}
  420  his own likenesses of their honor with a violent wound?
  421  And why does he mostly aim at high places, and why do we see
  422  the most traces of his fire on the tops of mountains?
  423  For the rest, it is easy to learn from these things
  424  how those which the Greeks, from what they are, named presters {{NOTE: prester, Greek "burner"; a fiery whirlwind or waterspout}}
  425  come down from above into the sea, by what principle.
  426  For it happens at times that a kind of column, let down
  427  from heaven, descends into the sea, and round it the waters
  428  boil, stirred up by heavily breathing blasts,
  429  and whatever ships are then caught in that tumult
  430  are harried and come into the utmost danger.
  431  This happens when at times the stirred-up force of the wind
  432  cannot burst the cloud it has begun on, but presses it down,
  433  so it is like a column let down from heaven into the sea,
  434  little by little, as if something were pushed by a fist
  435  and an arm's thrust from above, and stretched out into the waves;
  436  and when the wind has split it, from there its force bursts out
  437  into the sea and works a wondrous boiling in the waves;
  438  for a whirling eddy comes down and draws that cloud
  439  down together with it, a cloud of pliant body;
  440  and as soon as it has thrust it, heavy, down to the sea's levels,
  441  the eddy at once plunges whole into the water and stirs
  442  the whole sea with enormous sound, forcing it to boil.
  443  It happens too that the eddy of wind itself wraps itself
  444  in clouds, scraping together seeds of cloud from the air,
  445  and imitates a prester let down from heaven;
  446  when this has let itself down to the earth and broken up,
  447  it vomits out a monstrous force of whirlwind and storm.
  448  But because this happens only rarely, and the mountains must
  449  block it on land, the same thing is seen more often
  450  on the wide view of the sea and under the open sky.
  451  Clouds grow together when many bodies, flying
  452  up here in the space of heaven, have suddenly come together,
  453  rougher bodies, such as, though entangled in slight ways,
  454  can still be held together, pressed one upon another.
  455  These first make the small clouds take their shape;
  456  then these take hold of one another and flock together
  457  and by joining grow, and are carried by the winds,
  458  until at last a savage storm has risen.
  459  It happens too that mountain peaks, the nearer they are to heaven,
  460  the more do they smoke, lofty as they are,
  461  continually, with the thick murk of tawny cloud,
  462  because, when the clouds first come to take shape,
  463  before the eyes can see them, thin as they are, the winds
  464  carry them and drive them together to the mountain's topmost peaks;
  465  only here, when a greater throng has gathered,
  466  and condensed, can they appear, and at the same time
  467  they seem to rise from the mountain's very top into the clear sky.
  468  For that places high up lie open to the winds
  469  the fact itself shows, and our senses, when we climb high mountains.
