[DRAFT] (6.481) The "sign-bearing ether" (signifer aether) is the upper heaven that carries the zodiac, as at 1.2 ("the gliding signs of heaven"). Its heat, pressing down from above, is the second cause of cloud-formation here, after the vapors rising from sea, rivers, and earth.

[DRAFT] (6.485-488) Lucretius refers back to his proofs of infinite space and the speed of atoms (the infinity of the sum, the "profundum," is argued in Book 1; atomic speed in Book 2). The point is that bodies from outside our sky can pour in quickly, so sudden storms need not surprise.

[DRAFT] (6.494) "Elementis" here are the first bodies passing through the "pores" (caulas, spiracula) of the heavens, which he pictures as a porous boundary, not a solid shell. This is the "elementa" of the register; the letter-pun is not in play here.

[DRAFT] (6.497) "Semina aquai" (seeds of water) is the register's "seeds of things" used for the particular bodies that make up water. Lucretius holds that seeds of water rise together with those of the clouds from all things, which is why he compares the clouds' growth to the body growing along with blood and sweat.

[DRAFT] (6.526) The rainbow (arqui) is explained as sunlight shining against the opposing spray of the rain-clouds, producing a color in the dark cloud. Lucretius gives only this brief account here, without the optics later writers developed.

[DRAFT] (6.532-534) "Elementis reddita" (what is assigned or rendered to the elements) points back to the properties and powers of the first bodies set out in Books 1-2. Lucretius's claim is that once these are understood, snow, hail, ice and the rest need no separate explanation.

[DRAFT] (6.538) The Latin has "lucunas" (for lacunas), "pools" or hollows; the same word recurs at 6.552. It is a variant spelling, not a textual problem.

[DRAFT] (6.555) The text is doubtful at "inter aquas non quit constare, nisi umor": the translation follows the transmitted reading, "a vessel cannot stand steady among waters unless the liquid inside has ceased to be tossed." The conjecture "interdum" would instead give "sometimes cannot stand steady", which some editors prefer for sense.

[DRAFT] (6.565-567) The ignorant "men" (the ones who think heaven and earth incorruptible) are the target here and again at 6.601-607. Lucretius is turning a physical phenomenon into a lesson against fear of the world's end, though he himself holds that the world (mundus) is mortal (cf. Book 5), so their fear is mistaken in cause but not wholly in object.

[DRAFT] (6.576) "Perhilum" (very little, hardly at all) is a rare word, here marking the diminishing sway of the building from top to bottom. The line's tricolon (summa... media... ima) is a rhetorical flourish, not a doctrinal point.

[DRAFT] (6.578) "Anima" here (and at 586, 591) is air or breath, a blast of wind, not the vital "spirit" of Book 3. Where the register renders anima as "spirit," this passage deliberately departs and says "air" or "force of air"; the translator may wish to flag the shift at first occurrence.

[DRAFT] (6.582) The Latin text prints "versabundaQUE" with capital letters, a sign of a corrupt or doubtful reading in the source. The translation treats it as "versabunda... portatur" ("carried whirling round") and does not resolve the corruption.

[DRAFT] (6.585-587) The earthquakes at Sidon and Aegium are historical examples. Aegium in Achaea was struck in 373 BC, when nearby Helice sank into the sea; the Sidon event is less well attested. Note that "quas urbes" (which cities) is the grammatical object, with "exitus animai" and "terrae motus" as subjects.

[DRAFT] (6.601-607) The closing movement picks up the theme of fear that frames the whole book: even those who believe heaven and earth are secure feel at times the "goad" of terror that the earth may be pulled from beneath them. The final line ends the segment on "mundi confusa ruina," which echoes the "ruina" language of the book's other cosmic-collapse passages (cf. 5.95-109).
