 1138  A pattern of disease like this, and a death-bearing heat, once {{NOTE: ratio here is the "pattern" or "manner" of disease, not the poet's "reasoning".}}
 1139  made the fields deadly within the borders of Cecrops {{NOTE: Athens, whose legendary first king was Cecrops; the plague of 430 BC, told after Thucydides 2.47-54.}}
 1140  and laid waste the roads and drained the city of its citizens.
 1141  For coming from deep within, risen in the borders of Egypt,
 1142  having measured out much air and the swimming plains of the sea,
 1143  it fell at last upon the whole people of Pandion. {{NOTE: Pandion, a legendary king of Athens; his people are the Athenians.}}
 1144  From then on, in throngs, they were handed over to disease and death.
 1145  At first they carried their heads on fire with fever
 1146  and both their eyes red with a light suffused through them.
 1147  Further, the throat, black within, sweated
 1148  with blood, and the path of the voice, hedged with ulcers, closed,
 1149  and the tongue, the mind's interpreter, oozed gore,
 1150  weakened by its ills, heavy to move, rough to the touch.
 1151  Then, when through the throat it had filled the chest, and the very
 1152  force of the disease had flowed into the sick men's grieving heart,
 1153  then indeed all the barriers of life began to totter.
 1154  The breath rolled out from the mouth a foul stench,
 1155  the way rotten corpses stink when they are flung out.
 1156  And then the strength of the whole mind, and the whole
 1157  body, grew faint, now on the very threshold of death.
 1158  And with these intolerable ills an anxious anguish
 1159  was a constant companion, and complaint mixed with groaning,
 1160  and frequent retching, often through night and day,
 1161  forcing the sinews and limbs constantly to cramp,
 1162  broke them down, wearing them out, worn out already.
 1163  Nor could you see in anyone, with excessive heat,
 1164  the outermost part on the surface of the body burning,
 1165  but rather it offered a lukewarm touch to the hands,
 1166  and all the body at once reddened with ulcers as if burned in,
 1167  as it does when the sacred fire spreads through the limbs. {{NOTE: sacer ignis, probably erysipelas or a similar inflammation of the skin.}}
 1168  But the inmost part of these men blazed to the bones,
 1169  a flame blazed in the stomach as in furnaces within.
 1170  Nothing, then, however light and thin, could you turn
 1171  to use for anyone's limbs, but always wind and cold.
 1172  Some gave their limbs, burning with disease, to the icy
 1173  rivers, throwing their naked bodies into the waves.
 1174  Many plunged headlong, from high, into the nymphs of the wells, {{NOTE: nymphis putealibus, "the nymphs of the wells": the nymphs stand for the water.}}
 1175  coming at them with their mouths themselves gaping open:
 1176  thirst, not to be appeased, plunging their parched bodies in,
 1177  made a great flood of water no more than a few drops.
 1178  Nor was there any rest from the ill: their bodies lay
 1179  worn out. Medicine muttered in silent fear,
 1180  since so often, wide open and burning with disease,
 1181  they would roll the lights of their eyes, deprived of sleep.
 1182  And many signs of death besides were given then:
 1183  the mind's thought disordered in grief and fear,
 1184  the brow gloomy, the face frenzied and fierce,
 1185  the ears, further, troubled and full of sounds,
 1186  the breath quick, or else huge and drawn at long intervals,
 1187  and the glistening moisture of sweat soaking the neck,
 1188  spittle thin and in small flecks, tinged with the color of saffron
 1189  and salt, brought up through the throat with difficulty by a hoarse cough.
 1190  In the hands, too, the sinews would draw tight and the limbs tremble,
 1191  and from the feet the cold would climb little by little,
 1192  without fail. So too, finally, at the very last hour,
 1193  the nostrils pinched, the tip of the nose
 1194  sharp and thin, the eyes hollowed, the temples hollow, the skin cold
 1195  and hard, the mouth slack in a gaping grin, the brow stayed taut.
 1196  And not long after, their limbs lay stiff in death.
 1197  And about the eighth bright light of the sun,
 1198  or even at the ninth lamp of day, they gave back their life.
 1199  If any of them, as happens, had escaped death's funeral,
 1200  yet with foul ulcers and a black flux of the bowels
 1201  wasting and death awaited him later on,
 1202  or else, often with pain in the head, much
 1203  corrupted blood flowed out from the brimming nostrils.
 1204  Here the man's whole strength and body ran out.
 1205  Further, whoever had come through the harsh flood
 1206  of foul blood, still the disease went into his sinews and limbs
 1207  and into the very generative parts of the body.
 1208  And some, gravely fearing the threshold of death,
 1209  lived on, deprived by the knife of the male part,
 1210  and some, without hands and feet, remained
 1211  yet in life, and some lost the lights of their eyes.
 1212  So keen a fear of death had come upon them.
 1213  And some, too, were seized by forgetfulness of all
 1214  things, so that they could not even know themselves.
 1215  And though many bodies lay unburied on the ground, upon
 1216  bodies, yet the race of birds and of wild beasts
 1217  either leapt far off, to escape the sharp stench,
 1218  or, when it had tasted, grew faint with death close at hand.
 1219  Not that in those days any bird at all was readily
 1220  to be seen, nor did the gloomy generations of wild beasts
 1221  come out of the woods. Most of them grew faint with the disease
 1222  and died. Foremost, the faithful strength of dogs,
 1223  strewn in all the streets, gave up its spirit painfully;
 1224  for the force of the disease wrenched the life from their limbs.
 1225  Vast funerals, unattended, vied to be hurried away,
 1226  nor was any sure and common method of remedy given;
 1227  for what had granted some to roll the vital breezes of air
 1228  in their mouths, and to look upon the precincts of heaven,
 1229  this for others was destruction and prepared their death.
 1230  In these matters one thing above all was pitiable
 1231  and full of affliction: that each, as soon as he saw himself
 1232  caught in the disease, as though condemned to death,
 1233  failing in mind, lay there with grieving heart,
 1234  and, looking to funerals, gave up his spirit on the spot.
 1235  For indeed at no time did they cease to catch,
 1236  one from another, the contagion of the greedy disease,
 1237  just like woolly flocks and the horned generations of cattle,
 1238  and this above all heaped funeral upon funeral.
 1239  For whoever shunned visiting their own sick,
 1240  too greedy for life and fearful of death,
 1241  neglect punished a little later with a foul and evil death,
 1242  slaughtering them deserted, bereft of help.
 1243  But those who had stayed at hand went down by contagion
 1244  and by the labor that shame then compelled them to undergo,
 1245  and the coaxing voice of the weary, mixed with the voice of complaint.
 1246  And so the best of them, each one, met this kind of death.
 1247  Further, by now every shepherd and herdsman,
 1248  and likewise the sturdy guide of the curving plow,
 1249  grew faint, and deep in the hut the bodies lay crowded,
 1250  given over to death by poverty and disease.
 1251  Over lifeless children the lifeless bodies of parents
 1252  you might at times see, and again, the other way,
 1253  children giving up their life over mothers and fathers.
 1254  And not the least part of this grief flowed from the fields
 1255  into the city, brought by the faint multitude
 1256  of farmers, coming together sick from every quarter.
 1257  They filled all places and buildings; so all the more, in the heat,
 1258  death heaped them up in piles, crowded as they were.
 1259  Many bodies, laid low by thirst and rolled forward along the street,
 1260  lay stretched out at the fountain-spouts of the waters,
 1261  their spirit cut off by the too great sweetness of water,
 1262  and through the people's open places and streets everywhere
 1263  you could see faint limbs, with half-living bodies,
 1264  bristling with filth and covered with rags, perishing
 1265  in the body's foulness, skin alone upon their bones,
 1266  already almost buried in foul ulcers and dirt.
 1267  Death, finally, had filled all the holy shrines of the gods
 1268  with lifeless bodies, and everywhere, loaded
 1269  with corpses, stood all the precincts of the heavenly ones,
 1270  places their wardens had filled with guests.
 1271  For now neither the gods' religion nor their divine power
 1272  counted for much: the present pain overwhelmed them.
 1273  Nor did that custom of burial remain in the city
 1274  by which this people had always been buried before;
 1275  for the whole people, in confusion, was in alarm, and each
 1276  man, grieving, buried his own kin as the moment allowed.
 1277  And the sudden crisis and horrid poverty urged many things;
 1278  for on pyres built by others they would lay
 1279  their own blood-kin, with a huge outcry,
 1280  and set torches beneath, often with much bloodshed
 1281  brawling, rather than let the bodies be abandoned,
 1282  and, on top of others, a whole crowd of their own to bury
 1283  they strove; they came back worn out with tears and grief;
 1284  then a good part were given over to their beds through grief;
 1285  nor could anyone be found whom neither disease
 1286  nor death nor mourning assailed at such a time. {{NOTE: The poem ends here; whether Lucretius meant this as its close or left the work unfinished is disputed.}}
