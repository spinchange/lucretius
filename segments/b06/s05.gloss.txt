608-610: Latin: (men) mirantur [pres. ind., 3pl]; naturam non reddere mare maius [inf., negated]; quo sit tantus decursus aquarum [rel./whither, subj.]; quo veniant omnia flumina ex omni parte [subj.]. English: men wonder; nature does not make the sea larger; when so great is the downrush into it; when all rivers come to it from every side. MATCH

611-615: Latin: adde imbris, tempestates [imper.]; quae spargunt rigantque omnia maria ac terras [rel. to imbris/tempestates]; adde suos fontis; tamen omnia erunt vix instar unius guttai adaugmen ad summam maris [fut.]; quo minus est mirum mare magnum non augescere. English: add rains, storms; which sprinkle and drench; add its springs; yet all these will be scarcely a drop's increase; so it is less a wonder the great sea does not grow. MATCH

616: Latin: sol detrahit magnam partem aestu. English: the sun draws off a great part with its heat. MATCH

617-619: Latin: videmus solem exsiccare vestis madentis radiis; at videmus pelage multa et late substrata. English: we see clothes dried by the sun; and we see seas many and spread wide beneath it. MATCH (at softened to "and"; the contrast is still implied)

620-622: Latin: licet quamvis sol delibet parvam partem umoris ex uno quoque loco [concessive subj.]; tamen auferet largiter in tanto spatio [fut.]. English: however small a portion the sun may sip at any one place, yet over so great a space it will take much. MATCH

623-626: Latin: venti possunt tollere magnam partem umoris, verrentes aequora [ptcp. → venti]; quoniam videmus vias siccari ventis una nocte, crustas luti concrescere. English: the winds can lift, as they sweep the levels; since we see roads dried by the winds in one night, mud hardening. MATCH

627-630: Latin: docui [pf. 1sg] nubes quoque tollere multum umorem conceptum ex aequore ponti et spargere toto orbi; cum pluit et venti portant nubila. English: I have shown the clouds too lift much moisture, taken up from the sea, and scatter it over the lands, when it rains and winds carry clouds. MATCH

631-634: Latin: quoniam tellus est raro corpore et coniuncta, cingens oras maris; debet umor manare in terras ex aequore, ut venit umor de terris in mare. English: since the earth is porous and joined, girding the shores; it must be that, as moisture comes from land to sea, so it seeps into the lands from the salt level. MATCH

635-638: Latin: percolatur virus [pass.]; remanat materies umoris; omnis confluit ad caput amnibus; redit inde agmine dulci, qua via secta detulit undas. English: brine strained out; the matter of moisture oozes back; all gathers at the heads of the rivers; returns in a column of sweet water where the cut way carried the waves. MATCH

639-646: Latin: expediam [fut.] ratio quae sit, ut ignes expirent per fauces Aetnae turbine tanto; neque enim tempestas flammae, coorta non mediocri clade, dominata per agros, convertit ora gentium ad se; cum cernentes templa caeli fumida scintillare, complebant pectora pavida cura, quid moliretur rerum natura novarum. English: I shall unfold the principle by which fires breathe out; the storm of flame arose with no common disaster, held sway, turned the peoples' faces; when, seeing the precincts smoking and sparkling, they filled their hearts with fearful anxiety at what new things nature might be working. MATCH

647-652: Latin: videndum [gerundive] tibi late alteque, dispiciendum in cunctas partis; ut reminiscaris summam esse profundam et videas [subj., both under ut] quam parvula pars caelum unum sit summai totius, quam multesima constet, nec tota pars quota homo unus terrai totius. English: you must look widely and deeply and see far; that you may remember the sum is unfathomable, "and to see" how small a part one heaven is, not even as much as one man of the earth. DIFF: the second purpose verb (et videas) is rendered as a stray infinitive that does not join the "that you may" clause.

653-654: Latin: si plane contueare ac videas [subj.]; mirari multa relinquas [subj.]. English: if you set it before you and see it squarely, you would cease to wonder at many things. MATCH

655-657: Latin: numquis miratur, siquis accepit febrim coortam fervore aut alium dolorem? English: does any of us wonder if someone has taken a fever or other pain? MATCH

658-664: Latin: pes opturgescit; dolor arripit dentes, invadit oculos; sacer ignis existit et urit, serpens, quam arripuit partem, repit per artus; quia sunt semina multarum rerum et tellus caelumque fert satis morbi mali, unde queat vis morbi procrescere. English: foot swells; pain seizes teeth, invades eyes; sacred fire breaks out, burns, creeping, whatever part it seized, crawls; because there are seeds, earth and heaven bear enough of disease and harm for the force to grow. MATCH

665-672: Latin: putandum omnia ex infinito satis suppeditare toti caelo terraeque, unde queat tellus concussa moveri, turbo percurrere, ignis abundare, caelum flammescere; id quoque fit, templa ardescunt, tempestates pluviae sunt graviore coortu, ubi semina aquarum ita se tetulerunt. English: we must think all things are supplied from the infinite in plenty, so that the earth can be shaken, whirlwind run, Etna overflow, heaven burn; this happens, precincts burn, storms come with heavier gathering when the seeds have so assembled. MATCH

673: Latin: ardor incendi est nimis ingens. English: the blaze is too enormous. MATCH

674-679: Latin: fluvius visus maximus ei qui non vidit maiorem; arbor homoque videtur ingens; omnia maxima quae quisque vidit, haec ingentia fingit; cum omnia nil sint ad summam summai totius. English: a river seems greatest to him who has not seen greater; a tree or man seems enormous; the greatest each has seen he imagines enormous; though all are nothing to the sum of the whole sum. MATCH

680-683: Latin: expediam quibus modis flamma, inritata repente, efflet ex fornacibus; natura montis est subcava, fere suffulta cavernis silicum. English: I shall unfold how the flame, suddenly roused, blows out; the mountain is hollow beneath, held up for the most part on caverns of flint. MATCH

684-685: Latin: est ventus et aer in speluncis; ventus fit ubi aer est percitus agitando. English: there is wind and air in the caves; wind arises when air is stirred. MATCH

686-689: Latin: hic [ventus] ubi percaluit calefecitque saxa terramque, furens, et excussit ignem calidum ab ollis; tollit se ac eicit alte rectis faucibus. English: when this has grown hot, heated rocks and earth, raging, and struck out fire from them; it rises and flings itself out through the jaws. MATCH

690-693: Latin: fert ardorem, differt favillam, volvit fumum, extrudit saxa; ne dubites quin haec sit animai turbida vis. English: carries heat, scatters ash, rolls smoke, thrusts out rocks; so you need not doubt this is the stormy force of air. MATCH

694-695: Latin: mare magna ex parti frangit fluctus ad radices montis aestumque resolvit. English: along a great part of its length the sea breaks its waves at the roots and draws back its surge. MATCH

696-700: Latin: speluncae perveniunt ex hoc mari subter ad fauces; hac ire fatendumst [lacuna]; res cogit penetrare penitus mari aperto, efflare, extollere flammam, subiectare saxa, tollere nimbos arenae. English: caves reach beneath to the jaws; by this way we must admit there passes [lacuna]; the fact compels that it pierces from the open sea, breathes out, lifts flame, throws up rocks, raises sand. MATCH

701-702: Latin: sunt crateres in summo vertice, ut ipsi nominitant, quod nos fauces et ora perhibemus. English: there are craters, as they name them, what we call jaws and mouths. MATCH

703-707: Latin: sunt res quarum non satis est dicere unam causam, verum pluris, unde una tamen sit; ut, si conspicias corpus hominis iacere, fit ut conveniat dicere omnis causas, dicatur ut una illius. English: for some things one cause is not enough but several, though one is the true one; just as, if you see a body, it is fitting to name all causes so that the one is named. MATCH

708-711: Latin: neque possis vincere eum interiisse ferro, frigore, morbo, veneno; verum scimus aliquid ex hoc genere esse quod contigit ei; item in multis hoc dicere habemus. English: you could not prove he died by steel, cold, disease, poison; but we know something of this kind befell him; so we can say the same in many matters. MATCH

712-713: Latin: Nilus crescit, redundat, unicus, amnis Aegypti. English: same. MATCH

714-718: Latin: is rigat Aegyptum per calorem; aut quia aquilones sunt contra ostia aestate, eo tempore, qui etesiae esse feruntur; et flantes contra fluvium remorantur, cogentes undas sursus replent coguntque manere. English: it waters Egypt in the heat; either because the north winds blow against its mouths, when they are said to be the Etesians; blowing against the stream they hold it back, drive the waters upward, fill it, force it to stay. MATCH

719-723: Latin: flabra feruntur adverso flumine, quae aguntur ab stellis axis; ille amnis venit ex aestifera parti ab austro, inter saecla virum, exoriens a regione diei. English: these blasts are carried against the stream, driven from the stars of the pole; the river comes from the heat-bearing quarter, among men, rising from the region of midday. MATCH

724-726: Latin: est uti possit congestus harenae oppilare ostia contra fluctibus adversis [abl.: by/with the opposing waves]; cum mare permotum ventis ruit harenam intus. English: a heap of sand may block the mouths "against the opposing waves"; when the sea sweeps sand inward. DIFF: the ablative of the waves that pile the bar is turned into the thing the bar blocks against.

727-728: Latin: quo pacto fit uti exitus amnis minus liber, impetus undis minus proclivis fiat. English: by this means the outlet is less free and the waters have less downhill onrush. MATCH

729-734: Latin: fit uti pluviae magis fiant ad caput ei eo tempore quo flabra aquilonum coniciunt nubila in eas partis; cum eiecta convenerunt ad regionem diei, nubes contrusae ad montis coguntur, premuntur. English: more rains fall at its head when the Etesian blasts drive clouds there; when driven to the region of midday and come together, the clouds are crowded and pressed. MATCH

735-737: Latin: forsitan crescat de montibus Aethiopum, ubi sol subigit ningues descendere radiis. English: perhaps it grows from the mountains of the Ethiopians, where the sun forces the snows to come down. MATCH
