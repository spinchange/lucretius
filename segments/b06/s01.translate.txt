  121  In this way too all things, shaken by heavy thunder, are often
  122  seen to tremble, and, suddenly torn apart,
  123  the vast walls of the capacious world seem to have leapt asunder,
  124  when all at once a gathered storm of strong wind
  125  has twisted itself into the clouds and, shut in there,
  126  with its whirling eddy more and more on every side
  127  compels the cloud to grow hollow, its body thickened all around;
  128  afterwards, when the wind's force and keen onset have broken it,
  129  then, split, it gives a crash with a terrifying crackling sound. {{NOTE: perterricrepo, a Lucretian coinage, "terror-crackling"}}
  130  Nor is this a wonder, when a little bladder full of air
  131  often gives no little sound when it is suddenly burst.
  132  There is also a principle by which, when winds blow through the clouds,
  133  they make sounds; for indeed we see clouds, branching
  134  and rough, often borne along in many ways;
  135  just as, when the blasts of the northwest wind blow through a dense wood, {{NOTE: cauri, Caurus, the northwest wind}}
  136  the leaves give a sound and the branches a crash.
  137  It happens too at times that the roused force of a strong wind
  138  tears through the cloud, breaking it with a straight onset;
  139  for what a blast can do there the plain fact shows us
  140  here on earth, where it is gentler, when even so it overturns tall
  141  trees and wrenches them out from their deepest roots.
  142  There are also waves through the clouds, which give something like a murmur,
  143  heavily, as they break; as happens likewise in deep
  144  rivers and on the great sea, when the surf is broken.
  145  It happens too when from cloud into cloud the burning force
  146  of the thunderbolt falls; if this cloud by chance receives the fire
  147  in much moisture, it slaughters it at once with a great outcry;
  148  as iron glowing white from the hot furnaces at times
  149  hisses, when we have plunged it into icy water close by.
  150  Again, if a drier cloud receives the fire,
  151  it is kindled all at once and burns with a huge sound,
  152  as if flame were to roam through mountains leafy with laurel,
  153  burning them up with great onset in a whirlwind of winds;
  154  nor does anything, more than the Delphic laurel of Phoebus, {{NOTE: Phoebus is Apollo; the laurel was his sacred tree at Delphi}}
  155  burn with terrible sound in the crackling flame.
  156  Finally, often a great crashing of ice and a downfall
  157  of hail gives a sound high up in great clouds;
  158  for when the wind crams them together, there are broken, pressed into a narrow space,
  159  the frozen mountains of storm-cloud, mixed with hail.
  160  It lightens likewise when the clouds by their collision have struck out
  161  many seeds of fire, as if stone should strike
  162  on stone, or on iron; for then too a light
  163  leaps out and fire scatters bright sparks.
  164  But it happens that we receive the thunder with our ears after
  165  the eyes see the lightning, because always to the ears
  166  things arrive more slowly than the things that move the sight.
  167  You may learn this from this too: if you see someone
  168  far off cutting down the growth of a tree with a double-edged axe,
  169  it happens that you see the stroke before the blow through the ears
  170  gives its sound; so too we see the lightning before
  171  we receive the thunder, which is sent out together with the fire
  172  from a like cause, born of the same collision.
  173  In this way too clouds dye places with swift light
  174  and the storm lightens with a trembling onset.
  175  When wind has invaded a cloud and, turning about there,
  176  has made the cloud, as I showed before, grow hollow and thick,
  177  it grows hot by its own mobility; as you see all things,
  178  heated through by motion, take fire; indeed a leaden
  179  sling-bullet, whirled along a long course, even melts.
  180  Therefore when this wind, hot, has cut through the black cloud,
  181  it scatters the seeds of heat, squeezed out as if by force all at once,
  182  which make the flickering lightnings of flame;
  183  then the sound follows, which reaches our ears more slowly
  184  than the things that arrive at the lights of our eyes.
  185  This happens, of course, when clouds are dense and at the same time
  186  piled up high, one upon another, with wondrous onset.
  187  Do not let it deceive you that we, below, see
  188  how broad they are rather than how high they stand piled up.
  189  For look, when the winds carry clouds like mountains
  190  crosswise through the air,
  191  or when you see them heaped along great mountains,
  192  one above another, and the higher pressing down,
  193  set at rest in their station, the winds buried on every side;
  194  then you will be able to recognize their great masses
  195  and to discern caverns built as if of hanging rocks,
  196  which, when a storm has risen, the winds
  197  have filled, and, shut in, they chafe with a great murmur
  198  in the clouds, and threaten like wild beasts in cages;
  199  now from here, now from there, they send roars through the clouds,
  200  and, seeking a way out, they turn about and roll together
  201  seeds of fire out of the clouds, and so drive together
  202  many, and whirl the flame within the hollow furnaces,
  203  until the cloud is torn apart and they have flashed out, gleaming.
  204  For this cause too it happens that that swift
  205  golden color of liquid fire flies down to earth,
  206  because the clouds themselves must have very many seeds
  207  of fire; for indeed when they are without any moisture,
  208  their color is mostly flaming and bright.
  209  For indeed from the light of the sun they must
  210  take in much, so that with reason they glow red and pour out fires.
  211  When therefore the wind, driving these, has thrust them into one
  212  place and compressed them, crowding them, they pour out, squeezed,
  213  the seeds that make the colors of flame flash out.
  214  It lightens also when the clouds of heaven grow thin;
  215  for when the wind lightly draws them apart as they go
  216  and dissolves them, there must fall, against their will,
  217  the seeds that make the flash. Then without foul
  218  terror and sounds it lightens, and with no uproar.
  219  For the rest, with what nature thunderbolts are endowed
  220  is declared by their strokes, and by the burnt-in signs of heat
  221  and the marks breathing out heavy airs of sulphur;
  222  for these are signs of fire, not of wind or of rain.
  223  Further, they often set fire too to the roofs of houses
  224  and with swift flame are masters in the very dwellings.
  225  This fire, subtle above all other fires,
  226  nature has composed for you of tiny and mobile
  227  bodies, which nothing at all can withstand.
  228  For the strong thunderbolt passes through the walls of houses
  229  as shouts and voices do, passes through stones, through bronze,
  230  and in an instant of time makes bronze and gold liquid.
  231  Likewise it makes wine, though the jars are whole, suddenly
  232  vanish away, because, no doubt, easily all around
  233  it loosens and makes porous the sides of the jar,
  234  its heat, as it arrives, and, working its way into the wine itself,
  235  dissolving nimbly, scatters the first-beginnings of the wine.
  236  And this the heat of the sun seems unable, in a lifetime,
  237  to accomplish, strong as it is with its flashing glow.
  238  So much more mobile and more masterful is this force.
  239  Now, by what means these are begotten, and are made with so great
  240  an onset that by their stroke they can split towers apart,
  241  overthrow houses, tear away beams and rafters,
  242  and heave up and shift the monuments of men,
  243  take the life from men, lay low the herds everywhere,
  244  by what force they can do all other things of this kind,
  245  I shall unfold, and not delay you longer with promises.
