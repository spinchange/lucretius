  246  Thunderbolts, we must suppose, are born from thick clouds
  247  heaped up on high; for from a clear sky none,
  248  nor from clouds of slight density, are ever sent.
  249  For beyond doubt the plain fact shows that this is so:
  250  that at such times the clouds grow dense through the whole air, {{NOTE: source text garbled here (and at 253, 254, 304, 346) by an encoding fault; read as aëra, usque, superne, aëre}}
  251  so that we would think all the darkness on every side had left
  252  Acheron and filled the great caverns of the sky;
  253  so far, when the foul night of storm-clouds has risen,
  254  do faces of black terror hang over us from above,
  255  when the tempest begins to work up its thunderbolts.
  256  Further, very often a black storm-cloud out over the sea,
  257  like a river of pitch let down from the sky, falls
  258  upon the waves, crammed with darkness, far off, and drags
  259  a black tempest heavy with thunderbolts and gales,
  260  itself filled full, above all, with fires and winds,
  261  so that on land too men shudder and seek shelter.
  262  So, then, we must suppose that above our heads
  263  the tempest towers high; for they would not bury the lands
  264  in so great a gloom unless many clouds were built up
  265  on many others above, and the sun taken away;
  266  nor could they overwhelm the earth, coming, with so great a rain
  267  as to make the rivers overflow and the fields swim,
  268  if the upper air were not built high with heaped-up clouds.
  269  Here, then, all things are full of winds and fires;
  270  that is why on every side come roarings and flashes.
  271  For indeed I have shown above that the hollow clouds hold
  272  very many seeds of heat, and must take in many more
  273  from the rays of the sun and from their burning.
  274  When the same wind that drives them together into one
  275  place, whatever it may be, has pressed out many seeds
  276  of heat, and at once has mingled itself with that fire,
  277  a whirlwind, worked in there, spins in the narrow space
  278  and sharpens the thunderbolt in hot furnaces within;
  279  for it is kindled in two ways: it grows hot by its own
  280  mobility, and from its contact with the fire.
  281  Then, when the force of the wind has grown hot through and the heavy
  282  onset of fire has come in, the thunderbolt, ripe as it were,
  283  suddenly tears through the cloud, and the roused blaze is carried
  284  on, lighting up all places with its flashing lights.
  285  After it follows a heavy sound, so that the precincts of heaven
  286  seem to burst apart all at once and crush us from above.
  287  Then a trembling heavily assails the lands, and rumblings
  288  run across the high sky; for at that time nearly the whole
  289  tempest is shaken and trembles, and roarings are set moving.
  290  From this shaking follows a heavy and abundant rain,
  291  so that the whole upper air seems to be turned to rain
  292  and, plunging headlong so, to call us back to the flood;
  293  so great a downpour is sent by the rending of the cloud
  294  and the storm of the wind, when the sound flies out with the burning stroke.
  295  There are times, too, when the force of wind, roused from outside,
  296  falls upon a strong cloud when its thunderbolt is ripe;
  297  and when it has torn through it, at once there falls that fiery
  298  whirl which by our native name we call the thunderbolt. {{NOTE: "patrio nomine": the Latin word fulmen, as against the Greek keraunos}}
  299  The same happens toward other parts, wherever the force has carried it.
  300  It happens too that at times the force of wind, sent without fire,
  301  nonetheless catches fire in the space of its long passage,
  302  while, as it comes, it loses along its course certain bodies,
  303  large ones, which cannot pass through the air as readily,
  304  and, scraping up others from the air itself, carries
  305  tiny ones, which, mixed together, make fire as they fly;
  306  in a way not far different from how a lead
  307  bullet often grows hot in its course, when, shedding many {{NOTE: glans, the lead sling-bullet}}
  308  bodies of cold, it has taken fire in the air.
  309  It happens too that the force of the blow itself rouses fire,
  310  when the force of wind, sent cold and without fire, has struck,
  311  doubtless because, when it has struck with a violent stroke,
  312  elements of heat can flow together out of the wind itself
  313  and at the same time out of the thing that then receives the stroke;
  314  as, when we strike a stone with iron, fire flies out,
  315  nor, because the force of iron is cold, do the seeds
  316  of hot brightness rush together any the less at the stroke.
  317  So, then, a thing must also be kindled by the thunderbolt
  318  if it happens to be suited and fit for flames.
  319  Nor can the force of the wind be wholly and utterly cold,
  320  that force which is sent from on high with so much power,
  321  but that, if it is not kindled with fire before, along its course,
  322  it still arrives warmed and mingled with heat.
  323  Further, the mobility of the thunderbolt and its heavy stroke come about,
  324  and thunderbolts commonly run their course with swift gliding,
  325  because the force itself, first roused, gathers itself wholly
  326  within the clouds and takes on a great effort for going,
  327  then, when the cloud can no longer hold the growing onset,
  328  the force is squeezed out and so flies with wondrous onset,
  329  like the missiles that are carried, sent from mighty engines of war.
  330  Add that it is made of small and smooth elements,
  331  and it is not easy for anything to withstand such a nature;
  332  for it slips between and pierces through the loose-knit passages,
  333  and so is not held back by many collisions
  334  and delayed; for this reason it flies, gliding with swift onset.
  335  Then too, since by nature all weights strive
  336  downward, when a blow is added to this as well,
  337  the mobility is doubled and that onset grows heavier,
  338  so that more violently and more swiftly it shatters with blows
  339  whatever stands in its way and delays it, and keeps its path.
  340  Finally, since it comes with a long onset, it must take on
  341  mobility again and again, which grows as it goes
  342  and increases its strong powers and gives strength to its stroke;
  343  for it makes whatever seeds of it there are
  344  be carried all straight on, as it were into one place,
  345  throwing them all, as they roll, into that one course.
  346  Perhaps too, as it comes, it draws from the air itself certain
  347  bodies, which by their blows kindle its mobility.
  348  And it comes unharmed through things, and passes through many
  349  leaving them whole, because the liquid fire passes through their pores.
  350  And many it shatters, when the very bodies of the thunderbolt
  351  have fallen on the bodies of things where their texture holds together.
  352  Further, it easily dissolves bronze, and in an instant makes gold
  353  boil, because its force is made finely of small
  354  bodies and of smooth elements,
  355  which easily work their way in, and, once worked in, suddenly
  356  dissolve all knots and loosen the bonds.
