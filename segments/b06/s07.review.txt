  879  Cold too is the spring above which tow, when set there, often
  880  throws out a flame, catching fire at once,
  881  and a pine torch, lit on a like principle, shines on the waves
  882  wherever, floating, it is driven along by the breezes.
  883  No wonder: because in the water there are very many seeds
  884  of heat, and from the earth itself, from deep below, it must be
  885  that bodies of fire rise up through the whole spring
  886  and at the same time breathe out and go forth into the breezes,
  887  yet not so many that the spring could become hot;
  888  further, a force compels them, scattered, to break out
  889  suddenly through the water and to gather together above.
  890  Of this kind in the sea at Aradus is the spring of fresh {{NOTE: Aradus, an island city off the Phoenician coast, known for a freshwater spring rising from the seabed.}}
  891  water, that gushes and pushes away the salt waves round it;
  892  and in many other regions too the level sea offers
  893  a timely service to thirsty sailors,
  894  in that it spews up fresh water among the salt waves.
  895  So, then, through that spring those seeds can break out
  896  and bubble forth; and when they come together in the tow
  897  or cling to the body of the torch,
  898  they easily blaze up at once, because the tow and torches
  899  also hold in themselves many seeds of fire.
  900  Do you not see, too, that when you bring a wick, just now
  901  put out, close to the lights at night, it catches fire before
  902  it has touched the flame, and a torch on a like principle?
  903  And many other things, further, touched first by the heat itself,
  904  blaze up at a distance before fire soaks them from close by.
  905  This, then, we must think also happens in that spring.
  906  For the rest, I shall begin to set out by what covenant
  907  of nature it comes about that this stone can draw iron,
  908  which the Greeks call the Magnes, from its native name, {{NOTE: Magnes lithos, the Magnesian stone, our "magnet"; Lucretius keeps the Greek name, lapis Magnes (6.1046)}}
  909  because it arose within the native bounds of the Magnetes. {{NOTE: The Magnetes, people of Magnesia (in Thessaly, or Magnesia in Lydia); hence "magnet".}}
  910  Men marvel at this stone; for it often makes
  911  a chain out of rings hanging down from itself.
  912  For indeed you may see at times five or more
  913  in a hanging line, swung about by the light breezes,
  914  where one hangs down from another, clinging underneath,
  915  and each from the next comes to know the stone's force and bonds;
  916  so pervasively does its force prevail throughout.
  917  In matters of this kind many things must be established before
  918  you can give a reasoning of the thing itself,
  919  and it must be approached by very long windings;
  920  so all the more I ask of you attentive ears and mind.
  921  First, from all the things we see, whatever they are, {{NOTE: 6.921-935 closely repeat 4.216-229; the English repeats its Book 4 lines where the Latin agrees}}
  922  bodies must ceaselessly flow and be scattered, and they
  923  are sent out that strike the eyes and provoke the sight.
  924  And from certain things odors flow perpetually;
  925  as cold from rivers, heat from the sun, surge from the waves
  926  of the sea, eater of walls along the shores,
  927  nor do various sounds cease to stream through the air;
  928  finally, a moisture of salty flavor often comes into the mouth
  929  when we walk beside the sea, and when we watch
  930  wormwood being diluted and mixed, its bitterness touches us.
  931  So true is it that from all things each thing is carried off
  932  in a stream, and sent away in all directions on every side,
  933  and no delay, no rest is granted in the flowing,
  934  since we perpetually feel, and may always
  935  see all things, smell them, and sense them sounding.
  936  Now I shall go back to recall how porous of body
  937  all things are; which is clear also in my first song. {{NOTE: Book 1, esp. 1.346-357, on water, food, sound, and cold passing through solid things.}}
  938  For indeed, although to know this bears on many matters,
  939  among the first, for this very matter now at hand,
  940  which I set about discussing, it must be established
  941  that nothing is in view but body mixed with void.
  942  First, it happens that in caves the rocks above
  943  sweat with moisture and drip with trickling drops.
  944  Likewise sweat streams out from all our body,
  945  the beard grows, and hairs through all our members, through our limbs.
  946  All food is spread into the veins, and increases and feeds
  947  even the furthest parts of the body and the little nails.
  948  Likewise we feel cold and warm heat pass through bronze,
  949  we feel them likewise pass through gold
  950  and through silver, when we hold full cups.
  951  Finally, through the stone partitions of houses voices
  952  fly through, smell streams through, and cold, and the heat
  953  of fire, which is used to piercing even the strength of iron,
  954  finally where the breastplate of the sky encloses all around, {{NOTE: the "breastplate of the sky" is the outer wall of the world; the line is suspect and its connection uncertain.}}
  955  and the force of disease as well, when it creeps in from outside;
  956  and storms that have arisen in earth and sky
  957  withdraw, far off, into sky and earth, as is right; {{NOTE: 956-957 text and sense uncertain; rendered as Bailey's text stands.}}
  958  since nothing is put together except with porous body.
  959  Add to this that not all the bodies, whatever they are, thrown off
  960  from things are endowed with the same effect on sense
  961  and fitted in the same way to all things.
  962  First, the sun bakes the earth and makes it parched,
  963  but it melts the ice, and on the high mountains forces the high
  964  piled-up snows to waste away under its rays;
  965  finally, wax set in its heat becomes liquid.
  966  Fire likewise makes bronze liquid and dissolves gold,
  967  but hides and flesh it draws and contracts into one.
  968  The moisture of water, again, hardens iron from the fire,
  969  but softens hides and flesh hardened by heat.
  970  The wild olive so delights the bearded she-goats,
  971  as if it truly flowed with ambrosia and were steeped in nectar,
  972  though for man nothing grows more bitter than its sour leaf.
  973  Finally, the pig flees marjoram oil and fears every
  974  ointment; for to bristly pigs it is a sharp poison,
  975  though to us it sometimes seems, as it were, to give new life.
  976  But on the other hand, though mud to us is the foulest
  977  filth, to pigs this same thing seems delightful,
  978  so that they roll in it, whole, insatiably.
  979  This also remains, before I set about to speak
  980  of the thing itself, a point that seems to need saying first.
  981  Since many pores have been given to various things,
  982  they must be endowed with natures unlike one another
  983  and each must have its own nature and its own paths.
  984  For indeed living creatures have various senses in them,
  985  each of which takes in its own proper object;
  986  for we see sounds penetrate by one way, by another flavor
  987  from juices, by another the scents of reek.
  988  Clearly it is the nature of the paths that compels this,
  989  varying in many ways, as we showed a little before.
  990  Further, one thing seems to stream through rocks,
  991  another through wood, another to pass through gold,
  992  and another to make its way out through silver and glass;
  993  for here appearances are seen to flow, there heat to go, {{NOTE: species here = the visible appearances (the images) that pass through glass.}}
  994  and the same things to let one thing through more quickly than others.
  995  Clearly it is the nature of the paths that compels this,
  996  varying in many ways, as we showed a little before,
  997  because of the unlike nature and texture of things.
  998  Therefore, when these things have been well confirmed and laid down,
  999  all standing ready for us, set out beforehand,
 1000  for the rest, from here the reasoning comes easily, and the whole
 1001  cause laid open, that lures the force of iron.
