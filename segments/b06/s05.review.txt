  608  First, men wonder that nature does not make the sea larger,
  609  when so great is the downrush of waters into it,
  610  when all the rivers come to it from every side.
  611  Add the wandering rains and the flying storms
  612  which sprinkle and drench all seas and lands;
  613  add its own springs: yet all these, against the sum of the sea,
  614  will be scarcely the increase of a single drop;
  615  so it is less a wonder that the great sea does not grow.
  616  Further, the sun draws off a great part with its heat.
  617  For indeed we see that clothes dripping with moisture
  618  are dried out by the sun with its burning rays;
  619  and we see the seas are many and spread wide beneath it.
  620  Therefore, however small a portion of moisture the sun
  621  may sip from the level sea at any one place,
  622  yet over so great a space it will take much from the waves.
  623  Then again the winds too can lift a great part
  624  of moisture as they sweep the levels, since very often
  625  we see the roads dried by the winds in a single night
  626  and soft mud hardening into crusts.
  627  Further, I have shown that the clouds too lift much {{NOTE: refers back to 6.470-482, where clouds draw moisture from the sea}}
  628  moisture, taken up from the great level of the sea,
  629  and scatter it everywhere over the whole round of lands,
  630  when it rains on the earth and the winds carry the clouds.
  631  Finally, since the earth is of a porous body
  632  and is joined to the sea, girding its shores on every side,
  633  it must be that, as water's moisture comes from land into sea,
  634  so it seeps likewise into the lands from the salt level;
  635  for the brine is strained out, and the matter of moisture {{NOTE: virus, the sea's bitter salt, filtered out as the water passes through the earth}}
  636  oozes back, and all of it gathers at the heads of the rivers,
  637  and from there returns over the lands, a column of sweet water,
  638  where the way, once cut, has carried the waves down with liquid foot.
  639  Now what the principle is by which, through the jaws of Mount Etna,
  640  fires at times breathe out in so great a whirlwind
  641  I shall unfold; for with no common disaster did the storm
  642  of flame arise and, holding sway over the Sicilian fields,
  643  turned the faces of the neighboring peoples toward itself,
  644  when, seeing all the precincts of heaven smoking and sparkling,
  645  they filled their hearts with fearful anxiety
  646  at what new things nature might be working. {{NOTE: rerum ... novarum, "new things", also the Roman idiom for revolution; the words echo rerum natura}}
  647  In these matters you must look widely and deeply
  648  and see far out into every quarter,
  649  that you may remember the sum of things is unfathomable,
  650  and may see that one heaven, of the whole sum,
  651  is how small a part, how minute a fraction it makes,
  652  not even so great a part as one man is of the whole earth.
  653  If you set this well before you and look at it squarely,
  654  and see it squarely, you would cease to wonder at many things.
  655  For does any of us wonder if someone has taken
  656  into his limbs a fever arising with burning heat,
  657  or any other pain of disease through the body?
  658  For suddenly the foot swells; often a sharp pain
  659  seizes the teeth, invades the very eyes;
  660  the sacred fire breaks out and, creeping in the body, burns {{NOTE: sacer ignis, a spreading skin inflammation, probably erysipelas or shingles}}
  661  whatever part it has seized, and crawls through the limbs,
  662  no doubt because there are seeds of many things,
  663  and this earth and heaven bear enough of disease and harm
  664  for the force of measureless disease to grow up from them.
  665  So, then, we must think that to the whole of heaven and earth
  666  all things are supplied from the infinite in plenty,
  667  so that on a sudden the earth can be shaken and moved,
  668  and a rapid whirlwind run across sea and lands,
  669  Etna's fire overflow, and the heaven burst into flame;
  670  for this too happens, and the precincts of heaven burn,
  671  and storms of rain come with a heavier gathering
  672  when by chance the seeds of waters have so assembled.
  673  "But the stormy blaze of that fire is too enormous."
  674  Of course; and a river seems greatest to the man
  675  who has never before seen a greater, and enormous
  676  a tree or a man seems, and all things of every kind
  677  the greatest each has seen, he imagines are enormous,
  678  though all things, with heaven and earth and sea,
  679  are nothing to the entire sum of the whole sum.
  680  Now, however, in what ways that flame, suddenly roused,
  681  blows out from the vast furnaces of Etna
  682  I shall unfold. First, the nature of the whole mountain
  683  is hollow beneath, held up for the most part on caverns of flint.
  684  Further, in all the caves there is wind and air. {{NOTE: the source prints a doubled form of aer here and at 685, where the TEI marks a diaeresis; the plain word is read}}
  685  For wind arises when air is stirred and set in motion.
  686  When this has grown hot through and through and, raging, has heated
  687  all the rocks around, where it touches, and the earth, and from them
  688  has struck out fire, hot with swift flames,
  689  it rises and so flings itself out on high through the straight jaws.
  690  And so it carries its heat far, and far it scatters
  691  the ash, and rolls smoke in thick darkness,
  692  and thrusts out at the same time rocks of wondrous weight;
  693  so you need not doubt that this is the stormy force of air. {{NOTE: animai here is breath or wind, not the spirit of Book 3}}
  694  Further, along a great part of its length the sea breaks its waves
  695  at the roots of that mountain and draws back its surge.
  696  From this sea, caves reach beneath to the high jaws
  697  of the mountain. By this way, we must admit, there passes
       [lacuna]
  698  and the fact compels, that it pierces deep from the open sea
  699  and breathes out, and therefore lifts the flame
  700  and throws up rocks and raises clouds of sand.
  701  For on the topmost summit there are craters, as they themselves {{NOTE: krateres, Greek "mixing-bowls"; "they" are the Greeks of Sicily}}
  702  give them the name, what we call jaws and mouths.
  703  There are some things, too, for which to state one cause
  704  is not enough, but several, though one of them is the true one;
  705  just as, if you yourself should see the lifeless body
  706  of a man lying far off, it is fitting to name all
  707  the causes of death, so that the one cause of his death is named;
  708  for you could not prove that he died by steel
  709  or by cold, or by disease, or perhaps by poison,
  710  but we know that what befell him is something
  711  of this kind. So we can say the same in many matters.
  712  The Nile rises toward summer and overflows the plains,
  713  unique on earth, the river of all Egypt.
  714  It waters Egypt often in the middle of the heat,
  715  either because in summer the north winds blow against its mouths,
  716  at that time of year when they are said to be the Etesians, {{NOTE: the Etesian winds, steady northerlies of the Mediterranean summer}}
  717  and blowing against the stream hold it back, and driving
  718  the waters upward fill it and force it to stay.
  719  For beyond doubt these blasts are carried against
  720  the stream, blasts driven from the chill stars of the pole;
  721  that river comes from the heat-bearing quarter, from the south,
  722  among the black generations of men, their color baked through,
  723  rising far within, from the region of midday. {{NOTE: "the region of midday" is the south, where the sun stands at noon}}
  724  It may be, too, that a great heap of sand
  725  blocks up the mouths against the opposing waves,
  726  when the sea, stirred up by winds, sweeps sand inward;
  727  by this means it happens that the river's outlet is less free
  728  and the waters likewise have less downhill onrush.
  729  It happens too, perhaps, that more rains fall at its head
  730  at that time when the Etesian blasts of the north winds
  731  drive all the clouds into those regions.
  732  Of course, when they have been driven toward the region of midday
  733  and have come together there, at last, against the high mountains,
  734  the clouds are crowded together and pressed by force.
  735  Perhaps it grows from deep in the high mountains of the Ethiopians,
  736  where the sun, surveying all things, forces the white snows
  737  to come down into the plains with its melting rays.
