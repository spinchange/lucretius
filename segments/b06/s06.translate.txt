  738  Come now: whatever the Avernian places and lakes may be,
  739  I will explain to you with what nature they are endowed.
  740  First, the name by which they are called, Avernian, was given {{NOTE: Lucretius takes the name from Greek aornos, "birdless".}}
  741  from the fact itself, because they are hostile to all birds:
  742  when birds in their flight have come over those places,
  743  they forget the oarage of their wings, they slacken their sails,
  744  and fall headlong, their soft necks drooping, stretched out
  745  on the ground, if the nature of the places happens to bring it so,
  746  or into the water, if a lake of Avernus happens to lie beneath.
  747  Such a place is near Cumae, where the mountains, filled {{NOTE: Lake Avernus, near Cumae on the Bay of Naples, was the traditional entrance to the underworld.}}
  748  with pungent sulphur, smoke, enriched with hot springs.
  749  There is one also within the walls of Athens, on the very
  750  summit of the citadel, by the temple of nurturing Pallas, the Tritonian,
  751  where the hoarse ones never bring their bodies on the wing,
  752  the crows, not even when the altars smoke with offerings;
  753  so constantly do they shun it, not fleeing the fierce anger of Pallas
  754  for their watchfulness, as the poets of the Greeks have sung, {{NOTE: The myth is that Athena banished crows from the Acropolis because a crow told her that Cecrops' daughters had opened the chest holding Erichthonius.}}
  755  but the nature of the place does the work by its own power.
  756  In Syria too, it is said, a place is likewise to be seen
  757  where, as soon as even four-footed beasts have first set
  758  their footsteps in it, its very force makes them drop heavily,
  759  as if they had suddenly been slaughtered to the divine shades.
  760  All these things are brought about by a natural principle,
  761  and the causes they come from, their origin, are plain to see,
  762  so that it may not be believed there is a gateway of Orcus in these regions,
  763  and we then suppose that spirits are led from here to the shores of Acheron,
  764  led down, it may be, by the gods of the shades below,
  765  as wing-footed stags are often thought, with their nostrils,
  766  to draw from their lairs the crawling generations of wild things. {{NOTE: The belief was that stags draw snakes out of their holes by their breath (Pliny, Natural History 8.118).}}
  767  How far this is driven from true reasoning,
  768  take note; for now I attempt to speak of the thing itself.
  769  First, I say this, as I have often said before as well:
  770  that in the earth there are shapes of things of every kind;
  771  many that are life-giving, serving as food, and many that diseases
  772  can drive in upon us and that can hasten death.
  773  And that different things are better suited to different living creatures
  774  for the ordering of life we have shown before,
  775  because of their unlike nature and their unlike
  776  textures, one from another, and their first shapes.
  777  Many things pass hostile through the ears, many through the very
  778  nostrils make their way in, harmful and rough in their touch,
  779  nor are there few that must be shunned by touch, nor again few
  780  to be fled from by sight, or that are bitter in taste.
  781  Next, you can see how many things there are for a man
  782  bitterly hostile to sense, foul and oppressive;
  783  first, to certain trees an oppressive shade is given,
  784  so much so that they often cause pains of the head
  785  if someone has lain stretched out beneath them on the grass.
  786  There is also, on the great mountains of Helicon, a tree
  787  that is used to killing a man with the foul smell of its flower.
  788  Plainly all these rise up out of the earth for this reason,
  789  that many seeds of many things, in many ways,
  790  the earth holds mixed together, and gives them forth apart.
  791  And a night-lamp, newly put out, when with its sharp
  792  reek it strikes the nostrils, puts to sleep there and then
  793  the one whose sickness makes him fall and foam at the mouth. {{NOTE: Epilepsy, the "falling sickness".}}
  794  And heavy castoreum puts a woman to sleep so that she sinks back, {{NOTE: Castoreum: a strong-smelling secretion of the beaver, used in ancient medicine.}}
  795  and the bright work slips from her tender hands,
  796  if she has smelled it at the time of her monthly flow.
  797  Many things besides make the limbs droop, and through the joints
  798  loosen them, and shake the spirit in its seats within.
  799  Again, if you linger in hot baths
  800  and bathe on a full stomach, how easily, in the tub of boiling water,
  801  it comes about that you often collapse in the middle!
  802  And the heavy force and smell of charcoal makes its way
  803  how easily into the brain, unless we have first taken water!
  804  But when a burning fever has seized the body, mastering the limbs,
  805  then the smell of wine is like a killing blow.
  806  Do you not see, too, that in the earth itself sulphur
  807  is produced, and bitumen thickens with a foul smell,
  808  and again, where men follow the veins of silver and of gold,
  809  searching with iron the hidden places deep in the earth,
  810  what smells Scaptensula breathes out from below? {{NOTE: Scaptensula (Skapte Hyle): a gold-mining district in Thrace.}}
  811  Or what evil the gold mines breathe out!
  812  What faces, what colors they give to men!
  813  Do you not see, or hear, how in a short time
  814  they are apt to die, and how the store of life fails them,
  815  those whom the great force of necessity holds to such work?
  816  All these exhalations, therefore, the earth seethes out
  817  and breathes forth into the open and the free spaces of the sky.
  818  So also the Avernian places must send up against birds
  819  a deadly force, which rises from the earth into the breezes
  820  so that it poisons the space of sky over some part;
  821  and as soon as a bird has been carried on its wings into it,
  822  there it is hampered, seized by unseen poison,
  823  so that it falls straight down over the place where the exhalation is aimed.
  824  And when it has collapsed there, the same force of that exhalation
  825  takes the remnants of life from all its limbs.
  826  For indeed at first it stirs in it a kind of turmoil;
  827  afterwards it comes about, when they have fallen at last {{NOTE: The text and punctuation of 826-829 are uncertain; the sense is that birds fallen into the source of the poison must vomit out their life.}}
  828  into the very springs of poison, that there life too must be vomited out,
  829  because there is a great abundance of evil all around.
  830  It happens too that at times this force and exhalation of Avernus
  831  drives apart the air, whatever lies placed between the birds and the earth,
  832  so that the place there is left almost empty.
  833  And when, flying, they have come directly over this place,
  834  at once the effort of their wings limps, empty,
  835  and all the striving of the wings on either side is betrayed.
  836  Here, when they cannot lean their weight and rest upon their wings,
  837  plainly, it forces them by their weight to slip down to the earth,
  838  nature does, and lying now through the almost empty void
  839  they scatter their spirits through all the passages of the body.
       [lacuna]
  840  Further, the water in wells grows colder in summer,
  841  because the earth dries out with the heat, and whatever seeds
  842  of heat it may happen to have of its own, it lets go into the breezes.
  843  The more, therefore, the earth is exhausted by heat,
  844  the colder too becomes the water hidden in the earth.
  845  Again, when all the earth is pressed by cold and draws together
  846  and as it were congeals, it plainly happens that in drawing together
  847  it squeezes out into the wells whatever heat it holds.
  848  There is, by the shrine of Hammon, a spring in the light of day {{NOTE: Hammon (Ammon): the oracle of Zeus Ammon at the oasis of Siwa in the Libyan desert.}}
  849  cold, and hot in the time of night, so the story goes.
  850  At this spring men marvel too much, and suppose that by the fierce
  851  sun beneath the earth it partly grows hot
  852  when night has covered the lands in fearful darkness.
  853  And this is removed far, too far, from true reasoning.
  854  For when the sun, handling the naked body of the water,
  855  could not make it warm from its upper side,
  856  though its light above enjoys such burning heat,
  857  how could it here, beneath earth so dense in body,
  858  cook the water through and nurse it with warm heat?
  859  Especially since it can scarcely, through the walls of houses,
  860  make its heat work its way in with its blazing rays.
  861  What, then, is the principle? Doubtless that the earth is more
  862  porous around the spring than the rest of the earth,
  863  and there are many seeds of fire near the body of the water.
  864  So, when night has buried the earth in dew-bearing waves,
  865  at once the earth grows cold deep down and draws together.
  866  By this principle it comes about that, just as if pressed by a hand,
  867  it squeezes out into the spring whatever seeds of fire it holds,
  868  which make the water hot to the touch, and its steam hot.
  869  Then, when the sun has risen and parted the earth with its rays
  870  and made it porous, its warm heat mixing in,
  871  back again to their old seats return the first-beginnings
  872  of fire, and all the water's heat withdraws into the earth.
  873  For this reason the spring grows cold in the light of day.
  874  Further, by the rays of the sun the water's
  875  moisture is shaken, and in daylight it thins out with the trembling heat;
  876  therefore it comes about that whatever seeds of fire it has
  877  it lets go, just as often the frost it holds within itself
  878  it gives up, and dissolves its ice and loosens its knots.
