[DRAFT] (6.357) Autumn and spring are the stormy seasons because they are the meeting-points of cold and heat. This opens the argument that the strait of the year (fretus anni, 364) mixes opposites, and the same figure is named outright at 6.374.

[DRAFT] (6.364) "Strait" (fretus) is the narrows where two seas meet and churn. Lucretius uses it as a figure for the turning seasons, and the English keeps the nautical image so that "straits of the year" (freta anni, 374) can be read as its explanation. The same word freta later means "waters" or "sea" (427), where the English follows it.

[DRAFT] (6.367) The air "heaves like a sea" (fluctuet aer) takes up the strait image and carries it on, so the storm is pictured as a sea of air. The English keeps the verb "heaves" to hold the nautical figure.

[DRAFT] (6.381) The "Tyrrhenian chants" (Tyrrhena carmina) are the Etruscan books of thunder-lore. The haruspices used them to read lightning, by its quarter, direction and effect, as signs from the gods, and lines 383-386 list exactly those questions. Lucretius sets natural explanation against this practice and has no objection to the questions as such, only to the divine reading.

[DRAFT] (6.387) The argument from here to 6.422 is a series of rhetorical questions on the premise "if Jupiter and the other gods hurl the bolt" (si Iuppiter...iaciunt ignem). Each question points to a result the premise cannot explain. Lucretius makes the same case against Jupiter's thunder in Book 2, and the argument is the Epicurean one that the gods, being blessed and untroubled, do not direct such events.

[DRAFT] (6.397) "Exercising their arms" (bracchia consuescunt) is ironic. If the gods hurl bolts at empty places, Lucretius says, they must be practising like athletes or soldiers.

[DRAFT] (6.417) The "holy shrines" (sancta delubra) and the images in them are struck, which would be sacrilege if Jupiter were the thrower. This is the most pointed of the questions, since it makes the god strike his own temples.

[DRAFT] (6.419) "Images" (simulacra) here means ordinary statues of the gods. It is not the technical sense of the films that peel from objects in Book 4, which the register renders "images" as well. The reader should not import the Book 4 doctrine.

[DRAFT] (6.424) "Presters" (presteras) is Greek prester, "burner" or "blower", used of a fiery whirlwind or waterspout. Lucretius says "the Greeks named" them (Graii nominitarunt) because he is borrowing a technical term, and the English keeps the Greek word in its Latin form.

[DRAFT] (6.451) The account of cloud formation (nubila concrescunt) starts again here with a new topic, after the waterspout passage. The "bodies" (corpora) that "come together" are the first bodies in the atomist sense, rougher and hooked ones that cling in "slight ways" (modis exiguis). This is the plain "bodies" of the register and not "particles".
