  878  But neither were there ever Centaurs, nor at any time
  879  can there be creatures of double nature and twofold body
  880  made up of limbs from alien stocks, whose powers
  881  on this side and on that could be matched closely enough.
  882  This you may learn from what follows, however dull your heart.
  883  To begin with, when three years have come round, the horse
  884  is in its vigorous flower, the boy by no means; for often even then
  885  he will seek in his sleep the milk-giving teats of the breast.
  886  Later, when in old age the horse's strong powers
  887  and limbs fail, grown languid as life flees from them,
  888  then at last, for the boy in the flower of his age, youth
  889  comes on and clothes his cheeks with a soft down;
  890  so that you need not believe that from man and the seed
  891  of draft horses Centaurs can be formed or can exist,
  892  or Scyllas, their bodies half of the sea, girdled
  893  with ravening dogs, and the rest of their kind
  894  whose limbs we see to be at odds with one another;
  895  for these neither come to flower together nor take on strength
  896  in their bodies nor throw it off in old age,
  897  nor burn with a like Venus, nor agree in one manner {{NOTE: Venus here stands for sexual desire, as often in Lucretius; the name is kept}}
  898  of life, nor are the same things pleasant through their frames.
  899  For indeed you may see bearded flocks often grow fat
  900  on hemlock, which to a man is a bitter poison.
  901  And since flame is indeed accustomed to scorch and burn
  902  the tawny bodies of lions just as much as every kind
  903  of flesh and blood that exists anywhere on earth,
  904  how could it come about that, one in a triple body,
  905  lion in front, serpent behind, in the middle herself, the Chimaera {{NOTE: "herself": chimaera is also the Greek for a she-goat, so the middle is goat; the pun is lost in English}}
  906  breathed out from her body through her mouth a bitter flame?
  907  Therefore whoever imagines that, when the earth was new and the sky
  908  fresh, such living creatures could have been begotten,
  909  leaning on this one empty name of newness,
  910  may babble out many things by a like reasoning:
  911  let him say that then rivers of gold flowed
  912  everywhere through the lands, and trees used to flower with jewels, {{NOTE: the source prints "suessesuësse", a doubled corruption of suesse, "used to"}}
  913  or that a man was born with such a sweep of limbs
  914  that he could plant his steps across the deep seas
  915  and with his hands turn the whole heaven round about him.
  916  For though there were many seeds of things in the earth
  917  at the time when the earth first poured forth living creatures,
  918  still there is no sign that mixed beasts could be created
  919  and the limbs of living things joined together,
  920  because the kinds of herbs and crops and glad trees
  921  that even now abound from the earth
  922  still cannot be created intertwined with one another,
  923  but each thing proceeds after its own fashion, and all
  924  keep their distinctions by nature's fixed covenant.
  925  And the human race in the fields then was much
  926  harder, as was fitting, since the hard earth had created it,
  927  and built on bigger and more solid bones within,
  928  fastened through the flesh with strong sinews,
  929  not easily overcome by heat or by cold
  930  or by strange food or by any flaw of the body.
  931  And through many cycles of the sun rolling across the sky
  932  they led their lives in the roaming manner of wild beasts.
  933  Nor was there any sturdy steersman of the curved plough,
  934  nor did anyone know how to work the fields with iron
  935  or to dig young shoots into the earth, or from tall
  936  trees to cut away the old branches with pruning-hooks.
  937  What sun and rains had given, what the earth had created
  938  of her own accord, that gift was enough to content their hearts.
  939  Among the acorn-bearing oaks they tended their bodies
  940  for the most part; and those which now in winter time you see,
  941  the arbute berries, ripening to a scarlet color, {{NOTE: arbita, the fruit of the strawberry tree (Arbutus unedo)}}
  942  the earth then bore in great plenty, and larger still.
  943  And besides, the flowering newness of the world then
  944  bore many coarse foods, ample for wretched mortals.
  945  But rivers and springs called them to slake their thirst,
  946  as now the downrush of water from the great mountains
  947  summons far and wide, loud and clear, the thirsting races of wild beasts.
  948  Finally, as they wandered they dwelt in the woodland precincts
  949  of the nymphs, known to them, from which they knew that running streams
  950  of water washed with ample flood the wet rocks,
  951  the wet rocks, dripping down over the green moss,
  952  and in part welled up and burst out over the level plain.
  953  Nor yet did they know how to work things with fire, nor to use
  954  skins and clothe their bodies in the spoils of wild beasts,
  955  but they lived in groves and mountain hollows and forests,
  956  and hid their rough limbs among the brushwood
  957  when driven to escape the lash of winds and rains.
  958  Nor could they look to a common good, nor did they know
  959  how to use any customs or laws among themselves.
  960  Whatever prey fortune had offered to each, he carried off,
  961  each taught to be strong and to live for himself of his own accord.
  962  And Venus joined the bodies of lovers in the woods;
  963  for each woman was won either by mutual desire
  964  or by the man's violent force and urgent lust
  965  or by a price, acorns and arbute berries or choice pears.
  966  And trusting in the wondrous power of their hands and feet
  967  they hunted down the woodland races of wild beasts
  968  with stones for missiles and the great weight of the club;
  969  many they overcame, a few they avoided in hiding places;
  970  and like bristly boars they gave their woodland limbs
  971  naked to the earth when overtaken by the night,
  972  rolling themselves round in leaves and foliage.
  973  Nor did they seek the day and the sun with loud laments,
  974  wandering in terror through the fields in the shadows of night,
  975  but silent and buried in sleep they waited
  976  until the sun with rosy torch brought light into the sky.
  977  For since from childhood they had been used to seeing always
  978  darkness and light begotten in alternate times,
  979  it could not be that they should ever wonder
  980  or mistrust, fearing that eternal night would hold the earth
  981  forever, the light of the sun withdrawn.
  982  But this was more their care, that the races of wild beasts
  983  often made rest dangerous for the wretched men.
  984  Driven from home, they fled their rocky shelters
  985  at the coming of a foaming boar or a strong lion,
  986  and in the dead of night, in terror, they gave up
  987  to savage guests their beds strewn with leaves.
