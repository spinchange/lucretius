  146  Again, you cannot possibly believe that the holy
  147  dwellings of the gods exist in any parts of the world.
  148  For the nature of the gods is thin, and far removed
  149  from our senses, and is scarcely seen by the thought of the mind;
  150  and since it slips away from the touch and stroke of hands,
  151  it must touch nothing that is tangible to us;
  152  for what may not itself be touched cannot touch.
  153  Therefore their dwellings too must also be unlike
  154  our dwellings, thin, in keeping with their bodies;
  155  and this I shall prove to you later in ample discourse. {{NOTE: the promised account of the gods' dwellings never appears in the poem as we have it}}
  156  Further, to say that for the sake of men they willed to prepare
  157  the glorious nature of the world, and that therefore
  158  it is fitting to praise the praiseworthy work of the gods,
  159  and to think that it will be eternal and immortal,
  160  and that it is sin that what, by the gods' ancient reasoning,
  161  was founded for the races of men to last through endless age,
  162  should ever be shaken by any force from its seat,
  163  or harried with words and overturned from top to bottom,
  164  and to invent and add other things of this kind, Memmius,
  165  is folly. For what profit could our gratitude
  166  bestow upon immortal and blessed beings,
  167  that for our sake they should set about doing anything?
  168  Or what new thing could, so long after, lure beings at peace before
  169  to desire to change their former life?
  170  For it seems that one should rejoice in new things
  171  whom the old things harm; but one who met with nothing grievous
  172  in the time gone by, while he spent his life in beauty,
  173  what could kindle in such a one a love of novelty?
  174  Or what harm would it have been to us never to have been created?
  175  Did life, I suppose, lie in darkness and sorrow
  176  until the generative origin of things dawned?
  177  For whoever has been born must want to remain
  178  in life, so long as soft pleasure holds him;
  179  but for one who never tasted the love of life
  180  and was never counted, what harm is it not to have been created?
  181  Further, the pattern for creating things, and the very
  182  notion of men, from where was it first planted in the gods, {{NOTE: notities: the Epicurean "preconception" (prolepsis); the gods could not have known what men were before men existed}}
  183  so that they knew and saw in their mind what they wished to make,
  184  or in what way was the power of the beginnings ever known,
  185  and what they could do with their order changed among themselves,
  186  if nature herself did not give a likeness of creation?
  187  For so many first-beginnings of things, in so many ways,
  188  driven by blows from infinite time till now,
  189  and roused by their own weights, have been used to move
  190  and to meet in every way and to try out all things
  191  that they could create by meeting with one another,
  192  so it is no wonder if they fell into such arrangements
  193  and have come, as well, into such courses
  194  as those by which this sum of things is now carried on by renewal.
  195  But even if I did not know what the first-beginnings of things are,
  196  this, still, from the very order of heaven I would dare
  197  to affirm, and to show from many other things,
  198  that by no means for us, by divine power, was prepared
  199  the nature of things: so great the flaws it stands endowed with.
  200  First, of all that the vast sweep of heaven covers,
  201  a greedy portion the mountains and the forests of wild beasts
  202  have taken; rocks hold it, and vast marshes,
  203  and the sea, which keeps the shores of the lands wide apart.
  204  Then, further, nearly two parts the burning heat
  205  and the unceasing fall of frost take away from mortals.
  206  What is left of plowland, even that nature by her own force
  207  would choke with brambles, did human force not resist,
  208  used for life's sake to groan over the strong mattock
  209  and to cut through the earth with the plow pressed down.
  210  If we did not, turning the fertile clods with the plowshare
  211  and working the soil of the earth, rouse them to birth,
  212  of their own accord they could not come out into the clear air.
  213  And even so, at times, when things won with great labor
  214  are now in leaf throughout the lands and all in flower,
  215  either the sun in the ether scorches them with too much heat,
  216  or sudden rains destroy them, and chill frosts,
  217  and blasts of wind harry them with violent whirlwind.
  218  Further, the race of wild beasts, bristling with horror,
  219  hostile to the human kind on land and sea,
  220  why does nature feed and increase it? Why do the seasons bring
  221  diseases? Why does untimely death go wandering?
  222  Then again the child, like a sailor cast up by savage waves,
  223  lies naked on the ground, speechless, in want of every
  224  help for life, as soon as into the shores of light
  225  nature has poured him out with throes from his mother's womb,
  226  and he fills the place with mournful wailing, as is right
  227  for one who has so much of evil left to pass through in life.
  228  But the various flocks grow up, and herds, and wild beasts,
  229  and they need no rattles, nor must any of them be given
  230  the coaxing, broken baby-talk of a kindly nurse,
  231  nor do they look for changing clothes to suit the weather;
  232  finally, they need no arms, no high walls,
  233  to guard their own, since for all of them all things abundantly
  234  the earth herself brings forth, and nature, artificer of things.
  235  First, since the body of the earth, and moisture,
  236  and the light breaths of air, and the hot vapors,
  237  of which this sum of things is seen to consist,
  238  all consist of a body that is born and mortal,
  239  the whole nature of the world must be thought to be of the same.
  240  For indeed, things whose parts and members we see
  241  to be of a body that is born and of mortal shapes,
  242  these same we see to be, as a rule, mortal
  243  and born as well. Therefore, since I see the greatest
  244  members and parts of the world consumed and born again,
  245  we may know that heaven too, and likewise earth, have had
  246  some time of beginning, and will have their destruction.
  247  In these matters, do not think that I have taken too much
  248  for granted, in assuming earth and fire to be mortal,
  249  and in not doubting that moisture and the airs perish,
  250  and in saying that the same are born and grow again.
  251  First, no small part of the earth, scorched through
  252  by constant suns, beaten by the force of many feet,
  253  breathes out a haze of dust and flying clouds,
  254  which strong winds scatter through all the air. {{NOTE: the source prints "aereaëre", evidently a corruption of aere, "air"}}
  255  Part of the clods too is called back to flood
  256  by rains, and rivers graze and gnaw their banks.
  257  Further, whatever feeds and increases another, in its share
  258  is given back; and since beyond doubt it is seen
  259  that the mother of all is also the common tomb of things,
  260  therefore the earth, you see, is drawn upon and, increased, grows back.
