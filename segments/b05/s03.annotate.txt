[DRAFT] (5.381) The Romans called a war duly declared, with the fetial priests' rites observed, a *bellum pium* (pio ... bello); Lucretius turns the phrase around, so that "in no way holy" (nequaquam pio) says the elemental war is without any rite or sanction. The sense is "impious" as well as "undeclared."

[DRAFT] (5.395) The flood is not named here. The reader will probably think of Deucalion's flood, which the Greeks paired with Phaethon's conflagration as the two great catastrophes (umor ... regnarit in arvis). Lucretius gives it only the hedge "as the story goes" (ut fama est). The same phrase returns at 5.412, so the hedge is a refrain.

[DRAFT] (5.397) Phaethon, son of the Sun, borrowed his father's chariot and lost control of it, scorching the earth until Jupiter struck him down with a thunderbolt (Phaethonta ... rapax vis solis equorum). Lucretius tells the myth in full only to reject it at 5.406 as "driven very far away from true reasoning" (a vera ... ratione). He keeps the poets' version of the event but replaces its cause with a shift in the balance of matter.

[DRAFT] (5.419) Lines 419-431 closely repeat 1.1021-1028. That passage says the first-beginnings did not arrange themselves by design but, after trying every combination through endless time, fell into the patterns that make the world. The translation should therefore match its Book 1 rendering word for word wherever the Latin is the same (primordia rerum, ex infinito, plagis ponderibusque). Here "first-beginnings" is the fixed rendering of primordia.

[DRAFT] (5.421) "Nor did they bargain what motions each would give" (pepigere) uses the language of contract. The image is of the first-beginnings as parties to an agreement, which the poet denies. This is a negative counterpart to the later "covenants" of nature (foedera naturae).

[DRAFT] (5.440) Here the first-beginnings are called *principia* (omnigenis e principiis), a variant of primordia. The register's rule of distinct Latin for distinct English suggests keeping the variation visible. A rendering such as "beginnings of every kind," as the translation has it, can do that, with the Latin noted.

[DRAFT] (5.456) The pun on *elementa* ("elements") is the register's: the same word names the letters of the alphabet in Lucretius's analogy between atoms and letters (1.197, 2.688ff.). Here it means the smallest constituent bodies, "much smaller elements" than those of earth (multoque minoribus ... elementis). The doctrine is that the lighter, finer bodies are the ones that rise.

[DRAFT] (5.457) The earth's "open pores" (rara foramina) express the doctrine that even apparently solid things contain void, which is what lets the lighter bodies escape. The reader may associate this with Lucretius's earlier arguments for the inane (1.329ff.), though the verse does not repeat them.

[DRAFT] (5.478) The comparison of the heavenly bodies, which neither sink nor rise but turn in the middle region, to limbs that stay still while others move (in nobis quaedam ... membra) is Lucretius's own analogy. It sustains his picture of the world as a body with "members" (membra, 5.381, 5.439), and links the cosmic and the human.
