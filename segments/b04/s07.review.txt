  907  Now in what ways sleep floods the limbs with rest
  908  and loosens the cares of the mind from the breast,
  909  I shall set out in verses sweet-spoken rather than many,
  910  as the small song of the swan is better than that clamor
  911  of cranes scattered among the high clouds of the south wind.
  912  You, give me fine ears and a keen mind,
  913  lest you deny that what I say can come to be, and go off
  914  with a breast that thrusts the true words back,
  915  when you yourself are at fault and cannot see it.
  916  To begin with, sleep comes about when the force of the spirit
  917  is scattered through the frame, and part, thrown out of doors, has gone,
  918  and part, crowded together, has withdrawn deeper inside;
  919  for only then are the limbs loosened and go slack.
  920  For there is no doubt that it is by the work of the spirit
  921  that this sense is in us; and when slumber hinders it from being,
  922  then we must think that our spirit has been thrown into disorder
  923  and cast out of doors, though not all; for then the body would lie
  924  steeped in the eternal chill of death.
  925  Indeed, if no part of the spirit stayed hidden
  926  in the limbs, as fire lies hidden, buried under much ash,
  927  from where could sense be kindled again through the limbs on a sudden,
  928  as flame rises up out of hidden fire?
  929  But by what things this change is brought about, and from where
  930  the spirit can be thrown into disorder and the body grow languid,
  931  I shall explain: you, see I do not pour my words to the winds.
  932  To begin with, on its outer side the body must,
  933  since it is touched by the neighboring breezes of the air,
  934  be beaten and battered by their constant blows,
  935  and that is why nearly all things are covered either with hide
  936  or else with shells or with hard skin or with bark.
  937  On the inner side too, as we breathe, the air
  938  strikes this same body, as it is drawn in and breathed out.
  939  Therefore, since the body is beaten on both sides, and since
  940  the blows make their way through the small passages
  941  to the first parts and the first elements of our body,
  942  there comes about in us, as it were, a gradual ruin through the limbs.
  943  For the positions of the beginnings of body and mind
  944  are thrown into confusion. Hence it happens that part of the spirit
  945  is driven out, and part withdraws, hidden, inward,
  946  and part too, scattered through the frame, cannot hold
  947  together with itself nor carry out its motions in common;
  948  for nature walls off the meetings and the passages between.
  949  Therefore sense goes away, its motions changed deep within.
  950  And since there is nothing, as it were, to prop the frame,
  951  the body grows weak and all the limbs grow languid,
  952  the arms and the eyelids drop, and the knees, as one lies down,
  953  often give way all the same and let their strength go.
  954  Next, sleep follows food, because the very things the air does,
  955  these same things food brings about while it is spread into all
  956  the veins. And that slumber is by far the heaviest
  957  which you take when full or tired, since then the greatest number
  958  of bodies are thrown into confusion, bruised by great labor.
  959  By the same principle part of the spirit is crowded
  960  more deeply, and more of it is cast out of doors,
  961  and it is more divided within itself and more scattered inside.
  962  And whatever pursuit each of us is bound to and clings to,
  963  or whatever things we have dwelt on long before,
  964  the business in which the mind has been most intent,
  965  in dreams we generally seem to go about the same:
  966  advocates seem to plead cases and draw up laws,
  967  commanders to fight and go into battle,
  968  sailors to live through the war they have joined with the winds,
  969  and we to do this work, and seek the nature of things
  970  always, and set it out, once found, in our native pages. {{NOTE: patriis chartis: Latin, as against the Greek of Epicurus' own writings}}
  971  So other pursuits and arts are generally seen
  972  to hold the minds of men in dreams, deluding them.
  973  And whoever have given unbroken attention to the games
  974  for many days in a row, we generally see,
  975  when they have ceased to take them in with their senses,
  976  that there still remain passages open in the mind
  977  by which the same images of things can come;
  978  and so for many days those same things hover
  979  before their eyes, so that even awake they seem
  980  to see people dancing and moving supple limbs,
  981  and to take in with their ears the liquid song of the lyre
  982  and its speaking strings, and to see the same assembly
  983  and, all together, the varied adornments of the stage shining.
  984  So much does it matter what we are eager for and what we will,
  985  and in what things they have been used to busy themselves,
  986  not only men but indeed all living creatures.
  987  Indeed you will see strong horses, when their limbs lie down,
  988  still sweat in their sleep and keep on panting
  989  and strain their utmost strength as though for the palm,
  990  or as though the starting-gates were thrown open, give voice.
  991  And hunters' dogs often, in soft repose,
  992  still throw out their legs suddenly, and all at once give
  993  cries, and draw the air back through their nostrils again and again,
  994  as if they were holding to the tracks of wild beasts they have found,
  995  and, woken up, they often chase the empty
  996  images of stags, as though they saw them given over to flight,
