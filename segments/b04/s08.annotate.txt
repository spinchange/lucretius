[DRAFT] (4.1037) The "seed of which we spoke before" refers to the account of the generation of seed in the body at the opening of this section (the transition from the boy's first stirrings, 4.1030ff.). Here Lucretius begins the physiology of desire proper: seed ripens with age, is stirred by the sight of the beloved's simulacra, and is drawn toward its source of excitation.

[DRAFT] (4.1047) The line closely echoes 4.1034 (incitat inritans loca turgida semine multo); here it follows 4.1045-1046 awkwardly, repeating "swollen with seed" and introducing a second verb of driving on where the sentence has already reached its goal. Some editors bracket or transpose it; the translation keeps it in the source order and simply renders it, so the repetition shows.

[DRAFT] (4.1049) The comparison with a wound (vulnus) develops the image of 4.1048 (saucia amore): as blood spurts toward the injury and toward the striker, so the seed rushes toward the person who struck with the "weapons of Venus" (Veneris telis). The ictus language belongs to the Roman poetic tradition of love as a wound or dart, which Lucretius takes in a literal physiological sense.

[DRAFT] (4.1058) Venus here is the sexual drive itself, and "Love" (Amor) is its name, derived (hinc) from the physiology just described: in the Epicurean reading the gods Venus and Cupid are not persons but names for a bodily process. The drop (gutta) of Venus' sweetness is the first stirring, and "chill care" (frigida cura) follows, the sorrow the poet goes on to describe.

[DRAFT] (4.1061) Simulacra (images) are the Epicurean films of atoms shed from the surface of things and carried through the air to the eyes and mind. The beloved's image is thus present to the lover even when the beloved is absent, and the same name-word that keeps hovering about the ears (nomen dulce obversatur) keeps desire alive. This explains why Lucretius advises fleeing them (fugitare decet simulacra).

[DRAFT] (4.1071) The "wandering Venus" (volgivaga Venus) is the Venus of the street, casual or bought love; the doubled "wandering" (volgivaga ... vagus) is deliberate, and the translation keeps it. The advice is therapeutic rather than moralizing: discharge the seed into any body, so that it does not fix on one (4.1065-1066).

[DRAFT] (4.1073) Lucretius's argument that the man who avoids love loses nothing of Venus' fruit rests on Epicurus's distinction between pleasure and the pain that often accompanies it; the "pure" pleasure (purast ... voluptas) is that of the sane man, who takes the advantages without the penalty (sine poena). This is Epicurean hedonism applied to sex, not an ascetic rejection of it.

[DRAFT] (4.1088) The sense is that nature "fights back" (repugnat): love alone intensifies with satisfaction, unlike hunger and thirst, which are filled because food and drink enter the body and occupy fixed parts (certas ... partis, 4.1092). The doctrine behind it is that the image-films of the beloved never become the stuff of the body, so they can feed no real need and only inflame it.

[DRAFT] (4.1097) The simile of the thirsty dreamer who drinks in his sleep and still thirsts (bibere in somnis sitiens) is a standard figure for frustrated desire, and here it makes the point that in love one chases only simulacra (laticum simulacra). The translation renders the paradox of thirsting in the middle of a river (in medioque ... torrenti flumine) literally.

[DRAFT] (4.1107) The "woman's fields" (muliebria ... arva) belong to the old agricultural metaphor of marriage and procreation as sowing and ploughing, found throughout Latin and Greek poetry; conserat is the verb of sowing. The translation keeps the image and does not explain it away.

[DRAFT] (4.1124) Babylonian perfumes (Babylonia ... unguenta), Sicyonian shoes (Sicyonia), emeralds, Alindan and Cean fabrics: the list catalogues the luxury goods of the Roman demimonde, and each geographical adjective stands for a specific fashionable import. The shoes "laugh" (rident) in the sense of gleam, and are on her feet, not his, so the spender is bankrupting himself on a mistress's finery.

[DRAFT] (4.1129) The fathers' "well-gotten wealth" (bene parta patrum) is the family patrimony squandered; the anademata (Greek headbands) and mitrae (turbans) are named in Greek or Greek-derived words because they are the foreign luxuries of a courtesan, whose finery Lucretius mocks. The translation gives them in English.

[DRAFT] (4.1141) "Love that is true and at its most prosperous" (in amore ... proprio summeque secundo) renders the Latin literally: even in happy love the bitterness rises from the "fountain of delights" (4.1133), and unhappy love (in adverso atque inopi) is worse. The point is that the evils are inseparable from the passion, not accidents of failure.

[DRAFT] (4.1145) "By the principle I have taught" (qua docui ratione) points back to the earlier advice to fly the images and turn the mind elsewhere (4.1063-1072) and, more broadly, to the whole reasoning of the book. This is the register's "ratio" used instrumentally.

[DRAFT] (4.1148) The "nets" and "knots" (retibus ... nodos) belong to the hunting and fishing imagery of love as a snare, which runs from Greek epigram through Roman elegy. The translation keeps the physical image and does not tone it down.

[DRAFT] (4.1160) The catalogue of pet names that follows (4.1160-1169) is a famous passage, imitated by Ovid (Ars Amatoria 2.657-662) and Molière. In the Latin, many of the euphemisms are Greek words transliterated (melichrus, acosmos, Palladium, dorcas, chariton mia, cataplexis, traulizi, ischnon eromenion, rhadine, philema), a mark of the lovers' affectation; the translation puts them into English and in quotation marks. The text here is also disputed in places, and some lines show corruption.

[DRAFT] (4.1168) "Ceres herself, nursing Iacchus" (Ceres ... ab Iaccho): Iacchus is the child-god of the Eleusinian mysteries, associated with Demeter (Ceres) and Dionysus; here a big-breasted woman is called Ceres for her fullness. The reading ab Iaccho is uncertain, and the translation follows the source text as given; the line is a comic compression of the epithet.

[DRAFT] (4.1169) The "she-Silenus" and "satyress" (Silena ac Satura) are female versions of Silenus and satyrs, the snub-nosed, thick-lipped followers of Dionysus, so the flattering names for a snub-nosed woman are a joke at her expense. "Kiss" (philema) is Greek for a kiss.

[DRAFT] (4.1186) The "backstage parts" (postscaenia) are the area behind the stage of a Roman theater, here a figure for the unglamorous routines (perfume, hygiene, etc.) that women hide from the men they want to keep. The translation renders the theatrical word literally.

[DRAFT] (4.1191) The closing "make allowance for human things" (humanis concedere rebus) is a mild and tolerant note after the satire: the poet recommends that a woman of good heart be forgiven her imperfections, in return, so that love becomes a livable arrangement rather than a madness. This line ends the section on love and passes to the end of Book 4.
