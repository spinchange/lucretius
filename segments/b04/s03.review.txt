  379  Yet we do not grant that the eyes are deceived one whit in this.
  380  For to see in what place there is light and shadow
  381  is their task; but whether the lights are the same or not,
  382  and whether the shadow that was here now passes over there,
  383  or rather what we described a little before comes about,
  384  this the reasoning of the mind alone must discern,
  385  nor can the eyes come to know the nature of things.
  386  Do not, then, fasten this fault of the mind upon the eyes.
  387  The ship in which we are carried moves while it seems to stand still;
  388  the one that stays at its mooring is believed to be going past.
  389  And hills and plains seem to flee astern
  390  as we drive past them in our ship and fly under sail.
  391  The stars all seem to rest, fixed fast to the vaults of the ether,
  392  and yet all of them are in unceasing motion,
  393  since, once risen, they go back to their far-off settings
  394  when they have measured out the sky with their bright bodies.
  395  And on a like principle the sun and the moon seem to stay
  396  at rest, though the fact itself shows that they are carried on.
  397  And mountains that stand out far off from the middle of the sea,
  398  between which a wide passage lies open, free for fleets,
  399  seem nonetheless to make one island, joined together.
  400  That the halls turn round and the columns run in circles
  401  seems so strongly to children, once they themselves
  402  have stopped spinning, that they can hardly now believe
  403  the whole roof is not threatening to fall in on them.
  404  Again, when nature begins to raise on high the red radiance
  405  with its trembling fires, and to lift it above the mountains,
  406  those mountains above which the sun then seems to you to be,
  407  touching them close at hand, burning, with its own fire,
  408  are scarcely two thousand bowshots away from us,
  409  often scarcely even five hundred casts of a javelin;
  410  between them and the sun lie the immense levels
  411  of the sea, spread out beneath the vast shores of the ether,
  412  and many thousands of lands lie in between,
  413  held by various peoples and the generations of wild beasts.
  414  But a pool of water no deeper than a single finger,
  415  which stands between the stones of the paving in the streets,
  416  offers a view down beneath the earth to a depth as great
  417  as the high gulf of the sky gapes open above the earth,
  418  so that you seem to look down on clouds and to see the sky,
  419  and bodies wonderfully hidden in a sky beneath the earth.
  420  Again, when our spirited horse has stuck fast in midstream
  421  and we have looked down into the river's rushing waves,
  422  some force seems to carry the body of the standing horse
  423  sideways and to thrust it swiftly against the current,
  424  and wherever we cast our eyes, all things seem
  425  to be carried and to flow on, to us, in the same way.
  426  Again, though a colonnade runs in an even line
  427  and stands from end to end propped on equal columns,
  428  yet when it is seen, all its length, from the top end,
  429  it gradually draws into the point of a narrow cone,
  430  joining roof to floor and all the right side to the left,
  431  until it has drawn them into the dim tip of the cone.
  432  On the open sea, to sailors the sun, risen from the waves,
  433  seems to set in the waves and to hide its light;
  434  for indeed they see nothing else but water and sky;
  435  lest you lightly believe the senses are shaken on every side.
  436  But to those who do not know the sea, ships in harbor
  437  seem crippled, their stern-posts broken, as they strain against the waves.
  438  For whatever part of the oars is raised above the brine
  439  is straight, and the rudders are straight above;
  440  but the parts that go down sunk in the water seem broken back,
  441  all turned about and bent, returning upward, upside down,
  442  and so bent back as almost to float on the water's surface.
  443  And when the winds carry thin clouds across the sky
  444  in the night-time, then the shining constellations seem
  445  to glide against the clouds and to travel above them
  446  in a far other direction than true reasoning shows they are carried.
  447  But if by chance a hand placed beneath one eye
  448  presses it from below, by a certain feeling it comes about
  449  that all things we look at seem, as we look, to become double:
  450  double the lights of the lamps, flowering with flames,
  451  and through the whole house the furniture doubled in pairs,
  452  and men with double faces and twin bodies.
  453  Again, when sleep has bound our limbs in sweet slumber
  454  and the whole body lies in deepest rest,
  455  yet we seem to ourselves to be awake and to move
  456  our limbs, and in the blind darkness of the night
  457  we think we see the sun and the light of day,
  458  and, in a closed room, sky, sea, rivers, mountains
  459  we seem to change, and to cross the plains on foot,
  460  and to hear sounds, when the stern silences of the night
  461  hold fast on every side, and to give answers, saying nothing.
  462  We see many other wonders of this kind,
  463  all seeking, as it were, to violate our trust in the senses,
  464  in vain, since the greatest part of them deceive
  465  because of the mind's suppositions, which we add ourselves,
  466  so that things not seen by the senses pass for seen;
  467  for nothing is harder than to separate things plain
  468  from doubtful ones, which the mind straightway adds of itself.
  469  Again, if anyone thinks that nothing can be known, he does not know
  470  whether even that can be known, since he admits he knows nothing.
  471  Against this man, then, I will forbear to argue the case,
  472  who has set himself with his head where his footprints should be.
  473  And yet, granting that he knows even this, still I would ask
  474  this very thing: since he has seen nothing true in things before,
  475  from where does he know what it is to know and, in turn, not to know,
  476  what thing has created the notion of the true and the false,
  477  and what thing has proved the doubtful to differ from the certain?
  478  You will find that from the senses first was created
  479  the notion of the true, and that the senses cannot be refuted.
  480  For that must be found to deserve greater trust
  481  which can of its own accord overcome false things with true.
  482  Further, what ought to be held in greater trust than the senses?
  483  Or will reasoning, sprung from a false sense, have the power
  484  to speak against them, when it has sprung wholly from the senses?
  485  And unless they are true, all reasoning too becomes false.
  486  Or will the ears be able to correct the eyes, or touch
  487  the ears? Or again will the mouth's taste accuse this touch,
  488  or will the nostrils confute it, or the eyes refute it?
  489  It is not so, I think. For to each a separate power
  490  has been allotted, each has its own force, and so it must be
  491  that what is soft, and cold or hot, is perceived apart, {{NOTE: text uncertain at 4.491: the transmitted "videre" ("to see") sits oddly with touch; editors emend or transpose; rendered by sense}}
  492  and that the various colors of things are perceived apart,
  493  and whatever things are joined to colors.
  494  Apart, likewise, the mouth's taste has its power, apart smells
  495  are born, apart sounds. And so it must be
  496  that one sense cannot convict another.
  497  Nor, further, will they be able to correct themselves,
  498  since equal trust must always be placed in them.
  499  Therefore what has seemed to them at any time is true.
