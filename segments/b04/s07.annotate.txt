[DRAFT] (4.907) Opening of the sleep section. The swan and crane comparison (parvus ut est cycni melior canor, ille gruum quam clamor) is a recycled proem-style apology for brevity: Lucretius promises few sweet verses rather than many. The same swan/crane contrast appears at 3.6-7 (hirundo and cycni, equine and kids), and the poet's self-presentation as a swan recurs there.

[DRAFT] (4.912) The address to Memmius resumes (tu mihi da tenuis auris animumque sagacem). "Fine ears" is the register's rendering of tenuis auris, meaning delicate, discriminating hearing. The plea anticipates that the reader will reject the account as impossible (ne fieri negites), a worry Lucretius voices elsewhere when a doctrine seems incredible.

[DRAFT] (4.916) "Spirit" (vis animae) is the register's fixed rendering of anima, the vital principle spread through the body, in contrast to animus, the mind, seated in the breast. On the Epicurean account sleep is a partial displacement of the anima: some is expelled and some is pushed deep inside, while enough stays to keep the body alive (4.920-928, with the buried-fire image, cinere obrutus ignis).

[DRAFT] (4.943) "First-beginnings" (principiorum) is the register's term for the atoms, here called by the alternative name that the register fixes as primordia, and this is the first time the segment uses it. It is paired with the "first parts and first elements" at 4.941 (primas partis elementaque prima), so the blows of the air are said to reach down to the atomic level of both body and mind.

[DRAFT] (4.970) Patriis chartis means "the pages of our native tongue," i.e. Latin, as against the Greek in which Epicurus wrote. The line is a rare moment in which Lucretius shows himself in his own dream-life, and it matches his complaint about the poverty of Latin (patrii sermonis egestas, 1.832, 3.260).

[DRAFT] (4.999) The text is disordered here. In the printed order, the puppies at 998-999 are followed by a repetition of 992-995 at 1000-1003, and 1004 ends with the dogs looking at "unfamiliar faces." Editors take 1000-1003 as a displaced duplicate of 992-995, and 999 as the broken end of a lost line about the puppies; the translation follows the printed order and does not repair it.

[DRAFT] (4.1029) Babylonica refers to costly embroidered Babylonian coverlets, a stock luxury item in Roman writing. The detail makes the dreamer's bed-wetting incongruous: he dreams of relieving himself at a pool or beside jars (dolia curta), and drenches expensive bedding.

[DRAFT] (4.1030) Here the argument turns from general dreams to adolescent wet dreams, which Lucretius explains by the doctrine of simulacra, rendered in the register as "images" (simulacra, 4.1032). The images of a beautiful face and coloring reach the seed-swollen parts of a youth at puberty, and in sleep act as if the act itself had happened (transactis omnibus rebus). This passage opens the longer treatment of love and desire that follows in the rest of Book 4.
