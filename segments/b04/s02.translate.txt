  269  Come now, learn why the image is seen beyond the mirror; {{NOTE: imago and simulacra are both rendered "image(s)" in this passage; a deliberate merge for the register}}
  270  for certainly it is seen withdrawn deep within.
  271  It is like those things outside that are truly seen through
  272  when a doorway offers through itself an open view
  273  and lets many things outside be seen from within the house;
  274  for that sight too comes about through a double, twin air.
  275  For first, on this side of the doorposts, the air is discerned,
  276  next the doors themselves follow, to right and left,
  277  after that an outer light sweeps through the eyes, and a second
  278  air, and those things outside that are truly seen through.
  279  So, when the image of the mirror first has cast itself forth,
  280  while it comes to our sight, it thrusts and drives along
  281  all the air that is set between itself and our eyes,
  282  and brings it about that we can perceive all this air
  283  before the mirror; but when we have perceived the mirror itself as well,
  284  at once the image that is carried from us into it
  285  arrives, and, thrown back, revisits our eyes,
  286  and, driving another air before it, rolls it on,
  287  and brings it about that we see this air before the image, and therefore
  288  it seems to stand removed from the mirror by just so much.
  289  Therefore again and again it is right to wonder not at all {{NOTE: a line appears to be lost after this one}}
       [lacuna]
  290  at those that give back a sight from the level surface of mirrors,
  291  since in either case the thing is brought about by two airs.
  292  Now, that part of our limbs which is on the right
  293  comes to be seen in mirrors on the left, because
  294  when the image, coming, strikes against the mirror's flat,
  295  it is not turned about unharmed, but is dashed straight
  296  backward, as if someone, before it was dry,
  297  should dash a clay mask against a pillar or a beam, {{NOTE: cretea persona: a mask of wet clay, which takes the impression inside out}}
  298  and it should at once keep its features straight in front
  299  and, dashed back, should press out its own form in reverse.
  300  It will come about that the eye that was the right before
  301  is now the left, and the left in exchange the right.
  302  It comes about too that an image is passed from mirror to mirror,
  303  so that even five or six images are accustomed to be made.
  304  For whatever things lie hidden back in the inner part of a house,
  305  even so, however winding and deep withdrawn the way,
  306  all can be brought out through crooked passages
  307  and by many mirrors be seen to be within the house.
  308  So thoroughly does the image shine across from mirror to mirror,
  309  and when it is given as left, it comes about that it becomes right again,
  310  then it turns back again and returns to the same.
  311  Further, whatever little sides of mirrors there are
  312  endowed with a curvature like that of our own side
  313  send back to us images that are right on that account,
  314  either because the image is carried across from mirror to mirror
  315  and from there, dashed twice, flies to us, or else because
  316  the image is whirled around as it comes, since
  317  the curved shape of the mirror teaches it to turn toward us.
  318  Further, images walk forward in step with us, and set
  319  their foot down with ours, you would believe, and imitate our gesture,
  320  because, from whatever part of the mirror you withdraw,
  321  from there at once the images cannot return;
  322  since nature compels all things to be carried back
  323  and to leap back from things, returned at equal angles.
  324  Further, the eyes shun bright things and avoid looking at them.
  325  The sun even blinds them, if you persist in straining toward it,
  326  because its own force is great, and from on high
  327  images are carried through the clear air
  328  and strike the eyes, throwing their structure into confusion.
  329  Further, whatever brightness is keen often scorches
  330  the eyes, because it holds many seeds of fire
  331  which, working their way in, beget pain in the eyes.
  332  Further, all things turn sallow that are looked upon
  333  by the jaundiced, since seeds of sallowness from their body {{NOTE: arquati: those with jaundice, the "rainbow disease" (arquatus morbus), from its color}}
  334  flow forth in numbers to meet the images of things,
  335  and many, finally, are mingled in their eyes,
  336  which with their contagion paint all things with pallor.
  337  Again, from darkness we see the things that are in light
  338  because, when the nearer air of the gloom,
  339  the black air, enters the eyes first and takes hold of them open,
  340  at once the shining, bright air follows after,
  341  which as it were purges them and scatters the black shadows
  342  of that air; for this one is by many degrees
  343  more mobile, by many finer, and more powerful.
  344  As soon as it has filled the eyes' passages with light
  345  and opened those that before the air had laid siege to,
  346  the black air, at once there follow the images of things
  347  that are placed in light, and provoke us to see.
  348  This, contrarily, we cannot do from light into darkness,
  349  because the air of gloom, coming after,
  350  follows thicker, and fills up all the openings
  351  and besieges the eyes' passages, so that the images
  352  of no things at all can be cast in and move them.
  353  And when from far off we discern the square towers of a city,
  354  it comes about that they often seem round
  355  for this reason: every angle is seen blunted at a distance,
  356  or rather is not seen at all, and its blow
  357  is lost, and its stroke does not glide through to our sight,
  358  because while the images are carried through much air,
  359  the air by frequent collisions forces the blow to grow dull.
  360  When in this way every angle at once has escaped the sense,
  361  it comes about that the stone structures look as if turned on a lathe;
  362  not, though, like things that are near at hand and truly round,
  363  but they seem a little like them, as if in shadowy sketch.
  364  A shadow too seems to us to move in the sun
  365  and to follow our footsteps and imitate our gesture,
  366  if you believe that air deprived of light can
  367  walk forward, following the motions and gesture of men;
  368  for it can be nothing else but air bereft of light,
  369  this thing that we are accustomed to call shadow.
  370  Doubtless, because the earth, in places in fixed order,
  371  is deprived of the sun's light wherever we, moving,
  372  get in its way, and is filled again where we have left it,
  373  it comes about that what was the shadow of the body
  374  seems to have followed us all along in the same direction.
  375  For always new light of rays pours itself forth,
  376  and the first perishes, as if wool were drawn into fire.
  377  Therefore the earth is easily both stripped of light
  378  and filled again, and washes its black shadows from itself.
