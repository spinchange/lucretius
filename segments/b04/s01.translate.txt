  143  Now, by how easy and how swift a principle these are produced,
  144  and how they flow perpetually from things and slip off and withdraw, {{NOTE: a lacuna follows; the opening of the account of how the images form is lost, and 145 resumes mid-argument}}
       [lacuna]
  145  for always there is something streaming off the surface of things
  146  for them to cast away. And when this reaches other things,
  147  it passes through, as above all through cloth; but when it meets rough rocks
  148  or comes upon the matter of wood, there at once
  149  it is torn apart, so that it can give back no image.
  150  But when things that are bright and dense are set against it,
  151  as above all a mirror is, none of this befalls;
  152  for it can neither pass through them, as through cloth, nor yet
  153  be torn apart; a safety their smoothness never fails to secure.
  154  And that is why the images stream back to us from there.
  155  And however suddenly, at whatever time, you set
  156  any thing against a mirror, an image appears;
  157  so that you may know there flow perpetually from the outermost body
  158  thin textures of things and thin shapes.
  159  Therefore many images are produced in a brief space,
  160  so that the origin of these things may rightly be called swift.
  161  And just as the sun must send up many lights
  162  in a brief space, so that all things may be perpetually full,
  163  so likewise, on a like principle, it must be that from things
  164  in a moment of time images of things are carried,
  165  many, in many ways, in all directions on every side;
  166  since to whatever quarter we turn a mirror's face,
  167  things answer there with like shape and like color.
  168  Further, when the weather of the sky has just been at its clearest,
  169  quite suddenly it turns foully turbid,
  170  so that you would think all the darkness on every side had left
  171  Acheron and filled the great caverns of the sky.
  172  So utterly, when the hideous night of storm clouds has risen,
  173  do the faces of black Terror hang over us from above; {{NOTE: Formido, Terror, personified as a figure with faces, like the shapes in the clouds at 4.136-140}}
  174  yet how tiny a part of these an image is, there is no one
  175  who could say, or render an account of it in words.
  176  Come now, with what swift motion the images are carried,
  177  and what mobility is granted them as they swim through the air,
  178  so that a long distance is worn away in a brief hour,
  179  toward whatever place each strains with its diverse bent,
  180  I shall set out in verses sweet-spoken rather than many; {{NOTE: 180-182 recur at 4.909-911; the English repeats them verbatim there}}
  181  as the small song of the swan is better than that clamor
  182  of cranes scattered among the high clouds of the south wind.
  183  To begin with, very often one may see that things light and made
  184  of minute bodies are swift.
  185  Of this kind is the light of the sun and its heat,
  186  because they are made of minute first bodies
  187  which are, as it were, hammered on, and through the interval of air
  188  do not hesitate to pass, driven on by the blow that follows;
  189  for at once light is supplied by light,
  190  and flash is goaded on by flash, as if by a team in tandem. {{NOTE: protelo, a team of oxen yoked one behind another, each pulling the next}}
  191  Therefore images, on a like principle, must
  192  be able to run across a space beyond telling
  193  in a moment of time, first because there is a very small cause
  194  far behind them to carry them forward and drive them on,
  195  and, what is more, they are carried with so winged a lightness;
  196  next, because they are sent out endowed with so rare
  197  a texture that they can easily penetrate any things
  198  and, as it were, seep through the interval of air.
  199  Further, if those small bodies of things that from deep within
  200  and from far inside are sent out, as the sun's light
  201  and heat are, are seen in a moment of the day to glide
  202  and spread themselves through the whole space of heaven
  203  and fly across sea and lands and flood the sky,
  204  what then of those that stand ready at the very front,
  205  when they are cast off and nothing hinders their release?
  206  Do you not see how much more swiftly and farther they must go
  207  and run across many times the space of place in the same
  208  time in which the sun's lights spread through the sky?
  209  This too above all seems a true proof
  210  of how swift the motion is with which images of things are carried:
  211  that as soon as a gleaming sheet of water is laid down
  212  under the open sky, at once, when the sky is starry, the calm
  213  stars of the world answer, radiant in the water.
  214  Now do you see, then, in what a moment of time an image
  215  falls from the shores of the ether to the shores of the earth?
  216  Therefore again and again you must confess that bodies
  217  are sent out that strike the eyes and provoke the sight.
  218  And from certain things odors flow perpetually,
  219  as cold from rivers, heat from the sun, spray from the waves
  220  of the sea, eater of walls along the shores,
  221  and various voices never cease to fly through the air.
  222  Finally, a moisture of salty flavor often comes into the mouth
  223  when we walk beside the sea, and when we watch
  224  wormwood being diluted and mixed, its bitterness touches us.
  225  So true is it that from all things each thing is carried off
  226  in a stream, and sent away in all directions on every side,
  227  and no delay, no rest is granted in the flowing,
  228  since we perpetually feel, and may always
  229  see all things, smell them, and sense them sounding.
  230  Further, since a certain shape, handled with the hands
  231  in the dark, is known to be the same as the one
  232  seen in the light and the clear brightness, it must be
  233  that touch and sight are moved by a closely similar cause.
  234  Now then, if we feel a square thing and it
  235  stirs us in the dark, what thing in the light could
  236  fall square upon our sight, if not its image?
  237  Therefore the cause of seeing is seen to lie
  238  in images, and without them no thing can be seen.
  239  Now these which I call images of things are carried
  240  everywhere, and are cast out spread in all directions;
  241  but because we can see with the eyes alone,
  242  it comes about that wherever we turn our gaze, all things
  243  there strike against it with shape and color.
  244  And how far each thing stands away from us, the image
  245  makes us see, and takes care that we distinguish it;
  246  for when it is sent off, at once it thrusts and drives before it
  247  whatever air is placed between itself and the eyes,
  248  and so all this air glides through our eyes
  249  and, as it were, sweeps the pupils, and so passes through.
  250  That is why we see how far off each thing
  251  stands. And the more air is driven on before it,
  252  and the longer the breeze that sweeps our eyes,
  253  the farther off each thing is seen to stand removed.
  254  Of course these things come about on a principle supremely swift,
  255  so that we see what a thing is, and at once how far off it stands.
  256  In these matters it must not be thought at all a wonder
  257  why, though the images that strike the eyes cannot
  258  be seen one by one, the things themselves are perceived.
  259  For when the wind too lashes us little by little, and when
  260  sharp cold flows, we are not used to feel each single
  261  particle of the wind and of its cold,
  262  but rather the whole at once, and we see blows come about
  263  then on our body just as if some thing were
  264  lashing it and giving a sense of its own body outside.
  265  Again, when we strike a stone with a finger, we touch
  266  the very outermost surface of the rock, its uppermost color,
  267  yet we do not feel that by touch; rather we feel
  268  the hardness itself, deep within the rock, far down.
