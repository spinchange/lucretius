  615  As for how we perceive flavor, the tongue and palate
  616  hold a little more reasoning in them, and more labor.
  617  To begin with, we perceive flavor in the mouth when, chewing,
  618  we press it out of food, as with a sponge full of water,
  619  if someone happens to begin to squeeze it in the hand and dry it.
  620  Then all that we press out is spread through the passages of the palate
  621  and through the winding pores of the loose-woven tongue.
  622  So when the bodies of the oozing flavor are smooth,
  623  they touch sweetly, and sweetly handle all
  624  the moist precincts sweating round about the tongue;
  625  but on the contrary they prick the sense and tear it as they come,
  626  the more so the more each is filled with roughness.
  627  Next, the pleasure from flavor lies within the bounds of the palate;
  628  but when it has plunged down through the throat,
  629  there is no pleasure, while it is all being spread into the limbs;
  630  nor does it matter at all on what food the body is fed,
  631  so long as what you take you can digest and spread
  632  through the limbs, and keep the stomach's full and even tenor.
  633  Now, so that we may see how one food is for some, another for others,
  634  I shall explain, and why what is harsh and bitter to some
  635  can yet seem to others exceedingly sweet,
  636  and why in these things there is such distance and difference
  637  that what is food to some is to others sharp poison;
  638  for indeed there is a snake which, touched by a man's spittle,
  639  perishes and destroys itself by gnawing at itself.
  640  Further, hellebore is sharp poison to us,
  641  but to goats and to quails it adds fat.
  642  So that you can learn by what means this comes about,
  643  first it is fitting to remember what we said before,
  644  that seeds are held mixed in things in many ways.
  645  Further, all living creatures whatever that take food,
  646  just as they are unlike outwardly, and, kind by kind,
  647  the outer contour of their limbs confines them,
  648  so too they consist of seeds and vary in their shape.
  649  Again, since the seeds differ, it must be that the intervals
  650  and the paths, which we call pores, differ too
  651  in all the limbs, and in the mouth and the palate itself.
  652  Therefore some must be smaller and some larger,
  653  for some creatures they must be three-cornered, for others square,
  654  many round, some many-angled in many ways.
  655  For as the order of the shapes and their motions demand,
  656  so the shapes of the pores must differ,
  657  and the paths vary, according as the texture confines them.
  658  So when what is sweet to some becomes bitter to others,
  659  for the one to whom it is sweet the smoothest bodies must
  660  enter the passages of the palate caressingly,
  661  but for those to whom the same thing is bitter within,
  662  rough and hooked bodies, doubtless, make their way into the throat.
  663  Now it is easy from these things to understand each case.
  664  For indeed when fever has risen in someone, the bile prevailing,
  665  or the force of disease has been roused by some other means,
  666  then the whole body is thrown into disorder, and all
  667  the positions of the first-beginnings are changed;
  668  so it comes about that bodies which before suited the sense
  669  now do not suit it, and others are more fitted,
  670  which, making their way in, can beget a bitter sense;
  671  for both kinds are mixed together in the flavor of honey,
  672  a thing we have often shown you above already.
  673  Come now, how the touch of odor strikes the nostrils
  674  I shall set out. First, there must be many things
  675  from which a varied stream of odors rolls flowing,
  676  and we must suppose they flow and are sent out and scattered everywhere;
  677  but one odor is more fitted to some creatures, another to others,
  678  because of their unlike forms. And so through the air
  679  bees are drawn, however far, by the odor of honey,
  680  and vultures by carcasses; then, where the cloven hoof of beasts
  681  has carried its steps, the far-reaching force of hounds
  682  leads on, and the human smell is scented from far away
  683  by the savior of the citadel of Romulus' sons, the white goose. {{NOTE: the sacred geese of Juno on the Capitol, whose cackling woke the defenders against the Gauls' night attack (Livy 5.47)}}
  684  So one scent, given to some, another to others, leads each to its own
  685  pasture, and makes it spring back from foul poison,
  686  and in this way the generations of wild beasts are preserved.
  687  This odor itself, then, whatever it is that provokes the nostrils,
  688  is such that one can be sent farther than another;
  689  but still none of them is carried as far
  690  as sound, as voice, not to speak now of the things
  691  that strike the sight of the eyes and provoke vision.
  692  For it comes wandering and slowly, and perishes on the way,
  693  little by little, easily torn apart into the breezes of air;
  694  first, because it is barely sent out from deep within the thing;
  695  for that odors flow and withdraw from the depths of things
  696  is shown by this, that all things seem to smell more when broken,
  697  when crushed, when dissolved by fire.
  698  Next, you may see that it is made of larger
  699  first-beginnings than voice, since through walls of stone
  700  it does not pass, where voice and sound are commonly carried.
  701  Therefore you will see as well that what smells is not so easy
  702  to track down, in what region it is placed;
  703  for the blow cools as it lingers through the air,
  704  and the messengers of things do not run down hot to the sense.
  705  And so dogs often go astray and search for the tracks.
  706  Nor yet is this found only in odors and in the class
  707  of flavors; likewise the appearances and colors of things
  708  do not all so suit the senses of all
  709  that some are not harsher to some to look at.
  710  Indeed, the cock, who with clapping wings drives out the night
  711  and is wont to call the dawn with ringing voice,
  712  ravening lions are not able to stand against
  713  and look upon: so instantly do they remember flight. {{NOTE: a common ancient belief, also in Pliny, Natural History 8.52 and 10.47}}
  714  Doubtless because there are in the body of cocks certain
  715  seeds which, when they are sent into the eyes of lions,
  716  bore into the pupils and give sharp pain,
  717  so that, fierce as they are, they cannot hold out against them,
  718  although these things cannot harm our sight at all,
  719  either because they do not get in, or because, once they do,
  720  a free way out of the eyes is given them, so that by lingering
  721  they cannot harm the eyes in any part.
  722  Come now, hear what things move the mind, and from where
  723  the things that come into the mind come, learn in a few words.
  724  First, I say this: that images of things wander,
  725  many, in many ways, in all directions on every side,
  726  thin images, which easily join with one another in the air
  727  when they meet, like a spider's web or a leaf of gold.
  728  For indeed these are much thinner in texture
  729  than those that take hold of the eyes and provoke vision,
  730  since these pass through the loose openings of the body and stir
  731  the thin nature of the mind within, and provoke sense.
  732  And so we see Centaurs and the limbs of Scyllas
  733  and the dog-faces of Cerberus, and images of those
  734  whose bones, after death, the earth embraces; {{NOTE: repeats 1.135 verbatim in the Latin; the English repeats its own line}}
  735  since images of every kind are carried everywhere,
  736  some that come to be of their own accord in the air itself,
  737  some that withdraw from all manner of things,
  738  and those that are made by combining the shapes of these.
  739  For certainly no image of a Centaur comes from a living one,
  740  since no such nature of living thing ever existed;
  741  but when by chance the images of horse and man meet,
  742  they easily cling together at once, as we said before,
  743  because of their subtle nature and thin texture.
  744  Other things of this kind are created by the same principle.
  745  And since these move nimbly with the utmost lightness,
  746  as I showed before, any one subtle image easily
  747  moves our mind with a single blow;
  748  for the mind is thin, and wonderfully mobile itself.
  749  That these things happen as I tell, you may easily learn from this:
  750  insofar as the one is like the other, what we see with the mind
  751  and with the eyes, it must come about by a like principle.
  752  Now, then, since I have shown that I see lions, say,
  753  by means of images, whatever images provoke the eyes,
  754  you may know that the mind is moved by a like principle
  755  by images of lions and the rest, which it sees just as
  756  well as the eyes, except that it perceives thinner ones.
  757  Nor by any other principle, when sleep has spread out the limbs,
  758  does the thought of the mind keep watch, except that the same images
  759  provoke our minds as when we are awake,
  760  so much so that we seem surely to see one
  761  whom, life left behind, death and the earth now possess.
  762  Nature compels this to happen because all
  763  the senses of the body, blocked, rest throughout the limbs,
  764  and cannot refute the false with things that are true.
  765  Further, memory lies idle and languishes in sleep,
  766  and does not protest that this one, in the grip of death and doom
  767  long since, is not the one the mind believes it sees alive.
  768  For the rest, it is no wonder that images move
  769  and throw their arms in rhythm, and their other limbs;
  770  for it happens that in dreams an image seems to do this.
  771  For indeed, when the first perishes and a second is born after
  772  in another posture, the earlier seems to have changed its gesture.
  773  Of course we must suppose that this happens by a swift principle:
  774  so great is the mobility, and so great the abundance of things,
  775  and so great, in any one moment we can perceive,
  776  the abundance of particles, that it can supply them. {{NOTE: particulae, Lucretius' diminutive, used here only; not the technical primordia or corpora, so rendered "particles"}}
