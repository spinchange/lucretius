    1  I range through the pathless places of the Pierides, trodden before {{NOTE: 4.1-25 repeat 1.926-950 almost word for word; the English repeats the Book 1 lines; the Pierides are the Muses, named from Pieria}}
    2  by no one's foot. It is a joy to come to untouched springs
    3  and drink from them, a joy to pluck new flowers
    4  and to seek for my head a crown of distinction from there
    5  where the Muses have veiled no one's temples before;
    6  first because I teach of great things, and go on
    7  to free the mind from the tight-tied knots of religion,
    8  then because on a dark matter I compose such lucid
    9  verses, touching all of it with the Muses' charm.
   10  For this too seems not to be without good reason;
   11  for just as physicians, with foul wormwood for children,
   12  when they try to give it, first touch the rims round the cups
   13  with the sweet and golden liquid of honey,
   14  so that the unforeseeing age of children may be fooled
   15  as far as the lips, and meanwhile drink down the bitter
   16  juice of wormwood, and, deceived, may not be undone,
   17  but rather, restored by such means, may grow strong,
   18  so I now, since this reasoning often seems
   19  rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd
   20  shrinks back from it, have wished, in sweet-speaking
   21  Pierian song, to set out for you our reasoning
   22  and, as it were, to touch it with the Muses' sweet honey,
   23  if by such means I might perhaps hold your mind
   24  on our verses, while you perceive the whole
   25  nature of things and feel its usefulness through and through.
   26  But since I have taught what the origins of all things
   27  are like, and how, differing in their various shapes,
   28  they fly of their own accord, driven by eternal motion,
   29  and in what way each thing can be created from them,
   30  I shall now begin to treat for you what bears closely on these things:
   31  that there exist what we call the images of things,
   32  which are to be named, as it were, films or bark; {{NOTE: 26-44 preserve two versions of the same transition (30-31 = 36-37); Bailey prints both, and the translation follows him}}
   33  and since I have taught what the nature of the mind is,
   34  and from what things, joined with the body, it is made and thrives,
   35  and in what way, torn apart, it returns to its first-beginnings,
   36  I shall now begin to treat for you what bears closely on these things:
   37  that there exist what we call the images of things,
   38  because the image carries a likeness and form like that thing's
   39  from whose body, whatever it is, it is said to be shed and wander;
   40  which, like films torn from the outermost body of things,
   41  fly this way and that through the air,
   42  and these same, meeting us when we are awake, terrify
   43  our minds, and in sleep too, when often we behold shapes
   44  strange to see, and the images of those who lack the light,
   45  which have often roused us in horror as we lay languid
   46  in sleep: lest we should think by chance that spirits escape {{NOTE: 45-53 are widely thought to be misplaced or a remnant of the earlier draft of this transition}}
   47  from Acheron, or that shades fly among the living,
   48  or that something of us can be left behind after death,
   49  when the body and the nature of the mind, destroyed together,
   50  have gone their separate ways, each into its own first-beginnings.
   51  I say, therefore, that effigies of things and thin shapes
   52  are sent off from things, from their outermost bark;
   53  which one may come to know from this, however dull one's heart.
   54  To begin with, since among things open to view many things
   55  send off bodies, some diffused loosely abroad,
   56  as logs send off smoke and fires heat,
   57  and some more closely woven and dense, as when at times
   58  cicadas put off their smooth coats in summer,
   59  and when calves at birth send off films
   60  from the outermost body, and again when the slippery serpent
   61  strips off its garment among the thorns; for we often see
   62  the brambles decked with their fluttering spoils.
   63  And since these things happen, a thin image too must
   64  be sent off from things, from the outermost body of things.
   65  For why those should fall and withdraw from things more
   66  than those that are thin, there is no power to open one's mouth;
   67  especially since on the surface of things there are bodies
   68  many and minute, which could be thrown off in the same order
   69  they had before and keep the outline of the form,
   70  and much more swiftly, inasmuch as they can be less hindered,
   71  being few and placed in the very front.
   72  For certainly we see many things throw off and give out,
   73  not only from the depth and from within, as we said before,
   74  but from the surface, often, the very color too.
   75  And this is commonly done by awnings, yellow and red
   76  and dark blue, when stretched over great theaters
   77  they flap and quiver, spread out on masts and beams;
   78  for there the assembly seated in the tiers beneath, and all
   79  the face of the stage, the fathers and the mothers down below,
   80  they stain, and make them ripple with their color.
   81  And the more closely the walls of the theater are shut in
   82  all around, the more everything inside, steeped in charm,
   83  laughs, now that the light of day is pent within.
   84  Therefore, since linens send off a dye from their surface,
   85  each thing too must send off thin effigies,
   86  since in both cases they are thrown from the surface.
   87  There are therefore by now sure traces of forms,
   88  which commonly fly about, endowed with a fine thread,
   89  and cannot be seen one by one, apart.
   90  Further, every smell, smoke, heat, and other things
   91  of the kind stream out from things diffused for this reason:
   92  because while they come out from the depths, risen to the outside,
   93  they are torn apart on their winding path, and there are no straight
   94  mouths to the roads by which, once risen, they can strive to get out.
   95  But on the other hand, when a thin film of surface color
   96  is thrown off, there is nothing that can tear it apart,
   97  since it is ready at hand, placed in the very front.
   98  Finally, whatever images appear to us in mirrors,
   99  in water, and in every shining surface, must,
  100  since they are endowed with a likeness like that of things,
  101  consist of images sent off from those things.
  102  For why those should fall and withdraw from things more
  103  than those that are thin, there is no power to open one's mouth.
  104  There are therefore thin effigies of forms, and like them,
  105  which, though no one can make them out
  106  one by one, yet thrown back by constant and repeated rebounding
  107  return a sight from the level surface of mirrors;
  108  and in no other way, it seems, could they be preserved
  109  so that shapes so like each thing are returned.
  110  Come now, how thin a nature the image is made of,
  111  learn. And in the first place, since the first-beginnings are so far
  112  beneath our senses, and so much smaller
  113  than those things which the eyes first begin to be unable to see,
  114  yet now, so that I may confirm this too, how fine the origins
  115  of all things are, learn in a few words.
  116  First, there are living creatures already so tiny that a third part {{NOTE: the source's "corum" is a misprint for "eorum", "of them"}}
  117  of them could by no means be seen.
  118  What must one think any one of their entrails is like!
  119  What of the heart's ball, or the eye's? What of the limbs? The joints?
  120  How tiny they are! And what, further, of each first-beginning
  121  of which the spirit and the nature of the mind must consist?
  122  Do you not see how fine they are, and how minute?
