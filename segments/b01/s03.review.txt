  483  Further, bodies are partly the first-beginnings of things,
  484  partly those that are formed from a union of beginnings. {{NOTE: principia, Lucretius's synonym for primordia; "beginnings" keeps the two Latin words apart}}
  485  But those that are the first-beginnings of things no force
  486  can quench; for it is by solid body that they prevail.
  487  And yet it seems hard to believe that anything
  488  among things can be found to be of solid body.
  489  For the thunderbolt of heaven passes through the walls of houses,
  490  as do shouting and voices; iron grows white-hot in fire,
  491  and rocks leap apart in fierce and seething heat;
  492  when the hardness of gold is shaken loose and dissolves in heat,
  493  then the ice of bronze, conquered by flame, melts away;
  494  heat seeps through silver, and so does penetrating cold,
  495  since, gripping cups in hand as is the custom, we have felt both
  496  when the dew of water was poured in from above.
  497  To such a degree there seems to be nothing solid in things.
  498  But since true reasoning and the nature of things nonetheless
  499  compel us, attend, while in a few verses we make it plain
  500  that there are things which consist of solid and eternal body,
  501  which we teach are the seeds of things and their first-beginnings,
  502  from which the whole sum of things now stands created.
  503  To begin with, since the twofold nature of two things,
  504  far unlike each other, has been found to exist,
  505  of body and of place, in which each thing is carried on,
  506  each of necessity must exist by itself and pure.
  507  For wherever there is empty space, which we call void,
  508  there body is not; and further, wherever body
  509  holds its place, there empty void by no means exists.
  510  The first bodies, therefore, are solid and without void.
  511  Further, since there is void in things that have been born,
  512  solid matter must stand around it;
  513  nor can anything be proved by true reasoning
  514  to hide void in its body and to hold it within,
  515  unless you grant that what encloses it stands solid.
  516  And that, further, can be nothing but a union
  517  of matter, such as could enclose the void in things.
  518  Matter, therefore, which consists of solid body,
  519  can be eternal, while all else is dissolved.
  520  Then again, if there were nothing that stood empty as void, {{NOTE: the text as printed has vocaret; most editors read vacaret, "was empty", and the translation follows that}}
  521  all would be solid; unless, on the other hand, there were definite bodies
  522  to fill up whatever places they occupied,
  523  all the space there is would stand as empty void.
  524  Body, therefore, is plainly marked off from void
  525  in alternation, since neither does the wholly full exist
  526  nor, again, the wholly empty; there are, then, definite bodies
  527  that can mark off empty space from full.
  528  These can neither be dissolved when struck by blows from outside
  529  nor, again, be unravelled by being pierced to the core,
  530  nor, assailed in any other way, can they be made to give;
  531  as I showed you above, a little while before.
  532  For nothing, it seems, can be crushed without void,
  533  nor broken, nor split in two by cutting,
  534  nor take in moisture, nor likewise seeping cold,
  535  nor penetrating fire, by which all things are undone.
  536  And the more void each thing holds within it,
  537  the more it gives way when assailed to the core by these.
  538  Therefore if the first bodies are solid and without void,
  539  as I have taught, they must be eternal.
  540  Further, unless matter had been eternal,
  541  before now all things would have gone back utterly to nothing,
  542  and from nothing would have been reborn whatever we see.
  543  But since I taught above that nothing can be created
  544  from nothing, nor what has been born be called back to nothing,
  545  the first-beginnings must be of immortal body,
  546  into which all things can be dissolved at their last hour,
  547  so that matter may be at hand for the renewal of things.
  548  The first-beginnings, therefore, are of solid singleness,
  549  nor in any other way could they have been kept through the ages
  550  to renew things, from time already infinite.
  551  Finally, if nature had set no limit
  552  to the breaking of things, by now the bodies of matter
  553  would have been so worn down by the breaking of past ages
  554  that nothing conceived from them, from a fixed time on,
  555  could reach the furthest goal of its age.
  556  For we see that anything can be dissolved more quickly
  557  than made again; therefore whatever the long span of days,
  558  the infinite age of all the time gone before,
  559  had broken till now, confounding and dissolving it,
  560  could never be repaired in the time remaining.
  561  But as it is, a fixed limit to breaking, plainly,
  562  remains assigned, since we see each thing made again,
  563  and at the same time fixed seasons, kind by kind, stand set
  564  for things, in which they can reach the flower of their age.
  565  Add to this that, although the bodies of matter
  566  are most solid, an account can still be given of all things
  567  that come to be soft, air, water, earth, vapors,
  568  how they come to be and by what force each is carried on,
  569  since void is mixed, once and for all, in things.
  570  But if, on the contrary, the first-beginnings of things were soft,
  571  how hard flints and iron could be created
  572  no account could be given; for utterly all
  573  nature will lack a beginning for its foundation.
  574  They are, therefore, strong in solid singleness,
  575  and by a denser union of them all things
  576  can be packed tight and show forth sturdy strength.
  577  Further, if no limit has been set to the breaking
  578  of bodies, still, out of eternal time, certain bodies
  579  must even now survive in things,
  580  such as have not yet been assailed by any danger.
  581  But since they are endowed with a fragile nature,
  582  it is inconsistent that they could have lasted through eternal time,
  583  harried through the ages by numberless blows.
  584  Finally, since a limit is set for things, kind by kind,
  585  to their growing and their holding on to life,
  586  and since what each can do by nature's covenants, {{NOTE: foedera naturai, first occurrence of the phrase; rendered "nature's covenants" throughout}}
  587  and what, further, each cannot, stands decreed,
  588  nor is anything changed, but all things are so constant
  589  that the varied birds, all of them in their order,
  590  show the markings of their kind upon their bodies,
  591  they must surely also have a body of unchangeable
  592  matter; for if the first-beginnings of things
  593  could be overcome in some way and be changed,
  594  it would then be uncertain, too, what can come to be
  595  and what cannot, and how at last each thing's power is bounded, {{NOTE: 1.594-596 repeat 1.75-77; the English repeats its own lines}}
  596  by what principle, and its boundary-stone set deep,
  597  nor could the generations, kind by kind, so often repeat
  598  the nature, the manners, the diet, and the movements of their parents.
