  875  Here there is left a certain slender chance of hiding,
  876  which Anaxagoras takes up for himself, holding that every
  877  thing lies hidden, mixed in among all things, but that one thing
  878  appears, the one of which the most parts are mixed in
  879  and lie more ready to hand and set in the forefront.
  880  But this is driven far away from true reasoning;
  881  for it would then follow that grain too, when by the menacing
  882  strength of the stone it is crushed, would often send out some sign
  883  of blood, or of some other thing that is nourished in our body.
  884  When we grind them stone upon stone, it would be fitting for gore
  885  by a like principle to ooze from grasses too, and often,
  886  and for them to send out juices, sweet drops of a savor like
  887  the savor of milk in the udder of the wool-bearing ewe;
  888  and, of course, that when clods of earth are often crumbled,
  889  kinds of grasses and grains and leaves should be seen
  890  parceled out through the earth, lying hidden in tiny bits;
  891  and finally, that ash and smoke should be seen in wood
  892  when it was snapped across, and tiny fires lying hidden.
  893  Since the plain fact shows that none of these things happens,
  894  you may know that things are not so mixed in things,
  895  but rather that seeds, mixed in many ways, must lie hidden
  896  in things, seeds common to many things.
  897  "But often on great mountains," you say, "it happens that the tall
  898  trees' neighboring tops are rubbed at their summits
  899  against each other, as the strong south winds force them to it,
  900  until a flower of flame springs up and they blaze."
  901  Indeed so; and yet fire is not implanted in the wood,
  902  but there are many seeds of burning, which, through the rubbing,
  903  when they have flowed together, create fires in the forests.
  904  For if flame, already made, were hidden in the forests,
  905  the fires could not be concealed for any length of time;
  906  they would consume the forests everywhere, burn up the groves.
  907  Now do you see, then, what we said a little before,
  908  that it often matters very much, for these same first-beginnings,
  909  with what and in what position they are held together,
  910  and what motions they give and receive among themselves,
  911  and that these same, a little changed among themselves, create
  912  fires and wood? Just as the words themselves, too,
  913  differ by elements a little changed among themselves,
  914  when we mark wood and fires with distinct sounds. {{NOTE: the Latin words, ligna and ignes, are made of nearly the same letters; the point of "elements" as letters is lost in English}}
  915  Finally, if you think that whatever you see in things plain to view
  916  cannot come to be unless you imagine the bodies of matter
  917  endowed with a nature like theirs,
  918  on this reasoning the first-beginnings of things perish for you:
  919  it will come about that they guffaw, shaken with trembling laughter,
  920  and wet their faces and cheeks with salt tears.
  921  Come now, for the rest: learn, and hear more clearly.
  922  Nor does it escape my mind how dark these things are; but with a sharp
  923  thyrsus the great hope of praise has struck my heart {{NOTE: the thyrsus, the ivy-wreathed wand of Bacchus, whose touch brought on divine frenzy}}
  924  and at the same time has driven into my breast sweet love
  925  of the Muses, inspired by which now, with vigorous mind,
  926  I range through the pathless places of the Pierides, trodden before {{NOTE: lines 1.926-950 recur almost word for word as the proem of Book 4 (4.1-25); the Pierides are the Muses, named from Pieria}}
  927  by no one's foot. It is a joy to come to untouched springs
  928  and drink from them, a joy to pluck new flowers
  929  and to seek for my head a crown of distinction from there
  930  where the Muses have veiled no one's temples before;
  931  first because I teach of great things, and go on
  932  to free the mind from the tight-tied knots of religion,
  933  then because on a dark matter I compose such lucid
  934  verses, touching all of it with the Muses' charm.
  935  For this too seems not to be without good reason;
  936  but just as physicians, with foul wormwood for children,
  937  when they try to give it, first touch the rims round the cups
  938  with the sweet and golden liquid of honey,
  939  so that the unforeseeing age of children may be fooled
  940  as far as the lips, and meanwhile drink down the bitter
  941  juice of wormwood, and, deceived, may not be undone,
  942  but rather, restored by such means, may grow strong,
  943  so I now, since this reasoning often seems
  944  rather grim to those who have not handled it, and the crowd
  945  shrinks back from it, have wished, in sweet-speaking
  946  Pierian song, to set out for you our reasoning
  947  and, as it were, to touch it with the Muses' sweet honey,
  948  if by such means I might perhaps hold your mind
  949  on our verses, while you see deep into the whole
  950  nature of things, the shape in which it stands composed.
  951  But since I have taught that the most solid bodies of matter
  952  fly about, unconquered, forever through the ages,
  953  come now, whether there is some limit to their sum
  954  or not, let us unfold; likewise, what has been found to be void,
  955  or place and space, in which all things are carried on,
  956  let us see clearly whether the whole of it is bounded utterly
  957  or lies open, immeasurable and vast and deep.
  958  All that exists, then, is bounded along none of its ways;
  959  for then it would have to have an outermost point.
  960  Further, it seems that nothing can have an outermost point
  961  unless there is something beyond to bound it, so that there seems to be
  962  a point past which the nature of our sense cannot follow.
  963  As it is, since we must admit there is nothing outside the sum,
  964  it has no outermost point; it lacks, therefore, end and measure.
  965  Nor does it matter in which of its regions you stand;
  966  so true it is that, whatever place each one takes, on all
  967  sides alike one leaves the whole as infinite as before.
  968  Further, if now we should set down as bounded
  969  all the space that exists, if someone should run out to its shores,
  970  last of all, at the very edge, and hurl a flying spear,
  971  would you rather that, whirled by strong force,
  972  it goes where it was sent and flies far off,
  973  or do you judge that something can stop it and stand in its way?
  974  For you must admit and adopt one or the other.
  975  Either one shuts off your escape, and forces you to grant
  976  that the whole, exempt from any end, lies open.
  977  For whether there is something that blocks it and brings it about
  978  that it does not reach where it was sent and lodge itself at its end,
  979  or whether it is carried on outside, it has not set out from the end.
  980  In this way I will follow, and, wherever you place
  981  the outermost shores, I will ask: what in the end becomes of the spear?
  982  It will come about that no end can stand anywhere,
  983  and the scope for flight will always draw out the escape.
  984  Further, if all the space of the whole sum
  985  were shut in on every side and stood within fixed shores
  986  and were bounded, by now the store of matter
  987  would, by its solid weights, have flowed from all sides to the bottom,
  988  nor could anything be carried on beneath the canopy of the sky,
  989  nor would there be a sky at all, nor the light of the sun,
  990  since all matter would lie heaped up,
  991  settling down from infinite time already past.
  992  But as it is, plainly, no rest is given to the bodies
  993  of the beginnings, because there is no bottom at all {{NOTE: principia, Lucretius's variant for primordia; rendered "beginnings" to keep the Latin distinct}}
  994  where they might, as it were, flow together and take up their seats.
  995  Always, in ceaseless motion, all things are carried on
  996  in every part, and from beneath are furnished,
  997  out of the infinite, the swift bodies of matter.
  998  Finally, before our eyes one thing is seen to bound another;
  999  air walls off the hills, and mountains the air,
 1000  land bounds the sea, and the sea in turn bounds all lands;
 1001  but the whole, indeed, there is nothing outside to bound.
