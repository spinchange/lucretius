464-468. Latin: cum dicunt Tyndaridem raptam (= the carrying-off of Helen) et gentis subactas (= the subjugation of the Trojans) esse, videndum ne haec cogant nos fateri esse per se, quando aetas abstulerit saecla quorum haec eventa fuerunt. English: when they say that the abducted daughter and the subdued nations exist; the persons, not the events, are the subject. DIFF: participial noun phrase read as persons, not events.

469-470. Latin: quod cumque actum erit poterit dici eventum aliud terris, aliud regionibus. English: same. MATCH.

471-477. Latin: si materies nulla fuisset nec locus, numquam ignis … accendisset certamina, nec equos inflammasset Pergama partu nocturno Graiiugenarum (by its night-time bringing-forth of the Greeks). English: … set Pergamum aflame by its birth in the night of sons of the Greeks. DIFF: partu = the horse's giving birth to the Greeks, rendered as the horse's own birth, and "night of sons of the Greeks" misattaches the genitive.

478-482. Latin: ut perspicere possis res gestas non constare per se ut corpus, nec cluere ratione qua inane, sed ut possis vocare eventa corporis atque loci. English: same. MATCH.

FINDINGS
477	MISTRANSLATION	MODERATE	0.70	"partu nocturno Graiiugenarum" is the horse's night-time bringing-forth of the Greeks hidden in it, but "by its birth in the night of sons of the Greeks" makes the horse the one born and attaches the genitive to "night"; "aflame by bringing forth the sons of the Greeks by night;"
464	AMBIGUITY	MODERATE	0.55	"Tyndaridem raptam … subactas Troiiugenas gentis … esse" (with 465) is the ab urbe condita idiom for the events (the abduction, the conquest), but "the abducted daughter of Tyndareus and the nations … exist" makes the persons exist, which blurs the point that these events are accidents (467); "Finally, when they say that the carrying-off of Tyndareus' daughter" (with 465: "and the conquest in war of the Trojan nations exist, we must take care")
