[DRAFT] (1.875) Anaxagoras of Clazomenae held that every thing contains a portion of every other (homoeomereia, "like parts"), and that a thing looks like what it is because that ingredient predominates; Lucretius is finishing his refutation of this doctrine, begun earlier in the book. The "slender chance of hiding" (latitandi copia tenvis) is the last refuge: if the other ingredients are present but concealed, the failure to see them is no objection.

[DRAFT] (1.897) The objection put in the mouth of an interlocutor ("inquis", "you say") is that forest fires start from rubbed branches, which seems to show fire was hidden in the wood. Lucretius answers that what is hidden is seeds of fire (semina ardoris), which "flow together" (confluxere) only when rubbing brings them into the right arrangement.

[DRAFT] (1.908) Here the first-beginnings (primordia) are named in the register's fixed term, the Lucretian equivalent of what a modern reader may know as atoms. Lucretius returns to the point "said a little before" (paulo quod diximus ante), the letter analogy first made at 1.196-198 and developed at 1.823-829, where the same elements make different words and different things.

[DRAFT] (1.912) The pun on elementa is the Latin word for both the first constituents of things and the letters of the alphabet. In ligna and ignes the same letters (l, i, g, n, e, s, a) are rearranged in nearly the same order, which the English "wood" and "fires" cannot show. Cf. the register's policy of keeping "elements" and noting the pun once. The English cannot reproduce the letter-sharing, so the argument rests on the reader accepting the claim. Consider keeping or expanding the placeholder note along these lines.

[DRAFT] (1.915) The reductio runs: if like must come from like, the constituents of laughing, weeping things would themselves have to laugh and weep (risu tremulo, lacrimis salsis). This reappears against Anaxagorean and other "homoeomeric" views at 1.916-920, and again in Book 2 (2.976-990) where Lucretius argues that sensation need not be present in the atoms of sentient beings, using the same image of laughing and weeping atoms.

[DRAFT] (1.922) The thyrsus (thyrso) is the ivy-wreathed, pine-coned wand of Bacchus and his followers, whose touch brought the frenzy of divine possession. Lucretius uses it of the "hope of praise" that has stricken his heart, so poetic inspiration is figured as Bacchic. The Muses (Musarum) are then named in the next line; cf. Ennius's own claims to inspiration.

[DRAFT] (1.926) Lines 926-950 recur almost verbatim as the opening of Book 4 (4.1-25), so the English here should be matched word for word with the Book 4 rendering, and the repetition recorded. The Pierides (Pieridum) are the Muses, named from Pieria, the region of Macedonia at the foot of Olympus where their cult was said to have begun. The claim to untrodden ground (nullius ante trita solo) is a conventional Hellenistic and Roman claim of poetic novelty.

[DRAFT] (1.936) The wormwood simile (absinthia taetra) is the famous honey-on-the-cup image: physicians coat the rim of the cup with honey so that children drink the bitter medicine unharmed. It stands for the poet's method, with the Epicurean doctrine as the bitter draught and the verse as the honey. Lucretius also repeats it at 4.11-25; the same wording governs both.

[DRAFT] (1.951) The argument returns here from the digression on poetics to the doctrine of the infinity of the universe. "Since I have taught..." (quoniam docui) refers back to the demonstration that the first bodies are solid and indestructible; the question now is whether the sum (summai) of them, and the void in which they move, is bounded or not.

[DRAFT] (1.958) The argument that the all (omne) has no limit begins here and rests on the principle that an edge must be an edge of something against something else: a boundary requires a beyond. Lucretius reaches it by the doctrine that the all is body and void, with nothing outside. (omne quod est = "all that exists", the totality.)

[DRAFT] (1.968) The thrown-spear argument, one of the best known in ancient cosmology, is Lucretius's own presentation (some ascribe it to Archytas of Tarentum, as Simplicius reports; the attribution here is a scholar's, not the poet's). The line-for-line English follows the Latin closely, and the dilemma is that whether the spear flies on or is stopped, something must lie beyond the supposed edge. The phrase "exempta fine" at 1.976 is carried by "exempt from any end".

[DRAFT] (1.984) The second argument from weight supposes that in a finite universe all matter, falling by its own weight (ponderibus solidis) through infinite past time (ex infinito iam tempore), would long since have settled at the bottom and formed a heap, leaving no sky or sun. Epicurean physics has no center to which things fall, only a direction "down" in the infinite; the argument assumes that in a bounded space there would be a bottom.

[DRAFT] (1.992) The principia (principiorum) is a variant of primordia that Lucretius also uses for the first-beginnings. The English "beginnings" in this line keeps it distinct from the fixed "first-beginnings" (primordia), as the register requires, but the placeholder note should record that the two are the same entity under metrical variety.

[DRAFT] (1.998) The last argument, from the observable fact that one thing bounds another (res rem finire), moves from experience to the whole by contrast: everything within the world has a neighbor, but the whole has no outside. This sentence is also what the poet's earlier "nothing outside" (1.963) depends on, so that the argument ends where it began.
