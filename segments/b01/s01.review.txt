  146  This terror of the mind, then, and this darkness {{NOTE: first occurrence of a refrain; 146-148 recur verbatim at 2.59-61, 3.91-93, 6.39-41}}
  147  must be scattered not by the sun's rays or the bright shafts of day
  148  but by the face and reasoning of nature.
  149  Its first principle will take its starting-point for us from here:
  150  that nothing is ever begotten from nothing by divine will.
  151  Indeed, fear so holds all mortals in its grip
  152  because they behold many things happening on earth and in the sky
  153  the causes of whose workings they can in no way see,
  154  and they suppose them to be done by divine power.
  156  For these reasons, when we have seen that nothing can be created {{NOTE: 156-158 printed before 155, an editorial transposition; the source order is kept}}
  157  from nothing, then we shall more rightly discern from there
  158  what we are seeking: both from what each thing can be created
  155  and in what manner all things come about without the labor of gods.
  159  For if things came to be from nothing, out of all things
  160  every kind could be born; nothing would need a seed.
  161  First, men could rise out of the sea, and out of the earth
  162  the scaly race, and birds could burst out of the sky;
  163  herds and other cattle, every kind of wild beast,
  164  born at random, would occupy the tilled land and the wild.
  165  Nor would the same fruits stay constant on the trees,
  166  but would change; all trees could bear all fruits.
  167  For where there were no generative bodies for each thing,
  168  how could there be a fixed mother for things?
  169  But as it is, since each thing is created from fixed seeds,
  170  it is born and comes forth into the shining shores of light
  171  from that place where the matter of each and its first bodies are;
  172  and for this reason all things cannot be begotten from all,
  173  because in fixed things there dwells a separate power.
  174  Further, why do we see the rose poured forth in spring, grain in the heat,
  175  vines when autumn urges them on,
  176  if not because, when fixed seeds of things at their own time
  177  have flowed together, whatever is created is disclosed
  178  while the seasons are at hand, and the living earth
  179  brings tender things safely forth into the shining shores of light?
  180  But if they came from nothing, they would spring up suddenly
  181  at uncertain intervals and in alien parts of the year,
  182  since there would be no first-beginnings that could be kept
  183  from generative union by an unfavorable season.
  184  Nor again, for things to grow, would there be need of time
  185  for the meeting of seed, if they could grow out of nothing;
  186  for little infants would suddenly become young men,
  187  and trees, risen from the earth, would leap up all at once.
  188  It is plain that none of this happens, since all things
  189  grow little by little, as is fitting, from fixed seed,
  190  and as they grow they keep their kind; so that you can know
  191  that each thing grows large and is nourished from its own matter.
  192  To this is added that without the year's fixed rains
  193  the earth could not send up its gladdening produce,
  194  nor again could the nature of living things, kept apart from food,
  195  propagate its kind and preserve its life;
  196  so that you should rather think that many bodies are common
  197  to many things, as we see elements are to words, {{NOTE: elementa means both the elements of things and the letters of the alphabet; Lucretius's recurring analogy, kept as "elements"}}
  198  than that anything could exist without beginnings.
  199  Again, why could nature not produce men so huge
  200  that they could cross the sea on foot, wading through its shallows,
  201  and tear great mountains apart with their hands,
  202  and in living outlast many generations of the living,
  203  if not because fixed matter has been assigned to things
  204  for their begetting, from which it stands what can arise?
  205  Therefore it must be confessed that nothing can come from nothing,
  206  since things need seed, from which each, once created,
  207  can be brought forth into the tender breezes of the air.
  208  Finally, since we see that tilled places surpass
  209  the untilled, and yield better produce to our hands,
  210  there are, it is clear, first-beginnings of things in the earth
  211  which we, turning the fertile clods with the plowshare
  212  and working the soil of the earth, rouse to birth;
  213  but if there were none, you would see each thing, without our labor,
  214  come of its own accord to be much better.
  215  To this is added that nature dissolves each thing back
  216  into its own bodies, and does not destroy things into nothing.
  217  For if anything were mortal in all its parts,
  218  each thing would suddenly perish, snatched from our eyes;
  219  for there would be no need of any force that could bring about
  220  division among its parts and undo their bonds.
  221  As it is, since each thing is made of eternal seed,
  222  until a force has met it that can shatter things with a blow
  223  or pierce within through the voids and dissolve them,
  224  nature does not allow the destruction of anything to be seen.
  225  Further, whatever things time removes through age,
  226  if it destroys them utterly, consuming all their matter,
  227  from where does Venus lead the race of living things, kind by kind,
  228  back into the light of life? Or, once led back, from where does the artificer earth
  229  nourish and increase them, kind by kind, providing food?
  230  From where do native springs and rivers from far outside
  231  keep the sea supplied? From where does the ether feed the stars?
  232  For all things that are of mortal body
  233  infinite time and the days gone by must have consumed.
  234  But if in that space, in that time gone by, there have been
  235  those things from which this sum of things is made up, renewed,
  236  they are surely endowed with an immortal nature.
  237  Therefore things cannot, any of them, return to nothing.
  238  Again, the same force and cause would destroy all things
  239  indiscriminately, unless eternal matter held them,
  240  more or less entangled in its bonds with itself;
  241  for a touch would surely be cause enough of death,
  242  since there would be nothing of eternal body whose
  243  fabric any force would have to unweave.
  244  But as it is, since the bonds of the beginnings with one another
  245  are unlike, and matter is eternal,
  246  things remain with body unharmed until a force
  247  meets them found keen enough for the texture of each.
  248  Therefore no thing returns to nothing, but all things
  249  by division return to the bodies of matter.
  250  Finally, the rains perish when father ether
  251  has flung them headlong into the lap of mother earth;
  252  but shining crops spring up, and branches grow green
  253  on the trees; the trees themselves grow and are weighed down with fruit.
  254  From this, in turn, our race and the race of wild beasts are fed,
  255  from this we see glad cities flower with children
  256  and leafy woods on every side sing with new birds,
  257  from this the weary cattle through the rich glad pastures lay down
  258  their bodies, and the white milky liquid
  259  flows from the swollen udders; from this the new offspring
  260  on unsteady limbs frisk through the tender grass
  261  and play, their young minds made drunk with unmixed milk. {{NOTE: merum is the word for unmixed wine; the young are tipsy on milk}}
  262  Therefore none of the things that are seen perish utterly,
  263  since nature remakes one thing from another, and does not allow
  264  anything to be born unless helped by another's death.
  265  Come now: since I have taught that things cannot be created
  266  from nothing, nor, once born, be called back to nothing,
  267  yet lest perhaps you begin to distrust my words
  268  because the first-beginnings of things cannot be seen with the eyes,
  269  hear further of bodies which you yourself must
  270  confess are among things and yet cannot be seen.
  271  First, the roused force of the wind lashes the body of the sea {{NOTE: the text reads corpus, "body"; many editors emend to pontum, "sea"; taken here as the sea's body}}
  272  and overturns huge ships and scatters the clouds,
  273  at times, running across the plains in a swift whirlwind,
  274  it strews them with great trees, and the mountaintops
  275  it harries with forest-breaking blasts: so with a fierce
  276  roar the sea rages, and storms with a threatening growl.
  277  Therefore winds, beyond doubt, are unseen bodies
  278  that sweep the sea, the lands, and finally the clouds of heaven,
  279  and harry them and snatch them up in a sudden whirlwind;
  280  nor do they flow or spread their havoc in any other way
  281  than when the soft nature of water is suddenly carried
  282  in an overflowing stream, swollen with abundant rains
  283  by a great downrush of water from high mountains,
  284  hurling together broken pieces of forests and whole trees,
  285  nor can strong bridges bear the sudden force
  286  of the oncoming water: so, turbid with great rain,
  287  the river runs against the piers with mighty strength,
  288  makes havoc with a great roar, and rolls beneath its waves
  289  great stones, and rushes on wherever anything stands against its flood.
  290  So therefore the blasts of wind too must be carried,
  291  which, when like a strong river they have leaned
  292  toward any quarter, drive things before them and rush on
  293  with repeated onsets, at times in a twisting eddy
  294  seize them and carry them off in a swift, spinning whirlwind.
  295  Therefore, again and again, winds are unseen bodies,
  296  since in their deeds and ways they are found to rival
  297  great rivers, which are of visible body.
  298  Then again, we perceive the various odors of things
  299  and yet never see them coming to our nostrils,
  300  nor do we look upon hot heats, nor can we take in cold
  301  with our eyes, nor are we used to seeing voices;
  302  yet all these must consist of bodily
  303  nature, since they can strike upon the senses;
  304  for to touch and be touched no thing can, unless it is body.
  305  Again, clothes hung up on the wave-breaking shore
  306  grow damp, and the same, spread out in the sun, grow dry.
  307  But neither how the moisture of water settled in them
  308  has been seen, nor again how it fled before the heat.
  309  Therefore the moisture is dispersed into small parts
  310  which the eyes can in no way see.
  311  Indeed, as many years of the sun come round again,
  312  a ring on the finger grows thin beneath from wearing,
  313  the falling of the drip hollows a stone, the curved iron
  314  plowshare secretly wastes away in the fields,
  315  and the stone pavements of the roads we see already
  316  worn by the feet of the crowd; then, beside the gates, bronze
  317  statues show their right hands thinned
  318  by the touch of those who greet them, often, and of those passing by.
  319  These things, then, we see are diminished, since they are worn away.
  320  But what bodies depart from them at any given time
  321  envious nature has shut off from the power of sight.
