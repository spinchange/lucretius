  548  And since the mind is a single part of a man and stays
  549  fixed in a certain place, just as the ears and eyes are,
  550  and the other senses that steer our life,
  551  and since, just as hand or eye or nostrils, set apart
  552  and severed from us, cannot feel or even be,
  553  but yet, in such a state, are abandoned in a short time, {{NOTE: "tali" and "lincuntur" as printed; many editors read "tabe", "melt away in decay"}}
  554  so the mind cannot by itself be without the body and the man
  555  himself, which seems to be a kind of vessel for it,
  556  or whatever else you would rather picture as more closely
  557  joined to it, since the body clings to it with a bond.
  558  Again, the living powers of body and mind
  559  have strength and enjoy life only joined to each other;
  560  for neither without the body can it put forth the motions of life,
  561  the nature of the mind, alone by itself, nor again
  562  can a body deprived of spirit endure and use its senses.
  563  Clearly, just as the eye itself, torn out by the roots,
  564  cannot make out any thing apart from the whole body,
  565  so the spirit and the mind by themselves seem to have no power.
  566  No wonder, since, mingled through veins and flesh,
  567  through sinews and bones, they are held in by the whole body,
  568  and their first-beginnings cannot leap apart freely
  569  across great intervals; therefore, shut in, they move
  570  with the sense-bearing motions which, outside the body,
  571  cast out into the breezes of air after death, they cannot move,{{NOTE: the source prints "aerisaëris" here and at 3.591 ("aeraër" at 3.573), a doubled spelling of aeris / aëris; read simply as "air"}}
  572  because they are not held in by a like principle;
  573  for air would be a body, a living thing, if the spirit
  574  could hold itself together and shut itself within those motions
  575  which before it carried on in the sinews and in the body itself.
  576  Therefore again and again, when the whole covering of the body
  577  is undone and the breaths of life are cast out,
  578  you must admit that the senses of the mind are dissolved,
  579  and the spirit too, since the cause of the two is joined.
  580  Again, since the body cannot endure the parting
  581  from the spirit without rotting away in a foul stench,
  582  why do you doubt that, risen from deep within, from the depths,
  583  the force of the spirit has flowed out, diffused like smoke,
  584  and that therefore the body, changed and rotten, has collapsed
  585  in such great ruin, because its foundations have been moved
  586  deep from their place, as the spirit flows outward through the limbs
  587  and through all the windings of the passages there are in the body
  588  and its openings? So in many ways you can learn
  589  that the nature of the spirit went out through the limbs, divided,
  590  and was torn apart in itself within the body itself
  591  before it slipped out and swam forth into the breezes of air.
  592  What is more, while it still turns within the bounds of life,
  593  often, from some cause, the spirit seems nonetheless shaken
  594  and about to go, and to be loosed whole from the whole body,
  595  and the face seems to grow faint as at the final hour
  596  and all the limbs to drop, slack, from the bloodless body.
  597  Such is the case when it is said that it has gone ill with the mind,{{NOTE: animo male factum and animam liquisse are everyday Latin for fainting; Lucretius takes the idioms at their word}}
  598  or that the spirit has failed; when now there is alarm and all
  599  long to catch hold again of the last bond of life;
  600  for then the mind and all the power of the spirit
  601  are shaken. And these totter together with the body itself,
  602  so that a cause a little heavier could dissolve them.
  603  Why do you doubt, then, that once driven out of the body,
  604  feeble, outside, in the open, its covering removed,
  605  not only could it not endure through all time
  606  but could not hold together for the least space of time?
  607  For no one dying seems to feel
  608  the spirit going out unharmed from the whole body,
  609  nor rising first to the throat and the gullet above,
  610  but failing, placed as it is in a fixed region,
  611  just as he knows the other senses dissolve, each in its own
  612  part. But if our mind were immortal,
  613  it would not, dying, so lament that it was being dissolved,
  614  but rather go out and leave its garment behind, like a snake.
  615  Again, why is the thought and judgment of the mind never
  616  born in the head or the feet or hands, but in all men clings
  617  to one seat and to fixed regions,
  618  if fixed places are not assigned to each thing for its birth,
  619  and where each thing, once created, can endure,
  620  and so exist with its many-fashioned members so divided
  621  that the order of the limbs never appears reversed?
  622  So strictly does thing follow thing, and flame is not wont
  623  to be created in rivers, nor cold to be begotten in fire.
  624  Further, if the nature of the spirit is immortal
  625  and can feel when separated from our body,
  626  we must, I think, suppose it furnished with five senses.
  627  Nor by any other principle can we picture to ourselves
  628  the spirits below wandering in Acheron.
  629  And so the painters and the earlier generations of writers
  630  have brought spirits on the scene furnished thus with senses.
  631  But neither eyes nor nostrils nor the hand itself can exist
  632  apart for the spirit, nor apart the tongue, nor ears;
  633  therefore they cannot by themselves feel, or even be.
  634  And since we feel that vital sense is present in the whole
  635  body, and see that the whole is a living thing,
  636  if suddenly, with a swift blow, it is cut through the middle
  637  by some force, so as to sever the two halves apart,
  638  no doubt the force of the spirit too, divided
  639  and cleft, will be torn asunder together with the body.
  640  But what is cut and parts into any pieces at all
  641  plainly denies that its own nature is eternal.
  642  They tell how scythe-bearing chariots lop off limbs,
  643  often so suddenly, hot with the mingled slaughter,
  644  that there seems to quiver on the ground the part which from the limbs
  645  has fallen, cut away, while yet the mind and the man's force,
  646  through the swiftness of the harm, cannot feel the pain;
  647  and at once, since the mind is given over to the zeal of battle,
  648  with what is left of the body it makes for battle and slaughter,
  649  and often does not grasp that its left arm is lost, shield and all,
  650  that wheels and ravening scythes have dragged off among the horses;
  651  another, that his right has fallen, as he climbs and presses on.
  652  Then another tries to rise when his leg is taken away,
  653  while nearby on the ground the dying foot works its toes.
  654  And the head cut off from the warm and living trunk
  655  keeps on the ground a living look and open eyes
  656  until it has given up all the remnants of the spirit.
  657  What is more, if, with its tongue flickering and threatening,
  658  a serpent's tail, and its long body, both of them,
  659  it pleased you to cut into many pieces with the steel,
  660  you will see all the pieces, severed, each apart, with the wound
  661  still fresh, writhe and sprinkle the earth with gore,
  662  and the fore part turn back to seek itself with its mouth,
  663  to grip with its bite, struck by the burning pain of the wound.
  664  Shall we say, then, that there are whole spirits in all those
  665  small pieces? But on that principle it will follow
  666  that one living creature had many spirits in its body.
  667  Therefore that which was one has been divided along with
  668  the body; and so both must be thought mortal,
  669  since alike they are cut apart into many pieces.
