[DRAFT] (3.262) The phrase "primordia principiorum" (primordia principiorum) is the "first-beginnings of the beginnings." Here it means the atoms that make up each of the four natures of the spirit (heat, air, wind, and the nameless fourth). Lucretius piles up two near-synonyms, and the English keeps "first-beginnings" for primordia and plain "beginnings" for principia.

[DRAFT] (3.266) The comparison to smell, color, and savor in one piece of flesh (odor et quidam color et sapor) shows how several distinct qualities can belong to one body without being separable from it. The point is that the four natures of the spirit are likewise inseparable parts of a single whole.

[DRAFT] (3.269) "Heat and air and the unseen power of wind" (calor atque aeraër et venti caeca potestas), together with the "mobile force," are the four natures introduced at 3.231-245. The fourth is the nameless one that supplies the first motion of sense.

[DRAFT] (3.275) "Anima animae" (animae … totius ipsa proporro) means that the nameless fourth nature is to the spirit as the spirit is to the whole body. It is the innermost level of a nested hierarchy, and the same idea returns at 3.280-281. The Latin text of 3.275 is awkward and is sometimes emended, but the sense is not in doubt.

[DRAFT] (3.279) The "force that has no name" (nominis expers) is the fourth nature of 3.241-245. Lucretius says elsewhere that Greek and Latin have no word for it, so he declines to name it. The translation follows him and leaves it as "this force that has no name."

[DRAFT] (3.280-281) These lines repeat 3.275 in different words ("quasi totius ipsa proporrost anima"). The doctrine is stated twice, first as a claim and then as an explanation by analogy to the mind and spirit within the body.

[DRAFT] (3.288) The next lines tie each of the three named natures to an emotion: heat to anger (calor … in ira), cold breath to fear (frigida … aura), and calm air to tranquillity (pacati … aeris). The same three-way scheme runs through the animal comparisons that follow. This is the poem's physiological account of temperament, and it is not a separate theory of the emotions.

[DRAFT] (3.296) The lions, deer, and oxen (leonum, cervorum, boum) are the standard emotional types in this scheme. Lions are hot and quick to anger, deer are cold and timid, and oxen are placid and balanced. Lucretius uses them as exemplars for the human types that follow at 3.307-313.

[DRAFT] (3.307) "Doctrina" (doctrina) is the training or philosophical education that can polish a man's character. Lucretius holds that it leaves behind the "first traces" (vestigia prima) of each one's natural makeup. This is the Epicurean middle position: education improves a person but does not remove innate temperament, and the traces are too small to prevent a life "worthy of gods" (3.322).

[DRAFT] (3.317) "Nomina quot sunt figurarum" (figurarum tot nomina quot sunt principiis) says that he lacks as many names as there are shapes among the first-beginnings. The atoms differ in shape, and the shapes would account for the differences in temperament. Lucretius admits he cannot give the full explanation, which is a modest limit on what the theory can show.

[DRAFT] (3.327) The comparison to frankincense (thuris glaebis) shows that a property such as smell cannot be drawn out of a thing without destroying the thing. A reader should see that the analogy is about inseparability, as at 3.266-268. Mind and spirit are bound to the body in the same way.

[DRAFT] (3.337) From here to 3.349, Lucretius argues from the body's own dependence on the spirit. The body "by itself" (per se) is never born, never grows, and does not outlast death, so the two share one fate. The water comparison (umor aquae, 3.339) is an example of the opposite case, a thing that can lose heat and survive.

[DRAFT] (3.350) "Quod super est" (quod super est) is the register's formula "for the rest," here introducing a new opponent. The people he answers deny that the body feels and say that the spirit alone feels. The view is that the body is a passive tool and the spirit does the sensing, and the lines that follow answer it.

[DRAFT] (3.356) The line in quotation marks (at dimissa anima …) is an objection voiced by the opponent, and Lucretius answers it in the next two lines (3.357-358). It is a rare instance where the dialogue form is made explicit. The translation marks it with quotation marks.

[DRAFT] (3.359) The "doors" image (foribus … reclusis, 3.360) is the target. It pictures the eyes as openings through which the mind looks out. Lucretius answers with two arguments, from dazzle (3.363-364) and from removal of the eyes (3.367-369), and he treats the idea as a mistake about how sight works.

[DRAFT] (3.371) Democritus is named here with the epithet "sancta" (sancta viri sententia), which is respectful. Lucretius corrects the doctrine, not the man. The view reported is that atoms of body and of mind alternate one by one through the limbs (3.372-373), and the lines that follow argue that this cannot hold because the spirit's atoms are fewer and smaller (3.374-380).

[DRAFT] (3.374) "Elementa" (elementa) here means the first-beginnings of the spirit, and the translation keeps "elements" as the register requires. Lucretius also uses it for the letters of the alphabet, and the double sense is noted at its first appearance, not here.

[DRAFT] (3.380) "Exordia prima" (exordia prima) is another term for the first-beginnings, and the translation gives it a distinct rendering as "first starting-points." The point of the argument is that the intervals between the spirit's atoms are as wide as the smallest body that can stir a sense-bearing motion, so they are spread thin through the body.
