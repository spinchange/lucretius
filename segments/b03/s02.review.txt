  262  For the first-beginnings of the beginnings run among one another {{NOTE: "primordia principiorum": the atoms of the four elements of the spirit (heat, air, wind, the nameless nature); principia here = "beginnings", kept distinct from primordia}}
  263  in their motions, so that no single one can be set apart
  264  nor its power be made separate by space,
  265  but they stand out, as it were, the many forces of one body.
  266  Just as in the flesh of any living thing, commonly,
  267  there is smell and a certain color and savor, yet from all these
  268  there is made up one complete growth of body,
  269  so heat and air and the unseen power of wind,
  270  mingled, create one nature, together with that mobile
  271  force which hands out from itself to them the beginning of motion,
  272  from which the sense-bearing motion first arises through the flesh.
  273  For this nature lies hidden deep down and underneath,
  274  and there is nothing further below it in our body,
  275  and it is in its turn the very spirit of the whole spirit. {{NOTE: "anima animae": the fourth, nameless nature is to the spirit what the spirit is to the body}}
  276  Just as in our limbs and through the whole body
  277  the force of mind and the power of spirit lie mingled and hidden,
  278  because it is created of bodies small and few,
  279  so, you see, this force that has no name, made of minute {{NOTE: "nominis expers": the fourth nature of 3.241-245, which Lucretius declines to name}}
  280  bodies, lies hidden, and is, as it were, the very spirit
  281  in its turn of the whole spirit, and rules through the whole body.
  282  On a like principle, wind and air and heat
  283  must be active, mingled with one another through the limbs,
  284  and one lie more beneath or stand out more than another,
  285  so that one thing may be seen to be made from them all,
  286  lest heat and wind apart, and apart the power
  287  of air, should destroy sense and, drawn asunder, dissolve it.
  288  The mind has also that heat which it takes on in anger,
  289  when it boils up and fire flashes more fiercely from the eyes;
  290  there is also much cold breath, the companion of fear,
  291  which stirs shuddering in the limbs and rouses the frame;
  292  there is also that state of air made calm,
  293  which comes about in a tranquil breast and a serene face.
  294  But there is more of the hot in those whose hearts are fierce
  295  and whose angry mind easily boils over into anger,
  296  of which kind above all is the violent force of lions,
  297  who often, groaning, burst their breasts with roaring
  298  and cannot contain the billows of their anger in their breast.
  299  But the cold mind of deer has more of wind
  300  and more quickly rouses icy breaths through the flesh,
  301  which make a trembling motion rise up in the limbs.
  302  But the nature of oxen lives more by calm air,
  303  nor ever does anger's smoky torch, set beneath, rouse it too far,
  304  spreading over it the shadow of blind darkness,
  305  nor is it numbed, pierced through by the icy shafts of fear;
  306  it is set between the two, the deer and the savage lions.
  307  So it is with the race of men: although training may make
  308  some equally polished, still it leaves behind
  309  those first traces of the nature of each one's mind.
  310  Nor must we think that faults can be torn out by the roots,
  311  so that one man does not run more readily to fierce anger,
  312  another not be assailed a little sooner by fear, a third
  313  not take certain things more mildly than is right.
  314  And in many other things the varied natures of men
  315  must differ, and the characters that follow on them;
  316  whose hidden causes I cannot now set out,
  317  nor find as many names as there are shapes
  318  of the beginnings, from which this variety of things arises.
  319  This I see that I can affirm in these matters:
  320  that so slight are the traces of our natures left behind,
  321  which reasoning cannot drive away from us,
  322  that nothing hinders us from living a life worthy of gods.
  323  This nature, then, is held in by the whole body
  324  and is itself the body's guardian and the cause of its safety;
  325  for they cling to one another by common roots
  326  and it seems they cannot be torn apart without destruction.
  327  Just as to tear the smell from lumps of frankincense
  328  is not easy, without its nature too perishing,
  329  so to draw out the nature of mind and spirit
  330  from the whole body is not easy, without all being dissolved.
  331  So, with their beginnings interwoven from their first origin,
  332  they come to be endowed with a life held in partnership,
  333  nor, it seems, can either, without the force of the other,
  334  the power of body or of mind, feel apart, by itself,
  335  but by common motions between them on both sides
  336  sense is fanned and kindled for us through the flesh.
  337  Further, the body by itself is never born,
  338  nor grows, nor is seen to last after death.
  339  For not as the liquid of water often gives off the heat
  340  that was given it, and is not for that reason torn apart itself,
  341  but stays unharmed, not so, I say, can the limbs, left behind,
  342  endure the separation of the spirit,
  343  but they perish utterly, torn apart, and rot away.
  344  So from the beginning of life the mutual contacts
  345  of body and spirit learn the motions of life,
  346  even while laid away in the mother's limbs and womb,
  347  so that separation cannot happen without ruin and harm;
  348  so that you may see, since the cause of their safety is joined,
  349  that their nature too must stand joined together.
  350  For the rest, if anyone denies that the body feels
  351  and believes that the spirit, mingled through the whole body,
  352  takes on this motion which we name sense,
  353  he fights against even plain and true facts.
  354  For who will ever tell us what it is for the body to feel,
  355  if not what the thing itself has openly shown and taught us?
  356  "But when the spirit is let go, the body lacks sense in every part."
  357  Yes, for it loses what was not its own in life,
  358  and loses much besides when it is driven out of life.
  359  Further, to say that the eyes can see nothing,
  360  but that the mind looks out through them as through open doors,
  361  is hard, since the sense of the eyes leads us the other way;
  362  for sense drags us and thrusts us to the pupils themselves,
  363  especially since often we cannot see bright things,
  364  because the light of our eyes is hampered by light.
  365  This does not happen with doors; for the doorways through which
  366  we ourselves look, when thrown open, take on no labor.
  367  Further, if our eyes serve in place of doors,
  368  then, it seems, the mind ought to see things better
  369  with the eyes taken out, the very doorposts removed.
  370  In these matters you could by no means accept
  371  what the holy judgment of the man Democritus lays down, {{NOTE: Democritus of Abdera, the atomist whom Epicurus followed and corrected; "sancta" is respectful, not ironic}}
  372  that the first-beginnings of body and of mind, one by one,
  373  set side by side alternately, diversify and knit the limbs.
  374  For not only are the elements of the spirit much smaller
  375  than those of which our body and flesh are made,
  376  but also yield to them in number, and are sparsely scattered
  377  through the limbs, so that you may promise this much at least:
  378  as small as are the first bodies which, falling on us,
  379  can stir sense-bearing motions in the body, so wide
  380  are the intervals the first origins of the spirit keep. {{NOTE: "exordia prima": Lucretius's third name for the first-beginnings, "origins" per the register (2.333)}}
