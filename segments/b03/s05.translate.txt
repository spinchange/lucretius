  670  Further, if the nature of the spirit is immortal
  671  and works its way into the body at our birth,
  672  why can we not remember, besides, the age lived before,
  673  nor keep any traces of the things we have done?
  674  For if the power of the mind is changed so greatly
  675  that all holding of past things has fallen away from it,
  676  that, I think, wanders now not far from death;
  677  and so you must confess that what was before
  678  has perished, and that what is now has now been created.
  679  Further, if, when our body is already complete,
  680  the living power of the mind is used to being brought in
  681  at the moment we are born and cross the threshold of life,
  682  it would not then be fitting that, with the body and together
  683  with the limbs, it should be seen to grow within the very blood,
  684  but fitting rather that it live alone, for itself, as in a cage,
  685  and yet in such a way that the whole body flows with sense.
  686  Therefore, again and again, we must not think that spirits
  687  are without a beginning, nor released from the law of death;
  688  for we must not think they could have been bound so closely
  689  to our bodies, having worked their way in from outside,
  690  a thing the plain fact shows to be wholly the reverse;
  691  for the spirit is so knit through veins, flesh, sinews,
  692  and bones, that even the teeth are given a share in sense,
  693  as toothache shows, and the twinge of icy water,
  694  and a rough stone bitten on, met suddenly in the bread;
  695  nor, since they are so closely woven in, do they seem able
  696  to go out unharmed and unloose themselves safe
  697  from all the sinews and the bones and joints.
  698  But if perhaps you think that the spirit, working its way in from outside,
  699  is used to seeping through our limbs,
  700  so much the more surely will it perish, being fused with the body;
  701  for what seeps through is dissolved, and therefore perishes;
  702  for it is parted out through all the passages of the body.
  703  As food, when it is dispersed into all the limbs and frame,
  704  perishes and supplies another nature out of itself,
  705  so spirit and mind, however whole they go fresh
  706  into the body, are yet dissolved in seeping through,
  707  while, as through passages, there are dispersed into all the frame
  708  the particles of which this nature of the mind is created,
  709  the nature which now has mastery in our body, born
  710  from that which perished then, parted through the frame.
  711  Therefore it seems the nature of the spirit is neither
  712  deprived of a birthday nor exempt from a funeral.
  713  Again, are seeds of the spirit left behind, or not,
  714  in the lifeless body? If they are left and are in it,
  715  there will be no way it can rightly be held immortal,
  716  since it has gone off diminished, with parts lost.
  717  But if it has fled away, taken off with its parts so intact
  718  that it has left no parts of itself in the body,
  719  from where do corpses, their flesh now going rancid,
  720  breathe out worms, and from where does so great a host of living things,
  721  boneless and bloodless, surge through the swollen frame?
  722  But if perhaps you believe that spirits work their way in from outside
  723  into worms, and can come one by one into bodies,
  724  and do not reckon why many thousands of spirits
  725  should gather where one has withdrawn, still there is this
  726  that it seems must be asked and brought to decision:
  727  whether, in the end, the spirits hunt down each of the seeds
  728  of little worms and build themselves a place to be,
  729  or work their way, as it were, into bodies already complete.
  730  But why they should do it themselves, or why they should labor,
  731  there is no saying. For indeed, when they are without a body,
  732  they do not flit about troubled by diseases and cold and hunger;
  733  for it is the body, more akin to these ills, that suffers them,
  734  and the mind endures many evils by contact with it.
  735  But grant that it is as useful as you like for them to make a body
  736  when they come in under; yet there seems no way they could.
  737  Therefore spirits do not make bodies and frames for themselves.
  738  Nor yet is there a way they could work their way into bodies
  739  already complete; for neither could they be finely
  740  knit in, nor will contact be made through shared feeling.
  741  Again, why does fierce violence go with the grim breed
  742  of lions, cunning with foxes, and flight with deer,
  743  handed down from their sires, their sires' fear spurring their frame,
  744  and why indeed do all other things of this kind
  745  come to be in limbs and temper from the beginning of life,
  746  if not because, fixed by its own seed and breed,
  747  the force of the mind grows equally with every body?
  748  But if it were immortal and used to change
  749  bodies, living things would have mixed characters:
  750  the dog of Hyrcanian seed would often flee {{NOTE: Hyrcania, by the Caspian, was famous for fierce hunting dogs}}
  751  the charge of the horned stag, and the hawk would tremble
  752  through the breezes of the air, fleeing at the coming of the dove,
  753  men would lack sense, and the wild generations of wild beasts be wise.
  754  For that is carried by false reasoning, when they say
  755  an immortal spirit is altered when its body is changed;
  756  for what is changed is dissolved, and therefore perishes;
  757  for its parts are transposed and shift from their order;
  758  therefore they must be able to be dissolved through the frame too,
  759  so that in the end they all perish together with the body.
  760  But if they say that the spirits of men always pass
  761  into human bodies, still I shall ask why from wise
  762  a spirit could turn foolish, and why no boy is prudent,
  763  if not because, fixed by its own seed and breed, {{NOTE: this line repeats 3.746 and makes no sense here; it is generally thought an intrusion, but the source prints it}}
  764  nor the mare's foal as well trained as the strong horse's force.
  765  No doubt they will take refuge in this: that in a tender body
  766  the mind grows tender. But if this happens, you must confess
  767  that the spirit is mortal, since, changed throughout the frame,
  768  it loses so greatly its former life and sense.
  769  Or how will it be able, in step with every body,
  770  to grow strong and touch the longed-for flower of age,
  771  this force of the mind, unless it is a partner at the first origin?
  772  Or why does it wish to go out of limbs grown old?
  773  Does it fear to stay shut up in a rotting body,
  774  fear that its house, worn out by the long length of years,
  775  may fall in on it? But there are no dangers for the immortal.
  776  Again, that spirits stand ready at the couplings of Venus
  777  and the births of beasts seems a thing to laugh at,
  778  immortals waiting for mortal limbs
  779  in number beyond number, and contending headlong
  780  among themselves which first and before all shall work its way in;
  781  unless perhaps the covenants of spirits are so agreed
  782  that whichever comes flying first works its way in
  783  first, and they do not strive with their strength one whit.
  784  Again, there can be no tree in the upper air, no clouds
  785  in the deep sea, nor can fish live in the fields,
  786  nor blood be in wood, nor sap in stones.
  787  It is fixed and ordered where each thing grows and is.
  788  So the nature of the mind cannot arise without the body,
  789  alone, nor exist far off from sinews and blood.
  790  For if it could, the force of the mind itself, much sooner,
  791  could be in the head or shoulders or the lowest heels,
  792  and be used to being born in any part you like,
  793  and still remain in the same man and the same vessel.
  794  And since even in our body it is evidently fixed
  795  and ordered, it seems, where they can be and grow,
  796  spirit and mind, each apart, all the more must we deny
  797  that it can, entire, endure outside the body, or be born.
  798  Therefore, when the body has perished, you must confess
  799  that the spirit has perished, torn apart through the whole body.
  800  For indeed, to join the mortal to the eternal, and to think
  801  that they can feel together and act each upon each,
  802  is folly; for what must be thought more at odds
  803  or more disjoined from itself and more discordant
  804  than that what is mortal, joined to the immortal
  805  and everlasting in union, should bear savage storms?
  806  Further, whatever things endure eternal must, of necessity, {{NOTE: 3.806-818 recur almost word for word at 5.351-363}}
  807  either, because they are of solid body, repel blows
  808  and suffer nothing to enter them that could tear apart
  809  their close-knit parts within, as the bodies of matter
  810  are, whose nature we have shown before; {{NOTE: the solid first-beginnings, argued in 1.483-634}}
  811  or be able to last through all time for this reason,
  812  that they are exempt from blows, as the void is,
  813  which stays untouched and is not affected one whit by a stroke;
  814  or else because there is no supply of place around them
  815  into which things could, as it were, part and be dissolved,
  816  as the sum of sums is eternal, nor is there outside it {{NOTE: the sum of sums is the whole universe}}
  817  any place into which they might scatter, nor are there bodies that
  818  could fall upon them and dissolve them with a strong blow.
