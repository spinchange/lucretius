  931  Finally, if the nature of things should suddenly find a voice {{NOTE: nature herself speaks (prosopopoeia), 3.933-949 and 3.955-962}}
  932  and should herself rebuke one of us in this way:
  933  'What ails you so, mortal, that you give yourself up too far
  934  to sick grieving? Why do you groan at death and weep?
  935  For if the life you have lived till now was welcome to you,
  936  and all its good things have not, as into a vessel full of holes,
  937  run through and perished unenjoyed,
  938  why not withdraw like a guest who has had his fill of life,
  939  and with an even mind, you fool, take your untroubled rest?
  940  But if whatever you enjoyed has been poured out and lost,
  941  and life offends you, why do you seek to add more,
  942  only to be lost badly again and perish all unenjoyed,
  943  and not rather make an end of life and of labor?
  944  For there is nothing more that I can devise and find for you
  945  to please you: all things are always the same.
  946  If your body is not yet withering with years, and your limbs
  947  are not worn out and slack, still all things remain the same,
  948  if you should go on living to outlast all generations,
  949  and still more if you were never going to die.'
  950  What do we answer, except that nature presses a just suit
  951  and sets out a true case in her words?
  952  But if now someone older, well on in years, should complain
  953  and lament his death, poor wretch, more than is right,
  954  would she not rightly cry out louder and rebuke in a sharp voice:
  955  'Away with your tears, you bottomless pit, and check your complaints. {{NOTE: baratre: the text is uncertain; taken as a vocative of barathrum, "pit", i.e. a glutton never filled; compare 3.966}}
  956  You have had all the prizes of life, and now you wither;
  957  but since you always crave what is absent and scorn what is here,
  958  your life has slipped away from you unfinished and unenjoyed,
  959  and death, unlooked for, has taken its stand at your head
  960  before you can depart full and sated with things.
  961  As it is, let go of all things that do not suit your age,
  962  and with an even mind, come, yield to greater ones: you must.' {{NOTE: textual crux; the source reads "magnis concede necessis"; editors differ (magnis, gnatis, dignis; necessest)}}
  963  Rightly, I think, she would plead, rightly rebuke and reproach;
  964  for the old, thrust out by the newness of things, gives way
  965  always, and one thing must be restored out of others.
  966  No one is handed down into the Pit or to black Tartarus;
  967  matter is needed, so that later generations may grow;
  968  and yet they all, their life lived through, will follow you;
  969  and so, no less than you, these have fallen before and will fall.
  970  So one thing will never cease to rise from another,
  971  and life is given to none to own, to all to use. {{NOTE: mancipium and usus are terms of Roman property law: full ownership versus the right of use}}
  972  Look back, again, how the age gone by of eternal time
  973  before we are born was nothing to us.
  974  This, then, is the mirror nature holds up to us of the time
  975  to come, after our death at last.
  976  Does anything horrible appear there, does anything seem
  977  grim? Is it not more untroubled than any sleep?
  978  And indeed whatever things are told to exist
  979  in deep Acheron, they are all here, in our life.
  980  No wretched Tantalus fears the great rock hanging in the air,
  981  as the story goes, numb with empty dread;
  982  rather, in life an empty fear of the gods oppresses
  983  mortals, and they fear the fall that chance may bring to each.
  984  Nor do birds enter Tityos as he lies in Acheron,
  985  nor can they truly find anything to search for
  986  beneath his great breast through all eternity.
  987  However vast the sprawl of his body as it lies there,
  988  though with outspread limbs it should cover not just nine acres
  989  but the whole circle of the earth,
  990  still he could not bear eternal pain
  991  nor furnish food from his own body forever.
  992  But Tityos is here among us: the one lying in love,
  993  whom birds tear, whom anxious anguish eats away,
  994  or cares rend through with some other craving.
  995  Sisyphus too is here in life before our eyes,
  996  the man who seeks from the people the rods and the savage axes, {{NOTE: the fasces and axes, insignia of Roman high office, won by election}}
  997  thirsting for them, and always withdraws beaten and grim.
  998  For to seek power, which is empty and is never given,
  999  and always to endure hard labor for it,
 1000  this is to push uphill, straining against the mountain,
 1001  a rock which yet from the very summit rolls back again
 1002  and rushes headlong for the levels of the open plain.
 1003  Then, always to feed the ungrateful nature of the mind,
 1004  to fill it with good things and never satisfy it,
 1005  as the seasons of the year do for us when they come
 1006  round again and bring their offspring and their varied charms,
 1007  and yet we are never filled with the fruits of life,
 1008  this, I think, is what they mean by the girls in the flower of age {{NOTE: the Danaids, punished in the underworld by carrying water in leaking vessels}}
 1009  who, they tell, pour water into a vessel full of holes,
 1010  which yet can by no means be filled.
 1011  Cerberus, too, and the Furies, and the lack of light,
 1012  Tartarus belching horrible blasts of heat from its jaws!
 1013  These are nowhere, and indeed cannot be;
 1014  but in life there is fear of punishment for evil deeds,
 1015  notable as the deeds are notable, and atonement for crime:
 1016  prison, and the horrible hurling down from the rock, {{NOTE: the Tarpeian Rock in Rome, from which criminals were thrown}}
 1017  scourges, executioners, the dungeon, pitch, hot plates, torches;
 1018  and even if these are absent, the mind, conscious of its deeds,
 1019  fearing beforehand, applies goads and scorches itself with whips,
 1020  and does not see meanwhile what limit to its evils there
 1021  can be, nor what end at last to its punishments,
 1022  and fears that these same things may grow heavier in death.
 1023  Here, in the end, the life of fools becomes an Acheron.
 1024  This too you might at times say to yourself:
 1025  'Even good Ancus has left the light with his eyes, {{NOTE: Ancus Marcius, fourth king of Rome; the line adapts Ennius}}
 1026  who was better than you, you shameless one, in many things.
 1027  Since then many other kings and lords of things
 1028  have perished, who ruled over great nations.
 1029  He too, the very one who once laid a road across the great sea {{NOTE: Xerxes, who bridged the Hellespont in 480 BC}}
 1030  and gave his legions a way to go over the deep
 1031  and taught them to cross the salt lagoons on foot
 1032  and, trampling with his horses, scorned the roar of the sea,
 1033  robbed of the light, poured out his spirit from a dying body.
 1034  The son of the Scipios, thunderbolt of war, terror of Carthage, {{NOTE: Scipio Africanus, conqueror of Hannibal (or possibly Aemilianus, who destroyed Carthage)}}
 1035  gave his bones to the earth as if he were the lowest servant.
 1036  Add the discoverers of learning and of graces,
 1037  add the companions of the Heliconian Muses; among them Homer,
 1038  who alone held the scepter, sleeps in the same rest as the others.
 1039  Finally Democritus, after ripe old age
 1040  warned him that the remembering motions of his mind were slackening,
 1041  went of his own will to meet death and offered up his head.
 1042  Epicurus himself died, the light of his life run through, {{NOTE: the only place in the poem where Epicurus is named}}
 1043  he who surpassed the human race in genius and put out
 1044  all the stars, as the sun in the ether does, once risen.
 1045  And will you hesitate, and resent it, to die?
 1046  You, whose life is all but dead while you still live and see,
 1047  who waste the greater part of your life in sleep,
 1048  and snore while awake, and never cease to see dreams,
 1049  and carry a mind troubled with empty dread,
 1050  and often cannot find what is wrong with you, when,
 1051  drunk, poor wretch, you are pressed by many cares on every side
 1052  and drift wandering in the wavering error of your mind.'
