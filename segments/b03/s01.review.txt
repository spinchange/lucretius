  136  Now I say that mind and spirit are held joined together
  137  with one another and make up from themselves a single nature,
  138  but that the head, so to speak, the lord over the whole body,
  139  is the counsel which we call the mind and thought. {{NOTE: animum mentemque: first technical pairing of animus and mens in Book 3; per register mens paired with animus = "thought"; both name the same ruling part}}
  140  And this is set and fixed in the middle region of the breast.
  141  For here dread and fear leap up, around these places
  142  joys caress us: here, therefore, are thought and mind.
  143  The rest of the spirit, scattered through the whole body,
  144  obeys, and moves at the nod and impulse of the mind. {{NOTE: numen here in its root sense, "nod", the sign of command, not divinity}}
  145  This alone, of itself, knows for itself and is glad for itself,
  146  when nothing moves either the spirit or the body along with it.
  147  And just as, when the head or the eye is hurt in us
  148  by an attack of pain, we are not tormented in our whole
  149  body, so the mind is sometimes hurt itself
  150  or thrives with joy, when the rest of the spirit
  151  through the limbs and frame is stirred by nothing new;
  152  but when the mind is moved by a more violent fear,
  153  we see the whole spirit through the limbs feel with it,
  154  and so sweat and pallor break out over the whole
  155  body, and the tongue falters and the voice dies away,
  156  the eyes grow dark, the ears ring, the limbs give way,
  157  and finally, from the mind's terror, we see
  158  men often collapse; so that anyone may easily learn from this
  159  that the spirit is joined with the mind, and that, struck by the mind's
  160  force, it at once drives the body forward and strikes it.
  161  This same reasoning teaches that the nature of mind and spirit
  162  is bodily; for when it is seen to drive the limbs forward,
  163  to snatch the body from sleep and change the face,
  164  and to steer and turn the whole man,
  165  none of which we see can happen without touch,
  166  nor touch, in turn, without body, must we not admit
  167  that mind and spirit consist of a bodily nature?
  168  Further, you see the mind is acted on together with the body
  169  and feels along with it, in our body.
  170  If the grim force of a weapon, though it does not strike out life,
  171  is driven in, laying open bones and sinews within,
  172  yet there follows a faintness, and a pleasant sinking
  173  to the earth, and, on the earth, the turmoil born in the mind,
  174  and now and then a wavering will, as it were, to rise.
  175  Therefore the nature of the mind must be bodily,
  176  since it suffers from bodily weapons and their blow.
  177  Now of what sort of body this mind of yours is, and from what
  178  it is composed, I shall go on to give an account in words.
  179  To begin with, I say that it is exceedingly fine, and is made
  180  of exceedingly tiny bodies. That this is so
  181  you may turn your mind to here, so as to know it thoroughly.
  182  Nothing is seen to come about in so swift a way
  183  as what the mind proposes to happen and itself begins;
  184  therefore the mind stirs itself more quickly than any of the things
  185  whose nature is seen plain before our eyes.
  186  But what is so very mobile must consist of exceedingly round
  187  and exceedingly tiny seeds,
  188  so that, struck by a small impulse, they can be moved.
  189  For water moves and flows at the slightest impulse,
  190  being made, of course, of small shapes that roll.
  191  But the nature of honey, by contrast, is more steady,
  192  its liquid more sluggish and its motion more delayed;
  193  for the whole store of its matter clings more together,
  194  surely because it is not made of bodies so smooth
  195  nor so fine and round.
  196  For a hovering, light breath of air can make
  197  a high heap of poppy seed flow away for you from the top,
  198  but against a pile of stones or of ears of grain
  199  it has no power. Therefore, as bodies are the smallest
  200  and smoothest, so they enjoy mobility;
  201  but whatever are found to be of greater weight
  202  and rougher, the more firmly they are fixed.
  203  Now therefore, since the nature of the mind has been found
  204  exceptionally mobile, it must consist of exceedingly
  205  small and smooth and round bodies.
  206  This fact, once known to you, my good friend, will be found useful
  207  in many matters and will be called timely.
  208  This too reveals its nature,
  209  how thin the texture it is made of, and in how small
  210  a place it would be held, if it could be gathered into a ball:
  211  that as soon as the untroubled quiet of death has taken hold
  212  of a man, and the nature of mind and spirit has withdrawn,
  213  you could see nothing taken away there from the whole body
  214  in appearance, nothing in weight: death leaves all intact
  215  except the vital sense and the warm heat.
  216  Therefore the whole spirit must be of exceedingly small
  217  seeds, linked through veins, flesh, sinews,
  218  inasmuch as, when all of it has gone from the whole body,
  219  the outer contour of the limbs still keeps itself
  220  unharmed, and not one jot of weight is lacking.
  221  It is just as when the flower of Bacchus has died away, or when {{NOTE: "flower of Bacchus": the bouquet of wine}}
  222  the sweet breath of a perfume has scattered into the air,
  223  or when savor has departed from some body;
  224  still the thing itself seems to the eyes no smaller
  225  for that, nor is anything taken from its weight,
  226  surely because many tiny seeds make up
  227  the savors and the scent in the whole body of things.
  228  Therefore, again and again, the nature of mind and spirit
  229  you may know is made of exceedingly minute
  230  seeds, since when it flees it takes away no weight.
  231  Yet we must not think this nature simple.
  232  For a certain thin breath leaves the dying,
  233  mixed with heat, and heat, in turn, draws air with it; {{NOTE: source file prints doubled forms here and at 234, 236, 248 (aeraaëra, aeraër, aerisaëris); read as aera/aër/aeris, "air"; check latin/ transcription}}
  234  nor is there any heat that has no air mixed in it;
  235  for since its nature is of loose texture, it must be
  236  that many first-beginnings of air move within it.
  237  So now the nature of the mind is found to be threefold;
  238  and yet these are not enough, all together, to create sensation,
  239  since the mind does not accept that any of these can create
  240  the sense-bearing motions, or what it then turns over in the mind.
  241  Therefore a certain fourth nature must also be
  242  added to these; it is altogether without a name; {{NOTE: the "nameless" fourth nature, the finest element of the soul, beyond breath, heat, and air}}
  243  than which nothing exists more mobile or more thin,
  244  nor made of smaller and smoother elements;
  245  it is first to distribute the sense-bearing motions through the limbs.
  246  For it is first stirred, made as it is of small shapes;
  247  then heat takes up the motions, and the unseen power of wind,
  248  then air; then all things are set in motion:
  249  the blood is shaken, then the flesh is thrilled through,
  250  all of it; last it is passed to the bones and the marrow,
  251  whether it is pleasure or the opposite burning.
  252  Nor can pain lightly penetrate this far, nor sharp
  253  harm seep through, without all things being thrown into confusion
  254  so far that life lacks room and the spirit's
  255  parts scatter through all the passages of the body.
  256  But mostly an end is made, as it were, at the surface of the body
  257  to these motions: and for this reason we are able to hold on to life.
  258  Now in what way these are mingled with one another, and in what
  259  modes arranged they thrive, though I am eager to give an account,
  260  the poverty of our native speech drags me back against my will; {{NOTE: recalls 1.139 and 1.832, the poverty of the Latin language}}
  261  but still, as far as I can touch on it in sum, I shall touch it.
