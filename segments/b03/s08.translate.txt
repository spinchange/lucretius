 1053  If men could, just as they plainly feel
 1054  that there is a weight in the mind that wears them out with its heaviness,
 1055  also come to know from what causes it arises, and from where
 1056  so great a mass, as it were, of evil stands in the breast, {{NOTE: text uncertain; "tam quam" printed for Bailey's "tamquam", "as it were"}}
 1057  they would hardly live their lives as now for the most part we see them,
 1058  each not knowing what he wants for himself, and always seeking
 1059  to change his place, as if he could set his burden down.
 1060  Often he goes out of doors from his great house,
 1061  the man who is sick to death of being at home, and suddenly comes back,
 1062  since indeed he feels himself no better off outside.
 1063  He races to his villa, driving his ponies headlong, {{NOTE: "mannos", small Gallic ponies, a fashionable luxury of the rich}}
 1064  as if pressing on to bring help to a house on fire;
 1065  he yawns at once, the moment he has touched the villa's threshold,
 1066  or goes off heavily into sleep and looks for forgetfulness,
 1067  or even hurries back to make for the city and see it again.
 1068  In this way each man runs from himself, but that self, of course, as happens,
 1069  he cannot escape: it clings to him against his will, and he hates it, {{NOTE: "ingratius" as printed; rendered as Bailey's "ingratis", "against his will"}}
 1070  for this reason, that, sick as he is, he does not grasp the cause of his disease;
 1071  if he saw it clearly, each man would leave his affairs behind
 1072  and study first of all to know the nature of things,
 1073  since it is the state of eternal time, not of a single hour,
 1074  that is in question, the state in which mortals must spend all
 1075  the age that remains to be waited out after death.
 1076  Finally, to tremble so much amid doubtful dangers,
 1077  what evil, what great craving for life compels us?
 1078  Surely a fixed end of life stands waiting for mortals,
 1079  and death cannot be avoided: we must go to meet it.
 1080  Further, we turn about in the same place and stay there always,
 1081  and no new pleasure is hammered out by living;
 1082  but while what we long for is absent, it seems to surpass
 1083  all the rest; afterwards, when that has come to us, we long for something else,
 1084  and an equal thirst for life holds us, always gaping.
 1085  And it is in doubt what fortune the coming age will carry,
 1086  or what chance will bring us, or what end is at hand.
 1087  Nor by prolonging life do we take away one jot
 1088  from the time of death, nor can we subtract anything from it
 1089  so that, once destroyed, we might perhaps be dead for less long.
 1090  Therefore you may live to complete as many generations as you like;
 1091  that eternal death will be waiting for you none the less,
 1092  and he will be no more for no less long who, with today's
 1093  light, made an end of life, than he
 1094  who perished many months and years before.
